\title{Displacement Before Escalation: Late Industrialisation and the Timing of Incumbent Constraint, 1850--2024}

\begin{abstract}
Late industrialisers meet tariffs, quotas, export restraints and technology controls from the powers they catch up with, yet the development literature treats this external constraint as background. This paper asks when it arrives and finds that its reach matches the reach of displacement. Across four episodes (Germany against Britain, Japan and Korea against the United States, China against the United States and the European Union) it dates 18 sectoral displacement milestones and codes 41 major incumbent responses by instrument reach; 36 follow the milestone of their sector, and only one broad instrument precedes it, the American export controls on advanced chips of October 2022. A complete register of 23 broad American instruments confirms the order, with one further exception in the multilateral textile regime. Reversing the test, a panel of 1,316 manufacturing groups shows that targeted broad instruments against Japan and Korea began 5.7 times as often after a challenger's exports overtook American exports as before, yet only about one displaced group in five drew one: displacement is close to necessary for broad constraint, but not sufficient. Narrow product remedies follow a different market: antidumping and countervailing duty cases track American imports from the challenger, rising from 1 to 30 per cent of displaced groups across thirds of import volume. British parliamentary debates from 1850 to 1914 add a scope condition: attention to Germany rose with its displacement of British exports in politically weighty sectors, while the United States, which outgrew Britain without displacing it in third markets, drew no comparable reaction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Every large economy that has caught up with the technological frontier has met resistance from the economy it was catching up with. Britain answered German competition with origin marking, an international sugar convention and, eventually, a general tariff. The United States answered Japan with export restraints on textiles, steel, televisions and cars, a managed-trade agreement in semiconductors and punitive tariffs. Korea met antidumping duties and pressure on its intellectual-property regime. China now faces tariffs on most of its exports to the United States, entity listings of its national champions and controls on the equipment needed to make advanced chips.

Development economics has much to say about how late industrialisers acquire technology and why their growth slows near the frontier. It has little to say about when the incumbent's reaction begins. The developmental-state literature treats the leader as a fixed benchmark \citep{gerschenkron1962,johnson1982,amsden1989,wade1990,evans1995}. \citet{chang2002} identifies the structural pattern, incumbents restricting the instruments they once used themselves, but gives it no clock. Convergence research explains the slowdown near the frontier from domestic causes \citep{acemoglu2006,eichengreen2012}. The political economy of protection explains who obtains protection and how much \citep{grossman1994}, not when.

The timing matters for policy and for theory. If incumbents constrain challengers already during the absorption of foreign technology, external constraint is part of the development environment from the start, and late industrialisers must plan around it. If incumbents constrain only after the challenger has displaced them in contested markets, constraint is a consequence of success, and its arrival marks the end of a particular phase of catch-up.

This paper tests the second account. It dates 18 sectoral displacement milestones, each defined as the first year in which the challenger leads in the market where the incumbent sells, and codes 41 documented incumbent responses on a five-point scale of instrument reach. The four episodes span 175 years and very different institutions: Germany against Britain (1850--1914), Japan against the United States (1950--1990), Korea against the United States and Japan (1960--2020) and China against the United States and the European Union (1978--2024).

The central finding is that the reach of constraint matches the reach of displacement. Thirty-six of the 41 major responses follow the milestone of their sector; of the five that precede it, four are speeches, investigations or narrow agreements scoring three or less, and only one is a broad instrument, the American export controls on advanced computing and semiconductor equipment of October 2022. Three checks guard against selecting cases on the outcome. A complete register of all 23 broad American instruments against Japan, Korea and China, compiled from official lists, places 22 of 31 instrument--target pairs after the challenger's exports overtook American exports and only two substantively before, both in the multilateral textile regime. A panel of 1,316 manufacturing groups, displaced or not, shows that targeted broad instruments against Japan and Korea began 5.7 times as often after displacement as before. And starting from displacement rather than from the response, only about one displaced group in five drew a broad instrument within ten years: displacement is close to a necessary condition for broad constraint, but not a sufficient one.

Narrow product remedies follow a different market. Register data on all 759 American and European antidumping and countervailing duty cases against Japan, Korea and China between 1980 and 2015 show that such remedies are routine and start long before sectoral displacement: 316 of 414 cases against China were opened before 2010. They track exposure in the incumbent's home market instead. China's share of American imports of the product had risen in the three years before 82 per cent of the American antidumping cases against it, and among displaced groups the share drawing a case rises from 1 to 30 per cent between the lowest and the highest third of American imports from the challenger. Product remedies follow the product market, sectoral instruments the sectoral market, and the economy-wide tariffs of 2018 followed economy-wide displacement.

Two further results refine the claim. First, escalation follows displacement in sectors with political voice. In the British parliamentary record, Germany becomes an issue in sugar debates after German beet sugar overtook colonial cane in the British market, and in steel and machinery debates when German exports approached or overtook British ones in third markets between 1899 and 1913; German leadership in dyes, which came first, was almost never debated. In the American population, neither import volume nor employment predicts which displaced sectors drew a broad instrument, so what gives a sector voice remains an open question. Second, growth alone does not trigger escalation. The United States overtook Britain in income per head around 1900, but it did not displace Britain in third markets, and British attention to it did not rise.

The paper makes a sequencing claim, not a causal one. It does not estimate the effect of displacement on policy, and it does not claim that incumbent constraint caused the slowdown of convergence that followed in Japan and Korea. What it shows is an ordering that recurs across four structurally different incumbents and that any account of external constraint in development has to explain.

\section{Literature and gap}\label{sec:lit}

Five literatures each cover one side of the relation between displacement and escalation and hold the other side constant.

The developmental-state canon explains how late industrialisers substitute institutions for missing markets: universal banks in Germany \citep{gerschenkron1962}, administrative guidance and controlled technology imports in Japan \citep{johnson1982}, performance-conditioned subsidies in Korea \citep{amsden1989}, governed markets in Taiwan \citep{wade1990} and embedded autonomy more generally \citep{evans1995}. In all of these accounts the incumbent is a benchmark, not an actor. Johnson's account ends in 1975, before the escalation phase of the American response to Japan; Amsden and Wade treat American pressure as part of the environment their planners navigated. \citet{chang2002} goes furthest: today's rich countries used protection and selective imitation on their way up and later demanded that others give them up. What his account lacks is timing. It does not say at which point in the catch-up incumbents begin to restrict, or whether that point recurs.

The convergence literature quantifies where growth slows. \citet{eichengreen2012} find that most of the slowdown in fast-growing economies is a slowdown of total factor productivity growth. \citet{acemoglu2006} model the switch from investment-based to innovation-based growth near the frontier, which stalls when domestic interests block it. The causes considered are domestic. Whether external constraint coincides with, reinforces or merely accompanies the slowdown is not asked.

The political economy of protection explains the cross-section of protection \citep{grossman1994} but not its timing. The closest empirical test of a sequence is \citet{bown2013aer}, who show that recent bilateral import growth raises the probability of a temporary trade barrier, and \citet{bown2013jie}, who relate new barriers to the business cycle. Their unit is the product and the year; they observe no intermediate stage between import growth and protection, and their window is two decades. This paper moves the test to the level of the sector and the dyad, adds an independent measure of political attention, and extends the window to 175 years, long enough to include changes in the instruments themselves.

The literature on technology denial dates controls by strategic threat perception. \citet{mastanduno1992} distinguishes a narrow strategic embargo from broader economic warfare in the Cold War; \citet{lind2025} show that the controls on China since 2022 differ in kind from those of the Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls, because they target the upstream tools of a civilian industry. Neither indexes the timing of controls to measurable displacement.

Economic historians have shown that catch-up economies ran permissive intellectual-property regimes \citep{khan2005,moser2005}, and that the German chemical industry rose under patent rules that protected processes rather than products \citep{murmann2001}. When incumbents began to complain, and whether their complaints followed displacement, has not been tested systematically.

What this paper adds is a dated, sector-level test of the order between displacement and escalation that treats the incumbent's response as the variable to be explained.

\section{Design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Cases}

The four episodes are the largest cases of late industrialisation measured against a frontier incumbent: Germany against Britain before 1914, Japan against the United States from the 1950s, Korea against the United States and, in shipbuilding, Japan, and China against the United States and the European Union. In 1913 Germany's manufactured exports reached 89 per cent of Britain's \citep{tyszynski1951}; Japan's total factor productivity reached 0.80 of the American level in 1991 \citep{pwt110}; China's income per head was 33 per cent of the American level in 2022 \citep{bolt2024}. The cases differ in political system, period, trade regime and alliance structure, which is the point: a regularity that holds across them is unlikely to be an artefact of one setting. They are not independent. Japan learned from Germany, Korea from Japan, China from all three. This works against the claim only if diffusion explains the incumbent's behaviour, which runs the other way.

\subsection{Displacement milestones}

A displacement milestone is the first year in which the challenger leads in the market where the incumbent sells. For Britain, which sold mainly abroad, this is the export market; for the United States, which sold mainly at home, it is the world market or the American home market; for Japan and the European Union it is the world market. Each case has several sectoral milestones, 18 in total (Table~\ref{tab:milestones}). Responses aimed at a sector are dated against that sector's milestone; responses aimed at the challenger's economy as a whole are dated against the case's first milestone.

As a robustness check we apply a uniform export rule: the first year in which the challenger's exports to the world reach those of the incumbent, from UN Comtrade for 1962 onwards \citep{comtrade} and from \citet{tyszynski1951} for Germany. Appendix~E reports where the two rules differ and why.

\subsection{Incumbent responses and their salience}

DS1 lists 41 incumbent responses: speeches and publications, official investigations, binding measures and technology denial. Each is scored on an instrument-reach score (IR) from 1 to 5 that measures how far and how bindingly the instrument constrains the challenger: 1 for rhetoric without an official procedure, 2 for an investigation or complaint, 3 for a narrow binding measure such as origin marking or a duty on a single product, 4 for a binding quantity or price limit on a whole sector, and 5 for tariffs on a large share of imports or the denial of technology to a sector or a national champion. In borderline cases the lower score applies. Measures that only strengthen the incumbent's own economy are excluded. Two coders scored all responses independently; agreement is [Krippendorff's $\alpha$, ordinal: to be reported] \citep{krippendorff2013}.

A response falls in the \emph{absorption phase} if it precedes the milestone of its sector. Its \emph{lag} is the response year minus the milestone year.

\subsection{An independent measure of attention}

Salience scores are judgements. For the German case the paper adds a count that does not depend on them: all debates in the House of Commons and the House of Lords recorded in Historic Hansard between 1850 and 1914 \citep{hansard}, 281,577 in all, of which 15,126 are economic debates by a fixed title filter. We count mentions of Germany (including Prussia and the Zollverein) and of the United States per 100,000 words, and flag debates in which the country appears within 30 words of a competition term or a sector term.

\subsection{Falsification}

The claim fails if binding instruments (IR 4 or 5) systematically precede displacement in the affected sector, or if the lags are fully explained by administrative processing, such as the months between an antidumping petition and a duty order.

\subsection{Germany and Britain}

German firms led the world in synthetic dyes from about 1870 \citep{murmann2001}. In 1899 German chemical exports were already 43 per cent larger than British ones \citep{tyszynski1951}. Britain's first responses were not aimed at chemicals: a Royal Commission on the depression of trade (1885), the Merchandise Marks Act of 1887, which required imports to show their country of origin, and the polemic \emph{Made in Germany} \citep{williams1896}. The first sector-specific binding instrument was the Brussels Sugar Convention of 1902 (IR 4), which abolished export bounties under threat of countervailing duties, 22 years after continental beet sugar overtook cane in the British market. In chemicals, the Patents and Designs Act of 1907 required patents to be worked in Britain, 37 years after the dye milestone. In steel and machinery, Germany displaced Britain in export markets only between 1899 and 1913: German iron and steel exports rose from 35 to 91 per cent of British exports, industrial equipment from 50 to 133 per cent, electrical goods from 58 to 211 per cent \citep{tyszynski1951}. These are the years in which Joseph Chamberlain launched the Tariff Reform campaign (1903). A general tariff came only with the Import Duties Act of 1932.

The British case therefore shows the sequence in its slowest form. No British response precedes a German milestone. The lags of binding measures before 1914 are long, from 17 years for the Merchandise Marks Act to 37 years for the Patents Act; free trade blocked a general tariff until 1932.

\subsection{Japan and the United States}

Japan is the case with the most sectors and the clearest ordering. Japan was again the world's largest exporter of cotton textiles by 1951; Japanese ceilings on cotton exports to the United States followed in December 1955 and a five-year restraint on all cotton textiles in January 1957. Japan became the largest steel exporter in 1969 \citep{ohashi2005}; a voluntary restraint arrangement on Japanese and European steel took effect the same year, and a trigger-price mechanism in 1978. By 1970 Japanese firms supplied about 30 per cent of the American market for medium-sized colour televisions; antidumping duties followed in 1971 and an orderly marketing agreement capping exports at 1.75 million sets a year in 1977. Japan became the world's largest car producer in 1980; the voluntary export restraint of 1.68 million cars a year began in May 1981 \citep{berry1999}. Japanese 64K DRAMs overtook American producers in 1981; the Semiconductor Industry Association petitioned in 1985, the semiconductor agreement followed in 1986 and 100 per cent tariffs on 300 million dollars of Japanese electronics in 1987.

All eleven binding instruments against Japan, ten American and the British and French invocation of GATT Article XXXV in 1955, come after the milestone of their sector, with lags of zero to nine years. The only Japanese response before a milestone is a dumping complaint by the Electronic Industries Association in 1968, two years before the television milestone (IR 2). The steel arrangement of 1969 has a lag of zero and rests on a milestone with a single source; it is the closest case.

\subsection{Korea}

Korea's two responses before a milestone both come from the United States in the mid-1980s: the Section 301 investigation of Korea's intellectual-property regime (1985, IR 2) and the resulting bilateral agreement (1986, IR 3). Both precede Samsung's DRAM leadership of 1992, and both concern intellectual property in general rather than memory chips. Binding trade measures in DRAM, an antidumping order in 1993 and a countervailing duty order in 2003, follow the milestone by one and eleven years. In shipbuilding, Korea overtook Japan around 2000; Japan's complaints to the World Trade Organization followed in 2018 and 2020. The lag of 18 years contrasts with the one-year lag in DRAM, where the injured incumbent, Micron, had a strong voice in Washington.

\subsection{China}

China overtook the United States in manufacturing value added in 2010. Binding responses followed in solar products in 2012 and 2013, Section 301 tariffs from July 2018, the listing of Huawei in May 2019 and tariffs and countervailing duties on electric vehicles in 2024; the lags of binding measures range from zero to eight years. The exception is the American export controls on advanced computing and semiconductor manufacturing equipment of 7 October 2022 (IR 5). They preceded the confirmation of SMIC's 7\,nm production in 2023 by a year and were designed to deny China a capability it had not yet acquired. We treat them as the bounded exception of the dataset: one preventive instrument in one sector, embedded in a campaign of tariffs and listings that followed China's manufacturing, solar and telecommunications milestones.

\subsection{Register evidence: narrow remedies and the level of displacement}

The 41 responses were collected from the literature on major disputes, which may under-record early, small actions. As an independent frame we use the Temporary Trade Barriers Database \citep{ttbd}, which records every antidumping and countervailing duty case of the United States and the European Union, 759 cases against the three Asian challengers from 1980 to 2015. Against Korea, 67 of 181 cases were opened before the DRAM milestone of 1992, 35 of them ending in definitive duties; against China, 316 of 414 cases were opened before the manufacturing milestone of 2010, 231 ending in definitive duties. (The database begins after almost all Japanese milestones and cannot test the Japanese case.)

These cases are narrow by construction: each covers a handful of products, and each would score 2 or 3 on our scale. They are also triggered by displacement, at the level of the product. For the 127 American antidumping cases against China opened between 1996 and 2015 with product codes, China's share of American imports of the products concerned rose from a median of 15 per cent four years before the case to 23 per cent in the year before, and it rose in 82 per cent of the cases \citep{comtrade}. This is consistent with \citet{bown2013aer}, who show that import surges predict temporary barriers. The register data thus confirm the ordering at a finer grain and refine the claim: product displacement is followed by product remedies, sectoral displacement by sectoral instruments, displacement across the economy by broad tariffs and technology denial.

\subsection{A complete register of broad instruments and the population of displaced sectors}

Two further checks address the selection of cases. First, we compile all broad American instruments against Japan, Korea and China from complete sources rather than from the literature on disputes: every Section 201 safeguard that ended in quantity limits or orderly marketing agreements (the USITC's own list, 1975--2016), the bilateral restraint agreements in textiles, steel, cars, machine tools and semiconductors, the Section 232 and 301 tariffs of 2018, the entity listing of Huawei, the export controls of 2022 and the tariff on Chinese electric vehicles of 2024: 23 instruments and 31 instrument--target pairs (Appendix~E). For each pair we date the first year in which the challenger's world exports of the products concerned reached American exports (UN Comtrade, SITC and HS). Twenty-two pairs come after that crossover, five cannot be dated, and four precede it. Two of the four are artefacts of measurement: the 1986 semiconductor agreement precedes the crossover in the broad SITC group, but not the DRAM milestone of 1981, and the steel safeguard of 2002 applied to all suppliers, at a time when China was a minor one. The other two are substantive and point to one sector: the Long-Term Arrangement on cotton textiles (1962) and the bilateral textile agreements of 1971 restrained Korean exports a decade or more before they matched American exports. The multilateral textile regime is the one setting in which incumbents institutionalised restraint of new low-wage suppliers before displacement; it is the second bounded exception, next to the export controls of 2022.

Second, we reverse the direction of the test and start from displacement. Taking every three-digit manufacturing group in which a challenger's exports overtook American exports (41 groups for Japan in 1963--1989, 38 for Korea in 1968--2009, 312 for China in 1993--2023), we ask how often a targeted broad instrument followed within ten years. For Japan the share is 22 per cent, for Korea 20 per cent; for China it is 1 per cent if the Section 301 tariffs of 2018 are left aside, and 74 per cent of the groups that crossed after 2008 if they are included. Most displacement thus draws no broad response at the level of the sector. Displacement is close to a necessary condition for broad constraint, but far from a sufficient one.

Third, we add a control group. A panel of all 1,316 manufacturing group series in which a challenger exported at least 50 million US dollars in some year, displaced or not, compares the rate at which targeted broad instruments begin before and after a group's crossover, with year effects and standard errors clustered by group. For Japan and Korea the rate rises from 0.24 to 1.15 per 100 group-years (incidence rate ratio 5.7, 95 per cent interval 2.3 to 14.0). For China, targeted broad instruments other than the Section 301 tariffs are too rare to estimate (five events), and the tariffs of 2018 covered all 813 groups, displaced or not: an economy-wide instrument that followed economy-wide displacement and did not discriminate between sectors.

At the level of the product the pattern differs. New American antidumping and countervailing cases against the challenger, matched to groups through their HS codes (1988--2015, the years in which the cases carry them), run at 6.5 per 100 group-years after the crossover against 3.4 before. The difference vanishes once the size of the challenger's exports is held constant (rate ratio 1.10, interval 0.75 to 1.60); only for Japan does it survive (2.0, interval 1.1 to 3.7). What predicts a case among displaced groups is the volume of American imports from the challenger: within five years of the crossover, 1 per cent of the groups in the lowest third of import volume drew a case, 11 per cent in the middle third and 30 per cent in the top third. Product-level instruments track exposure in the home market; sector-level instruments track displacement in the markets the incumbent contests. The reach of the measure follows the reach of the market the incumbent loses. Import volume does not predict which displaced groups of Japan and Korea drew a broad instrument (27, 14 and 23 per cent by third); that question is the one the parliamentary evidence on political voice in Section~\ref{sec:voice} begins to answer. Employment, the direct measure of a sector's political weight, gives the same split. American employment in the industries behind a group \citep[NBER-CES;][]{nberces} predicts antidumping cases (1, 16 and 25 per cent by third), but not once import volume is held constant, and it does not significantly predict broad instruments among the 63 displaced groups of Japan and Korea (19, 14 and 29 per cent by third; logit coefficient 0.24, $p=0.43$). With so few broad events, the test has little power; it shows no strong selection of broad instruments on the size of the domestic workforce.

\subsection{Tests}

Within ten years on either side of the milestone, 5 responses fall before and 26 from the milestone onwards (one-sided binomial test, $p=0.0001$); for binding responses the counts are 2 and 18 ($p=0.0002$). Shifting every milestone by a random number of years between $-10$ and $+10$ (10,000 draws) yields a mean post-milestone share of 0.60; the observed share of 0.84 is reached in 2.2 per cent of draws, the binding share of 0.90 in 0.7 per cent. The timing is therefore not an artefact of where in the window the milestones happen to lie. Nor is it an artefact of the business cycle, which predicts temporary trade barriers \citep{bown2013jie}: 38 per cent of the responses fall in years in which the incumbent's output was below its Hodrick-Prescott trend, against 45 per cent of all years in the case windows.

\subsection{Robustness}

Under the uniform export rule, 28 responses follow the export crossover, 3 cannot be placed because only an upper bound for the crossover exists, and 9 cannot be classified because trade statistics do not measure the contested product: DRAM is not separable from logic chips, and China's exports of integrated circuits consist largely of assembled foreign chips. One response precedes the export crossover, the European Union's countervailing duties on Chinese electric cars of October 2024 (IR 4), imposed while Chinese electric-car exports were still below German ones. The two rules thus produce one exception each, in different sectors, and neither produces a pattern of binding escalation before displacement (Appendix~E).

\section{Political voice and the American non-case}\label{sec:voice}

Aggregate parliamentary attention confirms the British sequence. Mentions of Germany in economic debates rose from 14 per 100,000 words in 1850--1869 to 20 in 1870--1892 and about 44 after 1893. A search for structural breaks that does not impose the dates finds the best single break in 1871 and, with two breaks, 1871 and 1894, one year after the dye and the steel milestones. The share of economic debates that mention Germany rose from 3.3 per cent in 1870--1892 to 8.8 per cent in 1903--1914 (Appendix~E, Table E3).

The rise is uneven across sectors (Table~\ref{tab:voice}). In sugar debates, Germany is almost absent before 1880 and appears in about one in six afterwards. In iron and steel debates its share rises from 15 to 24 per cent, with overlapping confidence intervals. Dyes and chemicals were debated only 18 times in 65 years. German leadership in the sector it led first went almost unnoticed in Parliament; German competition in sectors with many producers, workers or colonial interests did not. We use the number of debates on a sector as a first measure of its political voice; census employment is a natural alternative and is left for robustness work.

The United States provides a placebo. American income per head overtook British income around 1900 \citep{bolt2024}. If growth alone triggered escalation, British attention to the United States should have risen too. It did not: mentions of the United States stayed between 41 and 56 per 100,000 words of economic debate in all four periods, and the share of debates linking it to competition stayed near one per cent. The trade data explain why. In a fixed panel of 17 third markets, American exports reached 75 per cent of British exports by 1913 but only 51 per cent outside Canada, against 85 per cent for Germany \citep{fouquin2016}. In manufactures, the American share of world exports rose only from 11.2 to 12.6 per cent between 1899 and 1913, the German share from 22.2 to 26.4 per cent \citep{tyszynski1951}. \citet{crafts1989} finds that electrical goods became Germany's leading sector of revealed comparative advantage by 1913. The United States outgrew Britain at home; Germany displaced Britain in its export markets. The trigger for escalation is displacement in contested markets, in sectors with voice.

\section{Convergence after displacement}\label{sec:convergence}

The second claim is descriptive. Figure~\ref{fig:tfp} shows total factor productivity relative to the United States from the Penn World Table \citep{feenstra2015,pwt110}. Japan peaked at 0.80 in 1991 and fell back to 0.64 by 2023. Korea peaked at 0.71 in 2002 and has stayed between 0.62 and 0.70 since 2010. Germany has been at or above the American level for most years since 2000; part of the rise in the early 1990s reflects unification. China reached 0.47 in 2023, after a pause between 2007 and 2015 at about 0.40 to 0.42.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig2_tfp.pdf}
\caption{Total factor productivity relative to the United States, 1954--2023.}\label{fig:tfp}
\end{figure}

The two completed East Asian cases stalled between 0.70 and 0.80 of the frontier, and they did so after the escalation phase documented above. The paper does not claim that escalation caused the stall. Domestic explanations remain strong \citep{acemoglu2006,eichengreen2012}, and regression to the mean is observationally equivalent in a sample of this size \citep{pritchett2014}. China's pause began at a much lower level, which suggests domestic misallocation rather than an approach to the frontier. Whether China escapes the band at which Japan and Korea stalled is a question the historical cases cannot answer.

\section{Limits and conclusion}\label{sec:limits}

The evidence has clear limits. Four cases and 41 responses are a small sample, and the cases are linked. The dataset selects on successful catch-up; economies that absorbed technology without displacing anyone, such as Thailand and Vietnam, are the natural negative controls and are left for future work. Several milestones rest on one or two secondary sources, and the trade statistics do not always measure the contested product. The population check measures displacement by world exports and matches antidumping cases to SITC groups through a concordance of 1988--92 HS codes, which understates displacement in the American home market, and the target countries of several Section 201 actions are taken from secondary literature. The parliamentary check is available only for Britain: machine-readable full text of the American Congressional Record before 1994 is not openly available. The title filter for economic debates misses economic discussion under other headings. Each of these limits is documented in Appendix~E.

Within these limits the ordering is robust. Across four incumbents and 175 years, the reach of constraint follows the reach of displacement in contested markets, in sectors with political voice. Narrow remedies against single products run throughout catch-up and follow import exposure in the incumbent's home market; quantity limits on whole sectors, broad tariffs and technology denial come only once the challenger has taken the incumbent's markets in a sector. Displacement is close to a necessary condition for such broad constraint, not a sufficient one: most displaced sectors never draw it. The lag has shortened from decades in free-trade Britain to months in the American response to China, as each episode left the next with new instruments: antidumping law, Section 301, the entity list, export controls on equipment. The October 2022 controls show that the window can close before displacement when the incumbent regards a technology as strategic.

For late industrialisers the implication is practical. The phase in which foreign technology can be absorbed without systematic resistance ends not with imitation but with success, when the challenger begins to take the incumbent's markets in sectors that matter politically to the incumbent. Planning for that moment is part of development strategy.
