\title{Trade Policy Amnesia: When Leading Economies Turn Against Late Industrialisers, 1850-2024}

\begin{abstract}
Leading economies often forget that they once caught up through protection and imitation and turn against the next latecomer. This paper asks when that turn comes and how far it reaches. We date the years in which Germany, Japan, Korea and China first led in 18 sectors against Britain, the United States, Japan and the European Union, and score 41 responses by how far they reach. Narrow duties on single products follow imports and appear throughout catch-up. Limits on whole sectors come as the challenger approaches and takes the incumbent's markets in that sector: in a panel of 1,316 manufacturing groups, broad measures against Japan and Korea start more often the closer a group's exports come to American exports, at given export size. Tariffs on almost all goods came only when displacement covered much of the economy, as for China in 2018. Displacement is close to necessary but not sufficient: about one displaced group in five drew a sectoral measure within ten years, mostly sectors Congress had debated heavily before. Allies negotiated restraints; the rival met unilateral tariffs and export controls. Two exceptions came earlier: the textile regime, which restrained every low-wage supplier, and the controls on advanced chips of 2022, aimed at a military rival. Eight further pairs of countries show the same pattern. The results establish an order in time; they do not identify a causal effect. For latecomers they imply that resistance arrives with success and can be anticipated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Leading economies tend to forget how they themselves caught up. The United States protected its industries behind high tariffs in the nineteenth century \citep{chang2002} and gave foreign authors no copyright until 1891 \citep{khan2005}. In 1985 it opened a trade investigation against Korea for weak protection of intellectual property. Japan was itself shut out of British and French trade concessions when it joined the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) in 1955 and met American quotas on its textiles soon after. In 2018 it took Korea to the World Trade Organization (WTO) over subsidies to shipyards. Friedrich List described the pattern in 1841: a country that has reached the top ``kicks away the ladder'' by which it climbed \citep[pp.~4-5]{chang2002}. We call this forgetting \emph{trade policy amnesia}.

This paper asks when the amnesia sets in and how far it reaches. The question matters for any economy that is catching up. If the leader resists from the start, outside resistance is a condition of development from day one. If it resists only once the newcomer takes its markets, resistance is a price of success that can be foreseen. Development economics has studied how latecomers build capabilities and why their growth slows near the frontier. It has mostly treated the leading economy as a fixed benchmark.

We build a dated record of four episodes over 175 years: Germany against Britain (1850-1914), Japan against the United States (1950-1990), Korea against the United States and Japan (1960-2020) and China against the United States and the European Union (1978-2024). For each sector we date the year in which the challenger first led in the market where the incumbent sells. We call this year the \emph{displacement milestone}; there are 18 of them. We then list 41 documented responses of the incumbents and score each by how far it reaches, from a speech (1) to a tariff on most of the challenger's exports or a ban on technology sales (5). Complete registers check this record: all 759 American and European antidumping and countervailing duty cases against Japan, Korea and China, all 23 broad American measures against them, and a panel of 1,316 manufacturing groups. Records of parliamentary debate in London and Washington measure political attention independently of the coding.

The main result is that leading economies respond to what they lose, and the reach of the response matches the reach of the loss. Narrow duties on single products follow imports and appear throughout catch-up. Of 414 such cases against China, 314 came before China led in the sector concerned. Limits on whole sectors come later, as the challenger approaches and overtakes the incumbent in that sector. Of the 41 coded responses, 36 come after the milestone of their sector. In the panel, broad measures against Japan and Korea start more often the closer a group's exports come to American exports. At given export size, a doubling of the ratio of the challenger's exports to American exports raises the start rate by 30 per cent. The rate rises in the five years before the crossover; parity itself is not a sharp threshold. Tariffs on almost all goods came only when displacement covered much of the economy. In 2018 the groups China had overtaken made up 45 per cent of American manufactured exports, and the Section 301 tariffs of that year covered every group, whether China led in it or not.

Displacement is close to necessary for broad measures, but far from sufficient. Only about one displaced group in five drew a broad measure within ten years. Which ones did depended on political voice that existed before displacement. In the five years before Japanese exports overtook American ones, Congress debated the sectors that later drew a broad measure six times as often as the sectors that did not. For Korea the ratio is four. Against China, the measures went to sectors seen as strategic. Alignment shaped the form of the response: allies negotiated restraints, and the rival met unilateral tariffs, blacklists and export controls. Two exceptions came before displacement, and each is confined to one sector: the textile regime and the American controls on advanced chips of October 2022. Eight further pairs of countries show the same pattern.

\emph{Related literature.} The paper adds to three literatures. The first explains how latecomers catch up. In \emph{Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective}, universal banks fill the gap left by missing markets in Germany \citep{gerschenkron1962}. Later work traces the role of the state in Japan, Korea and Taiwan \citep{johnson1982,amsden1989,wade1990,evans1995} and the technological capabilities that firms and countries build \citep{lall1992}. East Asian electronics firms learned through subcontracting for foreign buyers \citep{hobday1995}. Studies of China show how its exports competed with those of other developing countries \citep{lall2004,gallagher2008} and how its segmented domestic markets formed a ladder for upgrading, from which restrictive policies at times knocked out rungs \citep{brandt2016}. This work treats the leading economy as a fixed environment. \emph{Kicking Away the Ladder} comes closest to our question: today's rich countries used protection and imitation on their way up and later asked others to give them up \citep{chang2002}. Views on protection for latecomers have shifted over time: import substitution lost favour among economists from the mid-1960s, as country evidence on its costs accumulated \citep{irwin2021}. The book does not say at which point of catch-up the leaders begin to resist. We date that point and tie it to a measurable event.

The second literature studies the political economy of protection. Protection goes to well-organised industries with much at stake \citep{grossman1994}. Import surges and downturns make temporary trade barriers more likely \citep{bown2013aer,bown2013jie}, and antidumping responds to macroeconomic pressure in developed and developing countries alike \citep{aggarwal2004}. These studies cover two or three decades and the instruments that WTO law permits. We extend the test from the product to the sector and the economy, and over 175 years, long enough for the instruments themselves to change. We also show when the demand for protection becomes active: at displacement, in sectors with a voice.

The third literature studies how rich countries and international rules constrain latecomers. Cold War export controls ranged from a narrow strategic embargo to broader economic warfare \citep{mastanduno1985,mastanduno1992}, and the controls on China since 2022 target the tools of a civilian industry \citep{lind2025}. The openness of the trading system depends on the distribution of power \citep{krasner1976}, and states trade more with allies \citep{gowa1993}. WTO rules and the conditions of multilateral loans narrowed the policy space of latecomers \citep{wade2003,rodrik2006,kentikelenis2016}. Countries that vote with the United States receive more aid and more programmes of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) \citep{thacker1999,barro2005,kuziemko2006,dreher2009eer,dreher2009jde,stubbs2016}. None of these studies links the constraint to the latecomer's position in the incumbent's markets. We show that the timing of constraint follows displacement and its form follows alignment.

Three works are closest to ours. \emph{Kicking Away the Ladder} shows that leaders turned against the policies they had used \citep{chang2002}; we date when, and show that resistance rises with the challenger's position relative to the incumbent. \citet{bown2013aer} show that import surges predict temporary trade barriers; we find the same for product duties and show that sectoral and economy-wide measures follow a different market, the incumbent's own exports. \emph{Clashing over Commerce} places the American conflicts with Japan in the long history of United States trade policy \citep{irwin2017}; we compare four incumbents and test the timing against complete registers.

The claim is about order in time. The paper does not estimate the causal effect of displacement on policy. We use \emph{amnesia} to name a pattern, the turn of a former latecomer against the policies it once used, not to describe what policymakers remember; the examples above illustrate it, and the paper does not measure it. Section~\ref{sec:background} describes the episodes and the instruments. Section~\ref{sec:framework} states the hypotheses and Section~\ref{sec:data} the data. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} sets out the empirical strategy. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, Section~\ref{sec:extensions} extends them to further cases, to the form of the response and to two exceptions, and Section~\ref{sec:validity} discusses threats to validity. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws implications for late industrialisers today, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Four episodes of catch-up}

The four core episodes are among the largest cases of catch-up against a leading economy. In 1913 Germany's manufactured exports reached 89 per cent of Britain's \citep{tyszynski1951}. Japan's total factor productivity reached 0.80 of the American level in 1991 \citep{pwt110}. China's income per head was 33 per cent of the American level in 2022 \citep{bolt2024}. The cases differ in political system, period, trade rules and alliances. A pattern that holds across them is therefore unlikely to be the product of one setting. The cases are not independent: Japan learned from Germany, Korea from Japan and China from all three \citep{kohli1994}. But the challengers' learning does not explain the incumbents' behaviour, which is what we study. Section~\ref{sec:further} adds eight further pairs of countries.

\subsection{The incumbent's instruments}

Incumbents have used instruments of very different reach. At the narrow end are antidumping and countervailing duties on single products. A few firms facing a surge of imports can file a petition, and the duty applies to the products named in it. Next come limits on whole sectors. Section 201 of the Trade Act of 1974 grants temporary relief to an injured industry. Agreements in which the exporting country caps its shipments are known as voluntary export restraints or orderly marketing agreements. In textiles, a standing regime ran from the Short-Term Arrangement of 1961 and the Long-Term Arrangement on cotton textiles of 1962 to the Multifibre Arrangement of 1974 and its end in 2005. At the broad end are measures that cover a whole economy or deny it technology. Section 232 allows measures on grounds of national security, and Section 301 allows retaliation against unfair foreign practices. The Entity List requires a licence for exports to listed firms, and the Export Administration Regulations govern export controls.

The rules changed over the period. Britain kept free trade until the Import Duties Act of 1932. GATT allowed members to withhold its treatment from a new member under Article XXXV, as Britain and France did with Japan in 1955. The WTO Agreement on Safeguards of 1995 (Article 11) outlawed negotiated restraints. Each episode thus faced a different set of tools.

\section{Conceptual framework}\label{sec:framework}

Our argument is that an incumbent responds to what it loses, in the market where it loses it, with a measure of matching reach. Three markets matter, and each yields a hypothesis.

\emph{H1: Product duties follow imports.} The incumbent first loses sales of single products to imports in its home market. Its answer is antidumping or countervailing duties. Because these duties are cheap to obtain, they should appear early in catch-up and follow imports, not the challenger's standing in world markets.

\emph{H2: Sectoral limits follow the loss of a sector, in sectors with a voice.} Later the incumbent loses its lead in the market where a whole industry sells: export markets for Britain before 1914, the world and the home market for the United States. Its answer is a limit on the whole sector. Such limits need an organised industry and the attention of the legislature or the government. They should follow the sector's displacement, and among displaced sectors they should go to those with political voice.

\emph{H3: Economy-wide measures follow the loss of the economy.} Finally the incumbent loses ground across a large share of its tradable output. Its answer is a tariff on most imports from the challenger or a ban on selling technology to its leading firms. Such measures should follow displacement across the economy and need not pick sectors.

\emph{H4: Alignment shapes the form.} An incumbent can negotiate restraints with an ally or act alone against a rival. Form should vary with alignment; timing should vary with displacement.

The argument fails if broad measures regularly come before displacement in the sector concerned. It also fails if the delay between displacement and response is only paperwork, such as the months between a petition and a duty order, or if product duties follow the challenger's world-market position rather than imports.

\subsection{Two measures of displacement}

The \emph{displacement milestone} is the first year in which the challenger leads in the market where the incumbent sells. For Britain this is the export market; for the United States the world market or its home market; for Japan and the European Union the world market. Table~\ref{tab:milestones} lists the 18 milestones, dated from the historical and industry literature (Appendix~B). A response aimed at a sector is dated against that sector's milestone. A response aimed at the whole economy is dated against the case's first milestone.

The \emph{export crossover} is a mechanical second measure. It is the first year in which the challenger's world exports in a manufacturing group reach those of the incumbent, with a floor of 50 million US dollars and a lead held in three of the next four years. It uses UN Comtrade \citep{comtrade} at the level of three-digit groups of the Standard International Trade Classification (SITC; Revision 1 for 1962-1999, Revision 3 for 1992-2009) and four-digit headings of the Harmonized System (HS; 2008-2023). For Germany before 1914, benchmark years of world trade in manufactures take its place \citep{tyszynski1951}. The milestone serves the case histories; the crossover serves the registers and the panel. Fig.~\ref{fig:exports} shows both measures for four sectors.

\subsection{Responses and their reach}

The core dataset lists 41 responses of the incumbents documented in the literature on the four episodes: speeches and publications, official investigations, binding measures and bans on technology sales (Appendix, Table C.1). Each response gets a reach score (IR) from 1 to 5. A speech without an official procedure scores 1, an investigation or complaint 2, and a narrow binding measure 3, such as origin marking or a duty on one product. A binding limit on a whole sector, or a trade law that creates new sanction powers, scores 4. A tariff on a large share of imports, or a ban on technology sales to a sector or a leading firm, scores 5. In doubtful cases the lower score applies (Appendix~A). Measures that only strengthen the incumbent's own economy, such as subsidies under the CHIPS and Science Act, are excluded. We call measures that score 4 or 5 \emph{broad}. To check the coding, four large language models (ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok and DeepSeek) scored all 48 responses and the 42 entries of the register of broad measures blind: they saw only the coding rules and a description of each measure, in random order, without dates relative to the milestones and without the first scores. Agreement with the first coder, measured by the ordinal $\alpha$ of \emph{Content Analysis} \citep{krippendorff2013}, is 0.87 against the median of the four models (0.91 for the responses, 0.76 for the register); among the four models it is 0.89. The main disagreements concern single-product tariffs, such as the American and European duties on Chinese electric vehicles of 2024, which the models score 3 and we score 4 (Appendix~A).

\subsection{Registers and panel}

The 41 responses come from the literature on major disputes. Three checks therefore use complete sources. The Temporary Trade Barriers Database \citep{ttbd} records all 759 antidumping and countervailing duty cases of the United States and the European Union against Japan, Korea and China from 1979 to 2015. A register of broad measures lists all 23 American measures scoring 4 or 5 against these three challengers from 1957 to 2024, or 31 pairs of measure and target. It includes every Section 201 safeguard that ended in quantity limits or orderly marketing agreements, and the restraint agreements in textiles, steel, cars, machine tools and semiconductors. Further entries are the tariffs of 2018 under Sections 232 and 301, the addition of Huawei to the Entity List, the export controls of 2022 and the tariff on Chinese electric vehicles of 2024. Finally, a panel follows every manufacturing group in which a challenger exported at least 50 million dollars in some year. It holds 1,316 group series for Japan, Korea and China, and marks each group as displaced from its export crossover onwards.

\subsection{Political attention}

Two text sources measure attention independently of the coding. For Britain we use all 281,577 debates of the House of Commons and the House of Lords from 1850 to 1914 recorded in Historic Hansard \citep{hansard}; a fixed filter on their titles selects 14,383 economic debates. For the United States we use the Congressional Record \citep{crec}: the daily edition from 1995 to 2025 in full, and for 1955-1994, when only scanned volumes exist, 20 randomly drawn sitting days per year (800 days). We count mentions of the challenger, and passages in which it appears within 30 words of a word for competition or for a sector. How much a legislature talks about a sector measures that sector's political voice. The share of that talk that names the challenger measures attention to the challenger (Appendices E and G).

\section{Empirical strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

The design places every response in event time. A response falls in the \emph{absorption phase} if it comes before the milestone of its sector. Its \emph{lag} is the response year minus the milestone year. If the incumbent resisted from the start of catch-up, responses would spread over the absorption phase; if it resists only after displacement, they would cluster at non-negative lags. We test this in three ways.

First, a window test counts responses in the ten years before and the ten years from the milestone and compares the split with an even one (one-sided binomial test). Second, a placebo test shifts every milestone by a random number of years between $-10$ and $+10$, 10,000 times, and asks how often a share of post-milestone responses as high as the observed one occurs by chance. Third, the panel compares each displaced group with groups not yet displaced in the same year. For group $g$ and year $t$ we estimate
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{E}[y_{gt}] = \exp(\alpha_t + \beta D_{gt} + \gamma' x_{gt}),
\end{equation}
where $y_{gt}$ counts the measures starting in group $g$ in year $t$, $D_{gt}$ equals one from the group's export crossover onwards, $\alpha_t$ are year effects and $x_{gt}$ holds optional controls such as the size of the challenger's exports. The rate ratio $\exp(\beta)$ compares how often measures start after the crossover with how often they start before. Because measures cannot target products the challenger barely sells, $x_{gt}$ includes the log of the challenger's exports and of American imports from it. We also replace $D_{gt}$ by the log ratio of the challenger's to the incumbent's exports, which measures relative position without a threshold, and estimate an event study with bins of years relative to the crossover, against groups never displaced. Standard errors are clustered by group (Appendix~D).

To ask which displaced sectors were hit (H2), we compare displaced sectors with and without a broad measure. The comparison uses congressional debate volume before the crossover, American employment \citep{nberces} and American imports from the challenger, with exact permutation tests that suit the small number of sectors. To ask about form (H4), we code every broad measure as negotiated or unilateral and match it to the state of the relationship in that year: a defence pact from the Alliance Treaty Obligations and Provisions data \citep{leeds2002}, the distance between voting positions in the UN General Assembly \citep{bailey2017} and the ratio of national capabilities from the Correlates of War data \citep{nmc}.

The design identifies an order in time, not a causal effect. Three threats matter most. Incumbents might anticipate displacement and act before it, which would bias the design against our finding. A common shock such as a downturn might raise both imports and protection; we check the business cycle directly. And the core responses might over-represent major disputes; the registers and the panel use complete sources for this reason.

\subsection{Responses follow displacement}\label{sec:sequence}

Only one broad measure came before displacement: the export controls of October 2022 (Section~\ref{sec:exceptions}).

\emph{Germany and Britain.} Britain's response to Germany came late and was mostly general. German firms led the world in synthetic dyes from about 1870 \citep{murmann2001}, and by 1899 German chemical exports were 43 per cent larger than British ones \citep{tyszynski1951}. Britain first answered with a Royal Commission on the depression of trade (1885) and the Merchandise Marks Act of 1887, which required foreign goods bearing British names or trade marks to state their country of origin. The polemic \emph{Made in Germany} followed in 1896 \citep{williams1896}. The first binding measure for a single sector was the Brussels Sugar Convention of 1902 (IR 4). It abolished export subsidies on sugar under threat of countervailing duties, 16 years after imports from beet-growing countries overtook cane in the British market. In chemicals, the Patents and Designs Act of 1907 required patents to be worked in Britain, 37 years after the dye milestone. In steel and machinery, Germany displaced Britain in export markets only between 1899 and 1913. In those years German exports of iron and steel rose from 35 to 91 per cent of British exports, of industrial equipment from 50 to 133 per cent and of electrical goods from 58 to 211 per cent \citep{tyszynski1951}. Joseph Chamberlain launched his campaign for tariff reform in 1903, but free trade blocked a general tariff until 1932. No British response came before a German milestone. Binding measures followed 17 to 37 years after the milestone.

\emph{Japan and the United States.} The American response to Japan followed each sector within a few years. Japan was again the world's largest exporter of cotton textiles by 1951. Japanese controls on cotton exports to the United States followed late in 1955, and a five-year restraint on all cotton textiles in January 1957. Japan became the largest steel exporter in 1969 \citep{ohashi2005}. A restraint on Japanese and European steel took effect the same year, and a minimum import price for steel, the trigger-price mechanism, in 1978. By 1970 Japanese firms supplied about 30 per cent of the American market for medium-sized colour televisions; antidumping duties followed in 1971 and a cap of 1.75 million units a year from 1977. Japan became the world's largest car producer in 1980, and a restraint of 1.68 million cars a year began in May 1981 \citep{berry1999}. Japanese 64K memory chips (dynamic random-access memory, DRAM) overtook American producers in 1981. The semiconductor agreement followed in 1986, and 100 per cent tariffs on 300 million dollars of Japanese electronics in 1987. All eleven binding measures against Japan come after the milestone of their sector, with lags of zero to nine years: ten American ones, and the British and French refusal in 1955 to extend GATT treatment to Japan under Article XXXV. The closest case is the steel restraint of 1969. Its lag is zero, and its milestone rests on a single source.

\emph{Korea.} The two responses to Korea before a milestone concern intellectual property in general, not memory chips: the Section 301 investigation of 1985 (IR 2) and the agreement of 1986 (IR 3). Binding measures on Korean DRAM chips, an antidumping order in 1993 and a countervailing duty order in 2003, came one and eleven years after Samsung became the largest DRAM maker in 1992. In shipbuilding, Korea overtook Japan in new orders in 1999. Japan's complaints to the WTO followed only in 2018 and 2020.

\emph{China.} China overtook the United States in manufacturing value added in 2010. Binding responses followed: duties on solar products in 2012 and 2013, Section 301 tariffs from July 2018, the listing of Huawei in May 2019 and duties on electric vehicles in the United States and the European Union in 2024. Binding measures followed one to eight years after the milestone.

\emph{Tests.} With measure dates, chance does not produce this timing. Within ten years on either side of the milestone, 5 responses fall before and 26 after (one-sided binomial test, $p=0.0001$). For binding responses the counts are 2 and 18 ($p=0.0002$). When every milestone is shifted at random, on average 60 per cent of responses fall after the shifted milestone. The observed share of 84 per cent occurs in 2.5 per cent of the draws, and the binding share of 90 per cent in 1.1 per cent. The business cycle does not explain the timing either, although downturns raise temporary trade barriers \citep{bown2013jie}. Only 38 per cent of the responses fall in years in which the incumbent's output was below its trend, against 45 per cent of all years in the case windows. 

\emph{Initiation dates.} A measure can follow the milestone while the decision behind it came earlier. We therefore date each binding response by the start of the formal process behind it: the petition filed, the investigation opened, the bill introduced or the negotiations opened (Appendix, Table C.3). The median delay from initiation to measure is one year. Three binding processes began one or two years before the milestone although the measure came after it: the steel talks of June 1968, the antidumping complaint on television receivers of March 1968 and the review of the Section 301 tariffs opened in May 2022, which raised the tariff on Chinese electric vehicles. With initiation dates, 20 of 25 binding responses still begin from the milestone, and the window test holds (5 against 15, $p=0.021$). The placebo, however, no longer separates the observed share from chance ($p=0.14$). The timing evidence is therefore weaker than the measure dates suggest. Formal processes often begin as the challenger approaches the lead, which matches the gradient in the panel (Section~\ref{sec:reach}).

\emph{Robustness.} The result holds with the export crossover instead of the milestone. Then 28 responses follow the crossover. Three cannot be placed because only an upper bound for the crossover exists. Nine cannot be classified because trade statistics do not measure the contested product: DRAM chips are not separable from logic chips, and China's exports of integrated circuits are largely assembled foreign chips. One response comes before the crossover: the European Union's countervailing duties on Chinese electric cars of October 2024 (IR 4), imposed while Chinese exports of electric cars were still below German ones (32.0 against 41.1 billion dollars). Each rule thus produces one exception, in different sectors, and neither shows broad measures before displacement. Of the 23 responses with an exactly dated crossover, none comes before it, with measure dates or with initiation dates. Two further checks address the dating of milestones and the dependence between responses. Without the advanced-chips milestone, which marks a capability rather than a market lead, 23 of 24 binding responses come from the milestone (placebo $p=0.003$). Counting each milestone once, the first binding response comes from the milestone in 13 of 15 cases (one-sided sign test, $p=0.004$), but in only 10 of 15 with initiation dates ($p=0.15$) (Appendix~C).

\subsection{The reach of the response matches the reach of the loss}\label{sec:reach}

Each kind of measure follows a different market (Table~\ref{tab:reach}). Product duties follow imports. Sectoral limits follow the loss of a sector. Tariffs on everything follow the loss of much of the economy.

\emph{Product duties follow imports (H1).} Antidumping and countervailing duty cases are routine during catch-up. Against Korea, 67 of 181 American and European cases were opened before the DRAM milestone of 1992, and 35 of them ended in duties. Against China, 314 of 414 cases were opened before the milestone of their sector, for most of them the manufacturing milestone of 2010; 229 ended in duties. The database begins after almost all Japanese milestones. Each case covers a handful of products and would score 2 or 3 on our scale.

These cases follow imports. In the 127 American antidumping cases against China of 1996-2015 that carry product codes, China's median share of American imports of the products concerned rose from 15 per cent four years before the case to 23 per cent in the year before. This matches the finding that import surges predict temporary trade barriers \citep{bown2013aer}. The cases do not follow displacement in the world market: at given export size, they start about as often after a group's crossover as before (rate ratio 1.10, interval 0.75 to 1.60; Fig.~\ref{fig:rates}). Among displaced groups, imports predict a case and employment does not, once imports are held constant (Table~\ref{tab:reach}). A petition needs a few firms hit by imports, and that is what the data show.

\emph{Sectoral limits follow the loss of a sector (H2).} Broad measures come after the challenger overtakes the incumbent in the products concerned. The register places 22 of 31 pairs of measure and target after that crossover. Five cannot be dated, and four come before it. Two of the four reflect how the groups are drawn. The semiconductor agreement of 1986 precedes the crossover of the broad SITC group, but not the DRAM milestone of 1981. The steel safeguard of 2002 applied to all suppliers, at a time when China was a minor one. The other two are real and concern one sector, textiles. The Long-Term Arrangement on cotton textiles of 1962, applied to Korea from 1963, and the textile agreements of 1971 restrained Korean exports nine and two years before they matched American exports (Section~\ref{sec:exceptions}).

The panel shows that resistance builds with relative position (Fig.~\ref{fig:rates}). The raw start rate of broad measures against Japan and Korea is 5.7 times as high after a group's crossover as before (interval 2.3 to 14.0). Much of this gap reflects scale: at given export size the ratio falls to 2.3 (1.0 to 5.2), and with the incumbent's exports of the group added it vanishes. What survives every control is a gradient. A doubling of the challenger's exports relative to American exports raises the start rate by 30 per cent (10 to 52 per cent), and the rate is already elevated in the five years before the crossover (Appendix, Table D.3). For seven further economies the picture is the same: the raw ratio of 5.8 falls to 0.9 at given export size, and a doubling of relative exports raises the rate by 17 per cent. In plain terms, the incumbent begins to resist as the challenger approaches parity in a group, not at the moment it passes it. Yet most displaced groups never met such a measure. Of the groups in which Japanese exports overtook American exports between 1963 and 1989, 22 per cent (9 of 41) drew a broad measure within ten years. For Korea the share is 20 per cent of the 25 groups that crossed between 1968 and 1992, and none of the 13 that crossed between 1997 and 2009. Section~\ref{sec:selection} asks which groups did.

\emph{Tariffs on everything follow the loss of the economy (H3).} How much of the economy a challenger displaced differs by an order of magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:breadth}). The groups Japan had overtaken accounted for 24 to 36 per cent of American manufactured exports in 1977-1987. The groups China had overtaken accounted for 45 per cent in 2018. For Korea and Taiwan the share stayed below 6 per cent before 1990 \citep{comtrade}. The response grew with this share. Korea and Taiwan met measures in single sectors. Japan met broad measures in six sectors between 1957 and 1987: textiles, steel, television sets, cars, machine tools and semiconductors. China met the Section 301 tariffs of 2018. They covered all 813 HS groups in which China exported at least 50 million dollars: the 609 it had displaced and the 204 it had not. Other broad measures against China were too rare to estimate a panel effect (five events; rate ratio 1.13, interval 0.11 to 11.24). Of the 242 groups that China overtook after 2008, 74 per cent drew a broad measure within ten years if the tariffs of 2018 are counted, and 1 per cent if they are not. Why Japan's broad displacement was answered sector by sector and China's at once is a question of form (Section~\ref{sec:form}).

\subsection{Which displaced sectors are hit}\label{sec:selection}

Political voice decided which displaced sectors were hit (H2). Size alone did not. Among the displaced groups of Japan and Korea, the share drawing a broad measure within ten years is 27, 14 and 23 per cent by third of American imports from the challenger. By third of American employment it is 19, 14 and 29 per cent (logit coefficient 0.24, $p=0.43$, 63 groups). With so few broad measures these tests have little power, but they show no strong selection on imports or jobs. Parliamentary records point to voice that existed before displacement (Table~\ref{tab:voice} and Fig.~\ref{fig:attention}).

\emph{Britain and Germany.} The British Parliament took up German competition sector by sector. Mentions of Germany in British economic debates rose from 14 per 100,000 words in 1850-1869 to 20 in 1870-1892 and about 44 after 1893. A search for breaks in this series, without fixing the dates in advance, finds the best single break in 1871. With two breaks it finds 1871 and 1894, one year after the dye and the steel milestones. The rise is uneven across sectors (Table~\ref{tab:voice}, panel A). Germany appears in 2 of 75 sugar debates before 1880, in 5 of 23 in 1880-1885, before imports from beet countries overtook cane, and in about one in six from 1886. This debate ended in the Brussels Convention. In iron and steel the share of debates linked to Germany rises from 15 to 24 per cent, with overlapping intervals. Dyes and chemicals, where German leadership came first, were debated only 18 times in 65 years. Parliament appears to have taken up German competition where it hurt sectors with many producers, workers or colonial interests.

Growth alone did not draw attention. American income per head overtook British income around 1900 \citep{bolt2024}. Yet mentions of the United States stayed between 41 and 56 per 100,000 words of economic debate in all four periods, and the share of debates linking it to competition stayed between 0.7 and 2.1 per cent. The phrase ``American invasion'' does not occur once in the 14,383 economic debates. Trade data explain why. In a fixed panel of 17 third markets, American exports reached 75 per cent of British exports by 1913, but only 51 per cent outside Canada, against 85 per cent for Germany \citep{fouquin2016}. In manufactures, the American share of world exports rose only from 11.2 to 12.6 per cent between 1899 and 1913, the German share from 22.2 to 26.4 per cent \citep{tyszynski1951}. The United States outgrew Britain at home; Germany took its export markets (Appendix~F).

\emph{Congress, Japan and Korea.} Congress debated the sectors that would later be protected long before they lost. On the sampled sitting days of 1955-1994, attention to Japan as a competitor stayed low through the 1960s and 1970s, when Japanese textiles, steel and television sets overtook American exports. It rose in the 1980s; the best single break falls in 1980, the year of the car milestone. Attention to Korea stayed at about a tenth of that level. Two measures must be kept apart here: how much Congress talks about a sector, and how much of that talk names the challenger. The second does not separate displaced sectors that met broad measures from those that did not ($p$ between 0.11 and 0.59). The first does, and already before displacement. In the five years before the crossover, Congress mentioned the six Japanese sectors that later drew a broad measure 111 times per million words: textiles, steel, television sets, cars, semiconductors and machine tools. It mentioned the eight sectors without one 17 times, among them ships, footwear, toys and tyres ($p=0.006$). For Korea the figures are 62 and 15 ($p=0.020$).

Debate about a sector goes with the size of the American industry. We therefore regress the indicator for a broad measure on log debate volume, log American employment and log American imports from the challenger across all 28 sectors. Debate volume keeps its effect (permutation $p=0.011$), employment loses its effect once volume is included ($p=0.33$) and imports keep a borderline one ($p=0.084$). The result is the same at the level of the 63 displaced groups. Debate volume may also reflect protection that predates displacement. Adding an indicator for the Korean sectors already protected against Japan (textiles, television sets and steel) lowers its coefficient from 0.18 to 0.14 (permutation $p=0.059$); without textiles and steel it is 0.14 ($p=0.054$). Voice before displacement thus partly reflects earlier protection, and the evidence for it weakens to the margin of conventional significance. Displacement appears to have set the timing, and voice that existed before it, partly built by earlier protection, to have chosen the sectors.

\emph{Congress and China.} China does not fit this pattern. Its trade status was itself the subject of legislation in the 1990s. Attention in economic debates peaked with permanent normal trade relations in 2000, at 399 mentions per 100,000 words, before most Chinese crossovers. A search for breaks finds no rise after them. Across 17 sectors, those that later drew a sector-specific measure had more debate, and more debate linked to China, than displaced sectors without one, but none of the differences is significant (Table~\ref{tab:voice}, panel B). Congressional voice does not predict how heavily the first Section 301 lists of 2018 hit a sector \citep[Spearman $\rho=0.06$;][]{ustr301}. It is only weakly linked to the sector increases of 2024 ($p=0.056$). With employment and imports held constant, the sectors that drew measures had lower American imports from China ($p=0.025$). The measures went to steel, aluminium, solar cells, washing machines, telecom equipment, semiconductors and cars. Consumer-goods sectors with larger average imports from China, such as computers, furniture, toys and footwear, were spared. Lists 1 and 2 of the Section 301 tariffs, the first to take effect at 25 per cent, concentrated on machinery, electronics and vehicles. Against a challenger that displaced the whole economy, the incumbent appears to have chosen sectors by their strategic importance.

\section{Extensions}\label{sec:extensions}

\subsection{Further cases}\label{sec:further}

The core episodes all ended in broad resistance. We therefore test the argument on eight further pairs, chosen before looking at the responses. They are Japan against Britain in the 1930s; four economies that displaced American exports in a few sectors (Taiwan, Hong Kong, Brazil and Mexico); and three usually described as having absorbed technology without displacing anyone (Thailand, Malaysia and Vietnam). For the American pairs we add 19 entries to the register from the Statutes at Large and the Federal Register \citep{statutes,moore1996,dinopoulos1991}, and re-run the crossover rule and the panel. Taiwan's exports are measured by the imports of all reporters from ``Other Asia, not elsewhere specified''. Hong Kong's exclude re-exports (Appendix~H).

\emph{Japan against Britain.} Britain defended the markets it lost, not the one it kept. Japanese cotton piece goods overtook British goods in the import market of British India in 1932 (552 against 542 million yards; \citealp{hansard}, 4 July 1934). In 1933 Japan's world exports of piece goods passed Britain's, with 2,090 against 2,031 million square yards \citep{robson1957,sandberg1974}. By value, Japanese exports were still 73 per cent of British ones in 1935, because Japanese cloth was cheaper \citep{lon1945}. Displacement was regional. Across 35 third markets, Japan's total exports rose only from 23 to 33 per cent of Britain's between 1924 and 1936. In seven Asian markets they rose from 42 to 86 per cent over the same years, and in the Netherlands Indies Japan led from 1930 \citep{fouquin2016}. The response was regional too. All four binding measures (IR 4) came from the milestone onwards. In August 1932 India raised its duty on non-British piece goods to 50 per cent, ``in consequence of the abnormal imports from Japan''. A 75 per cent duty followed in June 1933, an Indo-Japanese quota in January 1934 and colonial quotas on foreign cotton and rayon goods in May 1934. Before the milestone there were only an economic mission to the Far East and India's small preference of 1930, which also protected Indian mills against Lancashire. Mentions of Japan in British economic debates stayed between 1 and 10 per 100,000 words in 1920-1931. They reached 12 in 1932 and 102 in 1933, with a single break in 1932. The British home market, where Japan had not overtaken Britain, received no measure aimed at Japan.

\emph{Newly industrialised economies.} Where displacement was sectoral, so were the measures. Taiwanese footwear overtook American exports in 1970 and met an orderly marketing agreement in 1977. Brazilian steel overtook American exports in 1983 and was included in the restraint agreements of 1984. Three measures do not follow displacement. The colour-television agreement of 1979 and the machine-tool restraint of 1986 extended measures aimed at Japan to Taiwan, and the steel agreements of 1984 extended them to Mexico, in groups where these suppliers had not overtaken American exports. The incumbent closed the route around its earlier measures. The 100 per cent tariffs on Brazilian goods of 1988 retaliated against Brazil's refusal to patent pharmaceuticals. Broad measures against the seven economies began 5.8 times as often after a group's crossover as before (95 per cent interval 2.8 to 12.0), but this ratio does not survive a control for export size (0.9); the start rate rises with relative exports instead, as for Japan and Korea. The estimates leave aside the end of tariff preferences for Hong Kong and Taiwan in 1989, an economy-wide but rule-based step. Displacement again was far from sufficient. A sectoral broad measure followed within ten years in 4 of 9 displaced groups for Brazil, all of them steel, in 1 of 24 for Taiwan and in none of 9 for Hong Kong outside the textile regime.

\emph{Negative controls.} Economies that displaced little met little. At their peak, the groups Thailand and Malaysia had overtaken accounted for 2 and 6 per cent of American manufactured exports. Apart from the quotas of the textile regime, no broad American measure was aimed at either. Vietnam and Mexico are harder cases. By 2023 Vietnam had overtaken American exports in 101 groups worth 6 per cent of American manufactured exports, and Mexico had reached 7 per cent. Neither drew a broad measure aimed at it before 2025. None of the 283 groups that Thailand, Malaysia, Mexico, Vietnam, Taiwan and Brazil overtook between 2009 and 2023 drew one within the period observed. In this period the WTO Agreement on Safeguards had outlawed the sectoral restraints of the earlier cases. The broad tariffs of 2025 on Mexico and Brazil were justified by migration, drugs and the prosecution of a former Brazilian president. The ``reciprocal'' tariffs of April 2025 covered all trading partners and were justified by bilateral deficits \citep{statutes}.

The further cases thus confirm the pattern. Sectoral displacement in Taiwan and Brazil drew sectoral measures, and marginal displacement in Thailand, Malaysia and Hong Kong outside textiles drew none. They add two points. Incumbents extend measures aimed at the main challenger to its followers before these displace them. And they also use broad tariffs for purposes that have nothing to do with displacement.

\subsection{Alignment and the form of the response}\label{sec:form}

Displacement sets the date of the response; alignment shapes its form (H4). Before 1995 the United States negotiated with its allies. It aimed 25 broad, trade-motivated measures at treaty allies (Japan, Korea, Taiwan until 1980, Brazil, Mexico and Thailand); 21 of them were negotiated restraints. The four unilateral ones were the trigger price for steel (1978), the motorcycle tariff (1983) and the Section 301 tariffs on Japanese electronics (1987) and on Brazilian goods (1988). After 1995 no such measure was aimed at an ally. All five hit economies without a defence pact with the United States: four unilateral measures against China, from the Section 301 tariffs of 2018 to the tariff on electric vehicles of 2024, and the textile agreement with Vietnam of 2003. Britain's binding responses to Japan in the 1930s, in a pair without an alliance, were three unilateral measures and one agreement. This would explain why Japan's broad displacement was answered with negotiated restraints sector by sector and China's with one unilateral tariff on every group. Two changes coincide in 1995, however, and the data cannot separate them. The WTO Agreement on Safeguards outlawed negotiated restraints, and the one challenger that displaced the United States across the economy after 1995 was also its main strategic rival (Appendix~I).

Alignment does not predict whether a displacing economy meets resistance. Mexico, an ally, and Vietnam, without an alliance, both displaced the United States in many groups after 2008. Neither drew a broad measure aimed at it before 2025. Their distance from American positions in the General Assembly was 2.5 and 3.4 points.

Loans from the IMF offer a third channel of constraint, but they do not follow displacement. We use the coded conditions of 1,173 programme years in 116 countries between 1989 and 2014 \citep{stubbs2020}. Closer voting with the United States goes with a higher chance of a programme, as earlier studies found \citep{thacker1999,barro2005}, and with more binding conditions; neither link survives country fixed effects. The challengers in our data entered programmes after debt or financial crises: Mexico in 1989-1992, 1995-1996 and 1999-2000, Korea in 1998-2000, Brazil in 1999 and 2001-2004 and Vietnam in 1994-1996 and 2001-2002. At those times, the groups they had overtaken accounted for at most 2.3 per cent of American manufactured exports. Their programmes carried twice as many conditions on trade and the external sector as other programmes with the same alignment, income, growth and openness (rate ratio 2.16), but no more conditions overall. Conditions narrowed the policy space of borrowers \citep{kentikelenis2016}; they were not aimed at the sectors in which the borrowers displaced the incumbent (Appendix~J).

\subsection{Two exceptions}\label{sec:exceptions}

Two sets of broad measures came before displacement. Each is confined to one sector.

\emph{The textile regime.} In textiles, incumbents restrained newcomers before these displaced anyone. The register shows this for every such supplier in our data. Korea was restrained under the Long-Term Arrangement from 1963, by a bilateral agreement from 1965 and by the agreements of 1971, nine and two years before its exports in the groups concerned matched American ones. The first comprehensive American cotton agreements with Hong Kong and Taiwan, both of 1963, came two and eight years before their textile and clothing exports overtook American exports. The agreements with Mexico (1967), Brazil and Malaysia (1970) and Thailand (1972) restrained suppliers that never overtook American exports in these groups. Vietnam's clothing met a bilateral quota in 2003, three years before its clothing exports as a whole overtook American ones, although two clothing groups (SITC 841 and 842) had done so in 2002. Textiles were an early export sector of the low-wage suppliers in the register, and the incumbent's textile industry was mature and politically organised. The regime turned the sectoral measure into a standing rule for each new supplier.

\emph{Export controls on advanced chips.} The American controls on advanced computing and chip-making equipment of 7 October 2022 (IR 5) came a year before the Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation (SMIC) was confirmed to produce chips at 7\,nm in 2023. They aimed to deny China a capability it did not yet have. They are the only broad measure among the 41 coded responses that comes before the milestone of its sector. They came in a campaign of tariffs and listings that followed China's milestones in manufacturing, solar cells and telecom equipment. And they fall in the one pair in which the challenger was a military peer. When the controls were imposed, China's distance from American positions in the General Assembly was 3.5 points, against 1.1 to 1.5 for Japan in the years of its milestones. China's national capabilities were 1.9 times those of the United States, on a measure that weights population and heavy industry. Germany's capabilities stood at 0.44 to 0.79 of Britain's in the years of its milestones, Japan's at 0.26 to 0.40 of America's and Korea's at 0.15 to 0.16. In this record, technology denial before displacement was used only against a rival.

\section{Threats to validity}\label{sec:validity}

\emph{Sample.} Four core cases and 41 responses are a small sample, and the cases are linked. The core includes only successful catch-up. The further cases add negative controls, but Thailand and Malaysia displaced too little to test much, and the displacement by Vietnam and Mexico falls in a period without sectoral restraints.

\emph{Coding.} The reach scores come from one human coder, checked against a blind coding by four language models ($\alpha=0.87$). A model coding is not a substitute for an independent human coder, and agreement for the register (0.76) falls short of the conventional threshold of 0.80. With the models' scores, 13 instead of 15 of the 41 responses are broad, and one of them still comes before its milestone. Several milestones rest on one or two secondary sources, among them Korean shipbuilding (1999, by orders, with Japan ahead again in 2001). Some depend on the measure: Chinese telecom equipment is a near tie in total revenue in 2012 that turns on exchange rates. The target countries of several Section 201 actions come from secondary literature.

\emph{Measurement.} The crossover rule measures displacement in world exports, which understates displacement in the American home market, and trade statistics do not always capture the contested product. Antidumping cases are matched to groups through HS codes of 1988-1992, and employment is allocated to groups by tariff lines. Taiwan's exports are measured from partner imports. For Japan against Britain, the bilateral trade data cover total exports, not cotton goods, and the British and Japanese series for piece goods are not fully comparable.

\emph{Attention.} Economic debates are selected by title, which misses economic talk under other headings. In the daily Congressional Record the filter captures only one in five passages that link China to competition. Before 1995 the American measure rests on 800 sampled sitting days with errors from the text recognition of the scans, too few for sector counts in single years. Debate measures attention, not lobbying or votes.

\emph{Identification.} After 1995, the lack of an alliance and displacement across the economy coincide in one case, China. The data cannot separate rivalry from the reach of displacement or from the WTO's ban on negotiated restraints. Above all, the evidence orders events; it does not identify the effect of displacement on policy. Two results narrow the claim further. In the panel, resistance rises with the challenger's relative exports rather than jumping at parity, and much of the raw contrast before and after the crossover reflects scale. With the dates on which formal processes began, the placebo test loses its significance. The paperwork criterion of Section~\ref{sec:framework} is thus only partly met: measures follow the milestone, but the processes behind some of them begin in the year or two before it. The amnesia itself is not measured: the paper shows when resistance comes, not what incumbents remember. Appendix~K documents each limit.

\section{Implications for late industrialisers}\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{The window for absorbing technology closes with success.} The phase in which foreign technology can be absorbed without systematic resistance appears to end when the latecomer begins to take the leader's markets in sectors that matter politically to the leader. Product duties come earlier, but they follow imports and stay narrow. A latecomer can therefore plan for two different costs: routine duties while its exports grow, and broader limits once it leads.

\emph{Breadth predicts reach.} How far the response will reach can be read from how far success reaches. Korea and Taiwan displaced American exports in groups worth less than 6 per cent of American manufactured exports before 1990 and met measures in single sectors. Japan reached 24 to 36 per cent and met broad measures in six sectors; China reached 45 per cent and met a tariff on every group. Table~\ref{tab:breadth} sets out the record for all ten challengers. Four economies are now at or above the level at which Korea and Taiwan met sectoral measures before 1990: Taiwan (9 per cent in 2023), Mexico (7 per cent), Vietnam (6 per cent) and Malaysia (4.5 per cent). None drew a sectoral measure aimed at its displacement after 1995, when negotiated restraints became illegal. On this record, a latecomer can track the share of the leader's exports it has displaced as an early warning, but the threshold at which this share now triggers a response is not known.

\emph{Alignment shapes the form, and the rules have changed.} Before 1995 allies negotiated restraints with the incumbent. Since 1995 the WTO Agreement on Safeguards has outlawed such restraints. The measures against China after 2018 were unilateral, and the ``reciprocal'' tariffs of 2025 covered all trading partners. For latecomers today, the softer negotiated form of the past may no longer be on offer.

\emph{Convergence may stall after the response.} Total factor productivity relative to the United States peaked at 0.80 in Japan in 1991 and at 0.71 in Korea in 2002, after the escalation documented here (Fig.~\ref{fig:tfp}). Japan fell back to 0.64 by 2023, and Korea has stayed between 0.62 and 0.70 since 2010 \citep[Appendix~L]{feenstra2015,pwt110}. We do not claim that resistance caused the stall. Domestic explanations remain strong \citep{acemoglu2006,eichengreen2012,mcmillan2014,lee2021}, and in a sample of this size the pattern cannot be told apart from regression to the mean \citep{pritchett2014}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Over four incumbents and 175 years, leading economies resisted latecomers in a predictable order. Narrow duties on single products run throughout catch-up and follow imports. Limits on whole sectors come as the challenger approaches and takes the incumbent's markets in a sector, and only in some displaced sectors: those with a voice in the incumbent's legislature, or, against a strategic rival, those its government regards as strategic. Tariffs on almost everything come when displacement covers much of the economy. Alliances decide whether the incumbent negotiates or acts alone. The delay between displacement and binding response has shortened, from 17 to 37 years in free-trade Britain to zero to nine years against Japan and one to eight against China. Each episode appears to have left the next with new tools: antidumping law, Section 301, the Entity List and export controls on equipment. The textile regime and the controls of October 2022 show that incumbents can act before displacement when an industry is organised for it or a technology is held to be strategic.

The trade policy amnesia of the introduction fits this order. Leaders appear to have tolerated imitation and protection while the latecomer was absorbing technology, and to have turned against both once the latecomer took their markets. The United States, which once denied copyright to foreign authors, opened its case on Korean intellectual property in 1985, seven years before Korea led in memory chips. Japan, once itself restrained, complained about Korean shipyard subsidies almost two decades after Korea overtook it in orders. The memory of one's own catch-up seems to fade when one's markets are at stake. For latecomers, planning for that moment would therefore belong to development strategy.
