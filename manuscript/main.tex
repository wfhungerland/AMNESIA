\title{Trade Policy Amnesia: How Far Leading Economies Go Against Late Industrialisers, 1850-2024}

\begin{abstract}
Leading economies often forget that they once caught up through protection and imitation, and they turn against the next latecomer. We ask which instruments they use and what decides their form. We date the years in which Germany, Japan, Korea and China first led in 18 sectors against Britain, the United States, Japan and the European Union, and score 41 responses by reach. The reach of the response matches the breadth of the loss. Narrow duties on single products follow imports and appear throughout catch-up. Limits on whole sectors come later and go where a large challenger meets a weak incumbent industry: in a panel of 1,316 manufacturing groups, broad measures against Japan and Korea went to groups where the challenger's exports were large relative to weak American exports. Tariffs on almost all goods came only when displacement covered much of the economy. Only about one displaced group in five drew a sectoral measure within ten years, more often in sectors Congress had debated heavily before. Alignment and the rules of the period shaped the form: allies negotiated restraints, while the rival met unilateral tariffs and export controls. Two exceptions came earlier: the textile regime and the controls on advanced chips of 2022. The statistical tests cover the American response to Japan and Korea; the other episodes rest on case evidence. The results establish an order, not a causal effect. For latecomers, the rungs can be anticipated, and their form depends on the latecomer's place in the leader's alliances.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Leading economies tend to forget how they themselves caught up. The United States protected its industries behind high tariffs in the nineteenth century \citep{chang2002} and gave foreign authors no copyright until 1891 \citep{khan2005}. In 1985 it opened a trade investigation against Korea for weak protection of intellectual property. When Japan joined the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) in 1955, Britain and France shut it out of their trade concessions. Soon after, it met American quotas on its textiles. In 2018 Japan took Korea to the World Trade Organization (WTO) over subsidies to shipyards. Friedrich List described the pattern in 1841: a country that has reached the top ``kicks away the ladder'' by which it climbed \citep[pp.~4-5]{chang2002}. We call this forgetting \emph{trade policy amnesia}. Leaders kick away the latecomer's ladder by using a ladder of their own: instruments of rising reach.

This paper asks which instruments leading economies use against latecomers, in what order, and what decides their form. The question matters for any economy that is catching up. If the leader uses its broadest tools from the start, outside resistance is a condition of development from day one. If it climbs from narrow to broad tools as the latecomer succeeds, each step is a price of success that can be foreseen. Development economics has studied how latecomers build capabilities and why their growth slows near the frontier. It has mostly treated the leading economy as a fixed benchmark.

We build a dated record of four episodes over 175 years: Germany against Britain (1850-1914), Japan against the United States (1950-1990), Korea against the United States and Japan (1960-2020) and China against the United States and the European Union (1978-2024). For each sector we date the year in which the challenger first led in the market where the incumbent sells. We call this year the \emph{displacement milestone}; there are 18 of them. We then list 41 documented responses of the incumbents and score each by how far it reaches, from a speech (1) to a tariff on most of the challenger's exports or a ban on technology sales (5). Complete registers check this record: all 759 American and European antidumping and countervailing duty cases against Japan, Korea and China, all 22 broad American measures against them, and a panel of 1,316 manufacturing groups. Records of parliamentary debate in London and Washington measure political attention independently of the coding.

The main result is that the reach of resistance matches the reach of the loss, on a ladder of three rungs. Episodes need not climb it in order. China met economy-wide tariffs in 2018, before any sectoral limit in our coded responses. Narrow duties on single products follow imports and appear throughout catch-up. Of 414 such cases against China, 314 came before China led in the sector concerned. Limits on whole sectors come later. Of the 41 coded responses, 36 come after the milestone of their sector. In the panel, broad measures against Japan and Korea went to groups where the challenger's exports were large relative to American exports. At given export size, a doubling of the ratio of the challenger's exports to American exports raises the start rate by 33 per cent. The rate rises once the challenger's exports reach a quarter of the incumbent's; parity itself is not a threshold. The gradient holds when standard errors allow for the few decisions behind the measures, and when any single measure is left out. It does not reflect import pressure alone. The panel cannot tell, however, whether it reflects the challenger's rise or the weakness of the American industry: the American trade balance against the rest of the world predicts the measures as well as the export ratio does. Tariffs on almost all goods came only when displacement covered much of the economy. In 2018 the groups China had overtaken made up 45 per cent of American manufactured exports. The Section 301 tariffs of that year covered every group, whether China led in it or not.

Displacement is far from sufficient for broad measures. Only about one displaced group in five drew a broad measure within ten years. Which ones did appears to have depended on political voice that existed before displacement. This evidence, however, weakens to the margin of significance once earlier protection is held constant. In the five years before Japanese exports overtook American ones, Congress debated the sectors that later drew a broad measure six times as often as the sectors that did not. For Korea the ratio is four. Against China, the measures went to sectors seen as strategic. Alignment shaped the form of each rung: allies negotiated restraints, and the rival met unilateral tariffs, blacklists and export controls. Two exceptions came before displacement. Each is confined to one sector: the textile regime and the American controls on advanced chips of October 2022. Eight further pairs of countries show a similar but weaker pattern.

\emph{Related literature.} The paper adds to three literatures. The first explains how latecomers catch up. In \emph{Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective}, universal banks fill the gap left by missing markets in Germany \citep{gerschenkron1962}. Later work traces the role of the state in Japan, Korea and Taiwan \citep{johnson1982,amsden1989,wade1990,evans1995} and the technological capabilities that firms and countries build \citep{lall1992}. East Asian electronics firms learned through subcontracting for foreign buyers \citep{hobday1995}. Studies of China show how its exports competed with those of other developing countries \citep{lall2004,gallagher2008}. Its segmented domestic markets formed a ladder for upgrading, and restrictive policies at times knocked out rungs \citep{brandt2016}. This work treats the leading economy as a fixed environment. \emph{Kicking Away the Ladder} comes closest to our question: today's rich countries used protection and imitation on their way up and later asked others to give them up \citep{chang2002}. Views on protection for latecomers have shifted over time. Import substitution lost favour among economists from the mid-1960s, as country evidence on its costs accumulated \citep{irwin2021}. \emph{Kicking Away the Ladder} does not say at which point of catch-up the leaders begin to resist. We describe the instruments in the order leaders use them and date when each appears.

The second literature studies the political economy of protection. Protection goes to well-organised industries with much at stake \citep{grossman1994}. Import surges and downturns make temporary trade barriers more likely \citep{bown2013aer,bown2013jie}, and antidumping responds to macroeconomic pressure in developed and developing countries alike \citep{aggarwal2004}. These studies cover two or three decades and the instruments that WTO law permits. We extend the test from the product to the sector and the economy, and over 175 years, long enough for the instruments themselves to change. We also show which displaced sectors drew sectoral limits: those where a large challenger met a weak domestic industry and, more tentatively, those with a political voice.

The third literature studies how rich countries and international rules constrain latecomers. Cold War export controls ranged from a narrow strategic embargo to broader economic warfare \citep{mastanduno1985,mastanduno1992}. The controls on China since 2022 target the tools of a civilian industry \citep{lind2025}. The openness of the trading system depends on the distribution of power \citep{krasner1976}. States trade more with allies \citep{gowa1993}. WTO rules and the conditions of multilateral loans narrowed the policy space of latecomers \citep{wade2003,rodrik2006,kentikelenis2016}. Countries that vote with the United States receive more aid and more programmes of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) \citep{thacker1999,barro2005,kuziemko2006,dreher2009eer,dreher2009jde,stubbs2016}. None of these studies links the constraint to the latecomer's position in the incumbent's markets. We show that the reach of constraint follows the breadth of the latecomer's success and that its form follows alignment.

Three works are closest to ours. \emph{Kicking Away the Ladder} shows that leaders turned against the policies they had used \citep{chang2002}. We show in what order they did so and how far each step reached. \citet{bown2013aer} show that import surges predict temporary trade barriers. We find the same for product duties. We also show that sectoral and economy-wide measures follow a different market: the incumbent's own exports. \emph{Clashing over Commerce} places the American conflicts with Japan in the long history of United States trade policy \citep{irwin2017}. We compare four incumbents and test the timing against complete registers.

The quantitative tests cover the American response to Japan and Korea, 1962-1999. Germany against Britain, China against the United States and the European Union, and the further cases are documented as case evidence and checked against registers where possible. The claim is about order in time. The paper does not estimate the causal effect of displacement on policy. We use \emph{amnesia} to name a pattern, the turn of a former latecomer against the policies it once used, not to describe what policymakers remember. The examples above illustrate it; the paper does not measure it. Section~\ref{sec:background} describes the episodes and the instruments. Section~\ref{sec:framework} states the hypotheses and Section~\ref{sec:data} the data. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} sets out the empirical strategy. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results: the ladder, its timing, the choice of sectors and the form of the response. Section~\ref{sec:extensions} extends them to further cases and two exceptions. Section~\ref{sec:validity} discusses threats to validity. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws implications for late industrialisers today, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Four episodes of catch-up}

The four core episodes are among the largest cases of catch-up against a leading economy. In 1913 Germany's manufactured exports reached 89 per cent of Britain's \citep{tyszynski1951}. Japan's total factor productivity reached 0.80 of the American level in 1991 \citep{pwt110}. China's income per head was 33 per cent of the American level in 2022 \citep{bolt2024}. The cases differ in political system, period, trade rules and alliances. A pattern that holds across them is therefore unlikely to be the product of one setting. The cases are not independent: Japan learned from Germany, Korea from Japan and China from all three \citep{kohli1994}. But the challengers' learning does not explain the incumbents' behaviour, which is what we study. Section~\ref{sec:further} adds eight further pairs of countries.

\subsection{The incumbent's instruments}

Incumbents have used instruments of very different reach. At the narrow end are antidumping and countervailing duties on single products. A few firms facing a surge of imports can file a petition, and the duty applies to the products named in it. Next come limits on whole sectors. Section 201 of the Trade Act of 1974 grants temporary relief to an injured industry. Agreements in which the exporting country caps its shipments are known as voluntary export restraints or orderly marketing agreements. In textiles, a standing regime ran from the Short-Term Arrangement of 1961 and the Long-Term Arrangement on cotton textiles of 1962 to the Multifibre Arrangement of 1974 and its end in 2005. At the broad end are measures that cover a whole economy or deny it technology. Section 232 allows measures on grounds of national security, and Section 301 allows retaliation against unfair foreign practices. The Entity List requires a licence for exports to listed firms, and the Export Administration Regulations govern export controls.

The rules changed over the period. Britain kept free trade until the Import Duties Act of 1932. GATT allowed members to withhold its treatment from a new member under Article XXXV, as Britain and France did with Japan in 1955. The WTO Agreement on Safeguards of 1995 (Article 11) outlawed negotiated restraints. Each episode thus faced a different set of tools.

\section{Conceptual framework}\label{sec:framework}

Our argument is that resistance follows a ladder of reach. An incumbent responds to what it loses, in the market where it loses it, with a measure of matching reach. Three markets give three rungs, and each yields a hypothesis. A fourth hypothesis concerns the form.

Part of this matching is built into the law. An antidumping or countervailing duty requires imports and injury to producers of the product, and Section 201 relief requires injury to a whole industry. The hypotheses therefore go beyond the legal triggers: product duties should not follow the challenger's standing in world markets; sectoral limits should go to some displaced sectors and not others; and the economy-wide rung should appear only when displacement is broad. A ladder of reach also need not be climbed in order. A challenger that displaces the incumbent across the economy at once may meet the top rung without the middle one.

\emph{H1: The first rung, product duties, follows imports.} The incumbent first loses sales of single products to imports in its home market. Its answer is antidumping or countervailing duties. These duties are cheap to obtain. They should therefore appear early in catch-up and follow imports, not the challenger's standing in world markets.

\emph{H2: The second rung, sectoral limits, rises with the loss of a sector, in sectors with a voice.} Later the incumbent loses ground in the market where a whole industry sells. For Britain before 1914 these were its export markets. For the United States they were the world and the home market. Its answer is a limit on the whole sector. Such limits need an organised industry. They also need the attention of the legislature or the government. As the challenger's exports approach and pass the incumbent's, the industry loses more. Its political pressure grows with its losses. Sectoral limits should therefore become more likely as the challenger's relative position rises, not only at parity. Relative position, not import volume alone, may drive them. If it does, it should predict them at given imports. A competing account needs no displacement at all: industries with a comparative disadvantage seek protection against any large supplier \citep{grossman1994}. If that account holds, the incumbent's weakness should predict limits as well as the challenger's position does. Among displaced sectors, sectoral limits should go to those with political voice.

\emph{H3: The third rung, economy-wide measures, follows the loss of the economy.} Finally the incumbent loses ground across a large share of its tradable output. Its answer is a tariff on most imports from the challenger. Such a measure should follow displacement across the economy and need not pick sectors. Technology denial is a separate branch, not the top of this ladder. A ban on selling technology to a rival's sector or leading firm targets capabilities, and it need not wait for displacement.

\emph{H4: Alignment shapes the form of each rung.} An incumbent can negotiate restraints with an ally or act alone against a rival. Form should vary with alignment and with the rules of the period; timing should vary with displacement. The evidence on form is limited, because alignment and the rules changed together in 1995.

The argument fails if broad measures regularly come before the challenger approaches the incumbent in the sector concerned. It fails if their timing follows import volume rather than relative position. It also fails if the delay between displacement and response is only paperwork, such as the months between a petition and a duty order. Finally, it fails if product duties follow the challenger's world-market position rather than imports.

\subsection{Two measures of displacement}

The \emph{displacement milestone} is the first year in which the challenger leads in the market where the incumbent sells. For Britain this is the export market; for the United States the world market or its home market; for Japan and the European Union the world market. Table~\ref{tab:milestones} lists the 18 milestones, dated from the historical and industry literature (Appendix~B). A response aimed at a sector is dated against that sector's milestone. A response aimed at the whole economy is dated against the case's first milestone.

The \emph{export crossover} is a mechanical second measure. It is the first year in which the challenger's world exports in a manufacturing group reach those of the incumbent, with a floor of 50 million US dollars and a lead held in three of the next four years. It uses UN Comtrade \citep{comtrade} at the level of three-digit groups of the Standard International Trade Classification (SITC; Revision 1 for 1962-1999, Revision 3 for 1992-2009) and four-digit headings of the Harmonized System (HS; 2008-2023). For Germany before 1914, benchmark years of world trade in manufactures take its place \citep{tyszynski1951}. The milestone serves the case histories; the crossover serves the registers and the panel. Fig.~\ref{fig:exports} shows both measures for four sectors.

\subsection{Responses and their reach}

The core dataset lists 41 responses of the incumbents documented in the literature on the four episodes: speeches and publications, official investigations, binding measures and bans on technology sales (Appendix, Table C.1). Each response gets a reach score (IR) from 1 to 5. A speech without an official procedure scores 1, an investigation or complaint 2, and a narrow binding measure 3, such as origin marking or a duty on one product. A binding limit on a whole sector, or a trade law that creates new sanction powers, scores 4. A tariff on a large share of imports, or a ban on technology sales to a sector or a leading firm, scores 5. In doubtful cases the lower score applies (Appendix~A). Measures that only strengthen the incumbent's own economy, such as subsidies under the CHIPS and Science Act, are excluded. We call measures that score 4 or 5 \emph{broad}. To check the coding, four large language models (ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok and DeepSeek) scored all 48 responses and the 42 entries of the register of broad measures blind. They saw only the coding rules and a description of each measure, in random order. They saw neither dates relative to the milestones nor the first scores. We measure agreement with the first coder by the ordinal $\alpha$ of \emph{Content Analysis} \citep{krippendorff2013}. It is 0.87 against the median of the four models (0.91 for the responses, 0.76 for the register). Among the four models it is 0.89. The main disagreements concern single-product tariffs, such as the American and European duties on Chinese electric vehicles of 2024. The models score these 3; we scored them 4. We reviewed all 14 disagreements against the rules. We revised eight scores, among them the European duties on electric vehicles, and kept six (Appendix, Table A.3). All results use the revised scores; the agreement figures refer to the original ones.

\subsection{Registers and panel}

The 41 responses come from the literature on major disputes. Three checks therefore use complete sources. The Temporary Trade Barriers Database \citep{ttbd} records all 759 antidumping and countervailing duty cases of the United States and the European Union against Japan, Korea and China from 1979 to 2015. A register of broad measures lists all 22 American measures scoring 4 or 5 against these three challengers from 1957 to 2024, or 30 pairs of measure and target. It includes every Section 201 safeguard that ended in quantity limits or orderly marketing agreements, and the restraint agreements in textiles, steel, cars, machine tools and semiconductors. Further entries are the tariffs of 2018 under Sections 232 and 301, the addition of Huawei to the Entity List, the export controls of 2022 and the tariff on Chinese electric vehicles of 2024. Finally, a panel follows every manufacturing group in which a challenger exported at least 50 million dollars in some year. It holds 1,316 group series for Japan, Korea and China, and marks each group as displaced from its export crossover onwards.

\subsection{Political attention}

Two text sources measure attention independently of the coding. For Britain we use all 281,577 debates of the House of Commons and the House of Lords from 1850 to 1914 recorded in Historic Hansard \citep{hansard}. A fixed filter on their titles selects 14,383 economic debates. For the United States we use the Congressional Record \citep{crec}, with the daily edition from 1995 to 2025 in full. For 1955-1994 only scanned volumes exist, so we use 20 randomly drawn sitting days per year (800 days). We count mentions of the challenger, and passages in which it appears within 30 words of a word for competition or for a sector. How much a legislature talks about a sector measures that sector's political voice. The share of that talk that names the challenger measures attention to the challenger (Appendices E and G).

\section{Empirical strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

The design places every response in event time. A response falls in the \emph{absorption phase} if it comes before the milestone of its sector. Its \emph{lag} is the response year minus the milestone year. If the incumbent resisted from the start of catch-up, responses would spread over the absorption phase. If it resists only after displacement, they would cluster at non-negative lags. We test this in three ways.

First, a window test counts responses in the ten years before and the ten years from the milestone. It compares the split with an even one (one-sided binomial test). Second, a placebo test shifts every milestone by a random number of years between $-10$ and $+10$, 10,000 times. It asks how often a share of post-milestone responses as high as the observed one occurs by chance. Third, the panel compares each displaced group with groups not yet displaced in the same year. For group $g$ and year $t$ we estimate
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{E}[y_{gt}] = \exp(\alpha_t + \beta D_{gt} + \gamma' x_{gt}),
\end{equation}
where $y_{gt}$ counts the measures starting in group $g$ in year $t$, $D_{gt}$ equals one from the group's export crossover onwards, $\alpha_t$ are year effects and $x_{gt}$ holds optional controls such as the size of the challenger's exports. The rate ratio $\exp(\beta)$ compares how often measures start after the crossover with how often they start before. Because measures cannot target products the challenger barely sells, $x_{gt}$ includes the log of the challenger's exports and of American imports from it. We also replace $D_{gt}$ by the log ratio of the challenger's to the incumbent's exports, which measures relative position without a threshold. And we estimate an event study with bins of years relative to the crossover, against groups never displaced. One measure often covers several groups. We therefore cluster standard errors by SITC two-digit division, leave out each large measure in turn and test at the level of measures (Appendix~D).

To ask which displaced sectors were hit (H2), we compare displaced sectors with and without a broad measure. The comparison uses congressional debate volume before the crossover, American employment \citep{nberces} and American imports from the challenger, with exact permutation tests that suit the small number of sectors. To ask about form (H4), we code every broad measure as negotiated or unilateral. We then match it to the state of the relationship in that year: a defence pact from the Alliance Treaty Obligations and Provisions data \citep{leeds2002}, the distance between voting positions in the UN General Assembly \citep{bailey2017} and the ratio of national capabilities from the Correlates of War data \citep{nmc}.

The design identifies an order in time, not a causal effect. Three threats matter most. Incumbents might anticipate displacement and act before it, which would bias the design against our finding. A common shock such as a downturn might raise both imports and protection; we check the business cycle directly. And the core responses might over-represent major disputes; the registers and the panel use complete sources for this reason.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A ladder of resistance}\label{sec:reach}

Resistance has three rungs, and each follows a different market (Table~\ref{tab:reach}). Product duties follow imports. Sectoral limits go where a large challenger meets a weak incumbent industry. Tariffs on everything follow the loss of much of the economy.

The rungs are a scale, not a sequence that every episode climbs (Table~\ref{tab:rungs}). Only Germany met them in order, and its top rung, the general tariff of 1932, was not aimed at Germany. Japan met sectoral limits on cotton in 1955 before its first coded product duty in 1971; the antidumping database begins only in 1979. China met economy-wide tariffs in 2018 and its first coded sectoral limit only in 2024. China's displacement arrived broad and fast, and the WTO had outlawed the negotiated sectoral restraints of the earlier episodes.

\emph{The first rung: product duties follow imports (H1).} Antidumping and countervailing duty cases are routine during catch-up. Against Korea, 67 of 181 American and European cases were opened before the DRAM milestone of 1992, and 35 of them ended in duties. Against China, 314 of 414 cases were opened before the milestone of their sector, and 229 ended in duties. For most of them the milestone is the manufacturing milestone of 2010. The database begins after almost all Japanese milestones. Each case covers a handful of products and would score 2 or 3 on our scale.

These cases follow imports. In the 127 American antidumping cases against China of 1996-2015 that carry product codes, China's median share of American imports of the products concerned rose from 15 per cent four years before the case to 23 per cent in the year before. This matches the finding that import surges predict temporary trade barriers \citep{bown2013aer}. The cases do not follow displacement in the world market. At given export size, they start about as often after a group's crossover as before (rate ratio 1.10, interval 0.75 to 1.60; Fig.~\ref{fig:rates}). Among displaced groups, imports predict a case and employment does not, once imports are held constant (Table~\ref{tab:reach}). A petition needs a few firms hit by imports, and that is what the data show.

\emph{The second rung: sectoral limits (H2).} Broad measures come after the challenger overtakes the incumbent in the products concerned. The register places 21 of 30 pairs of measure and target after that crossover. Five cannot be dated, and four come before it. Two of the four reflect how the groups are drawn. The semiconductor agreement of 1986 precedes the crossover of the broad SITC group, but not the DRAM milestone of 1981. The steel safeguard of 2002 applied to all suppliers, at a time when China was a minor one. The other two are real and concern one sector, textiles. The Long-Term Arrangement on cotton textiles of 1962 applied to Korea from 1963. It and the textile agreements of 1971 restrained Korean exports nine and two years before they matched American exports (Section~\ref{sec:exceptions}).

The panel shows that resistance builds with relative position (Fig.~\ref{fig:rates}). Against Japan and Korea, the raw start rate of broad measures is 6.7 times as high after a group's crossover as before (interval 2.7 to 16.6). Much of this gap reflects scale. At given export size the ratio falls to 2.9 (1.3 to 6.4). With the incumbent's exports of the group added, it falls to 1.3 (0.5 to 3.8). What survives every control is a gradient. A doubling of the challenger's exports relative to American exports raises the start rate by 33 per cent. The interval is 17 to 51 per cent with standard errors clustered by SITC division. The rate is already elevated in the five years before the crossover. In bins, the rate is about six times as high once the challenger's exports reach a quarter of American exports as below that level. It is eleven times as high once they are twice American exports. Relative position predicts measures at given American imports from the challenger. Imports do not predict them at given relative position.

The 26 starts behind these estimates come from only nine measures. Three of them supply 20 starts: the steel restraints of 1969 and 1984 and the textile agreements of 1971. The gradient does not depend on them. It holds without each of the three and without all three together (30 to 40 per cent per doubling). It also holds in a cluster bootstrap over divisions (Appendix, Tables D.3 and D.4). A further test counts each measure once. In the year a measure began, the covered groups' relative position is clearly above its level in shifted years ($p=0.005$). The jump at parity, by contrast, depends on the steel restraints of 1984 and vanishes without them. Two limits qualify this reading. First, at given challenger exports, the export ratio varies only through American exports. Measures went to groups where the challenger was large and American exporters were weak. The American trade balance of the group against the rest of the world, which does not depend on the challenger, predicts them as well as the export ratio; entered together, neither is significant. The data thus cannot separate displacement from American comparative disadvantage. Second, within groups, with common trends, the panel does not identify the timing (Appendix, Table D.5). The timing rests on the case histories and on the measure-level test above. In plain terms, broad measures went where a large challenger met a weak American industry, and they came as the challenger's position there rose. For seven further economies the evidence is weaker. The raw ratio of 7.0 falls to 1.0 at given export size. The gradient of 20 per cent per doubling rests on the steel restraints of 1984 with Brazil and Mexico. It also loses significance with clustering by division.

Yet most displaced groups never met such a measure. Of the groups in which Japanese exports overtook American exports between 1963 and 1989, 22 per cent (9 of 41) drew a broad measure within ten years. For Korea the share is 20 per cent of the 25 groups that crossed between 1968 and 1992. None of the 13 Korean groups that crossed between 1997 and 2009 drew such a measure. Section~\ref{sec:selection} asks which groups did.

\emph{The third rung: tariffs on everything follow the loss of the economy (H3).} How much of the economy a challenger displaced differs by an order of magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:breadth}). The groups Japan had overtaken accounted for 24 to 36 per cent of American manufactured exports in 1977-1987. The groups China had overtaken accounted for 45 per cent in 2018. For Korea and Taiwan the share stayed below 6 per cent before 1990 \citep{comtrade}. The response grew with this share. Korea and Taiwan met measures in single sectors. Japan met broad measures in six sectors between 1957 and 1987: textiles, steel, television sets, cars, machine tools and semiconductors. China met the Section 301 tariffs of 2018. They covered all 813 HS groups in which China exported at least 50 million dollars: the 609 it had displaced and the 204 it had not. Other broad measures against China were too rare to estimate a panel effect (five events; rate ratio 1.13, interval 0.11 to 11.24). China overtook 242 groups after 2008. If the tariffs of 2018 are counted, 74 per cent of them drew a broad measure within ten years. If they are not, 1 per cent did. Why Japan's broad displacement was answered sector by sector and China's at once is a question of form (Section~\ref{sec:form}).

\subsection{When incumbents climb the ladder}\label{sec:sequence}

The ladder also has a clock. Almost every response came after displacement. Table~\ref{tab:phases} summarises the 41 responses and Fig.~\ref{fig:lags} shows each one by its lag. Five responses came before the milestone of their sector. Four of them score 3 or less: a dumping complaint against Japanese television sets (1968), the American investigation of Korea's intellectual-property rules (1985), the agreement that settled it (1986) and a speech by the American National Security Adviser (2022). Only one broad measure came before displacement: the export controls of October 2022 (Section~\ref{sec:exceptions}).

\emph{Germany and Britain.} Britain's response to Germany came late and was mostly general. German firms led the world in synthetic dyes from about 1870 \citep{murmann2001}, and by 1899 German chemical exports were 43 per cent larger than British ones \citep{tyszynski1951}. Britain first answered with a Royal Commission on the depression of trade (1885) and the Merchandise Marks Act of 1887, which required foreign goods bearing British names or trade marks to state their country of origin. The polemic \emph{Made in Germany} followed in 1896 \citep{williams1896}. The first binding measure for a single sector was the Brussels Sugar Convention of 1902 (IR 4). It abolished export subsidies on sugar under threat of countervailing duties, 16 years after imports from beet-growing countries overtook cane in the British market. In chemicals, the Patents and Designs Act of 1907 required patents to be worked in Britain, 37 years after the dye milestone. In steel and machinery, Germany displaced Britain in export markets only between 1899 and 1913. In those years German exports of iron and steel rose from 35 to 91 per cent of British exports, of industrial equipment from 50 to 133 per cent and of electrical goods from 58 to 211 per cent \citep{tyszynski1951}. Joseph Chamberlain launched his campaign for tariff reform in 1903, but free trade blocked a general tariff until 1932. No British response came before a German milestone. Binding measures followed 17 to 37 years after the milestone.

\emph{Japan and the United States.} The American response to Japan followed each sector within a few years. Japan was again the world's largest exporter of cotton textiles by 1951. Japanese controls on cotton exports to the United States followed late in 1955, and a five-year restraint on all cotton textiles in January 1957. Japan became the largest steel exporter in 1969 \citep{ohashi2005}. A restraint on Japanese and European steel took effect the same year, and a minimum import price for steel, the trigger-price mechanism, in 1978. By 1970 Japanese firms supplied about 30 per cent of the American market for medium-sized colour televisions. Antidumping duties followed in 1971, and a cap of 1.75 million units a year from 1977. Japan became the world's largest car producer in 1980, and a restraint of 1.68 million cars a year began in May 1981 \citep{berry1999}. Japanese 64K memory chips (dynamic random-access memory, DRAM) overtook American producers in 1981. The semiconductor agreement followed in 1986, and 100 per cent tariffs on 300 million dollars of Japanese electronics in 1987. All eleven binding measures against Japan come after the milestone of their sector, with lags of zero to nine years. Ten are American. The other is the British and French refusal in 1955 to extend GATT treatment to Japan under Article XXXV. The closest case is the steel restraint of 1969. Its lag is zero, and its milestone rests on a single source.

\emph{Korea.} The two responses to Korea before a milestone concern intellectual property in general, not memory chips: the Section 301 investigation of 1985 (IR 2) and the agreement of 1986 (IR 3). Samsung became the largest DRAM maker in 1992. Binding measures on Korean DRAM chips came one and eleven years later: an antidumping order in 1993 and a countervailing duty order in 2003. In shipbuilding, Korea overtook Japan in new orders in 1999. Japan's complaints to the WTO followed only in 2018 and 2020.

\emph{China.} China overtook the United States in manufacturing value added in 2010. Binding responses followed: duties on solar products in 2012 and 2013, Section 301 tariffs from July 2018, the listing of Huawei in May 2019 and duties on electric vehicles in the United States and the European Union in 2024. Binding measures followed one to eight years after the milestone.

\emph{Tests.} With measure dates, chance is unlikely to produce this timing. Within ten years on either side of the milestone, 5 responses fall before and 26 after (one-sided binomial test, $p=0.0001$). For binding responses the counts are 2 and 18 ($p=0.0002$). When every milestone is shifted at random, on average 60 per cent of responses fall after the shifted milestone. The observed share of 84 per cent occurs in 2.5 per cent of the draws, and the binding share of 90 per cent in 1.1 per cent. Downturns raise temporary trade barriers \citep{bown2013jie}, but the business cycle does not explain the timing either. Only 38 per cent of the responses fall in years in which the incumbent's output was below its trend, against 45 per cent of all years in the case windows. 

\emph{Initiation dates.} A measure can follow the milestone even when the decision behind it came earlier. We therefore also date each binding response by the start of its formal process. This is the petition filed, the investigation opened, the bill introduced or the negotiations opened (Appendix, Table C.3). The median delay from initiation to measure is one year. Three binding processes began one or two years before the milestone, although their measures came after it. They are the steel talks of June 1968, the antidumping complaint on television receivers of March 1968 and the review of the Section 301 tariffs opened in May 2022. That review raised the tariff on Chinese electric vehicles. With initiation dates, 20 of 25 binding responses still begin from the milestone. The window test holds (5 against 15, $p=0.021$). The placebo, however, no longer separates the observed share from chance ($p=0.15$). The timing evidence is therefore weaker than the measure dates suggest. Formal processes often begin as the challenger approaches the lead. This matches the gradient in the panel (Section~\ref{sec:reach}).

\emph{Robustness.} The result holds with the export crossover instead of the milestone. Then 28 responses follow the crossover. Three cannot be placed, because only an upper bound for the crossover exists. Nine cannot be classified, because trade statistics do not measure the contested product. DRAM chips are not separable from logic chips, and China's exports of integrated circuits are largely assembled foreign chips. One response comes before the crossover. It is the European Union's countervailing duties on Chinese electric cars of October 2024 (IR 3). The duties came while Chinese exports of electric cars were still below German ones (32.0 against 41.1 billion dollars). Each rule thus produces one exception, in different sectors. Neither exception is a broad measure before displacement in the sector concerned. Of the 23 responses with an exactly dated crossover, none comes before it, with measure dates or with initiation dates. Two further checks address the dating of milestones and the dependence between responses. The advanced-chips milestone marks a capability rather than a market lead. Without it, 23 of 24 binding responses come from the milestone (placebo $p=0.003$). We also count each milestone once. The first binding response then comes from the milestone in 13 of 15 cases (one-sided sign test, $p=0.004$). With initiation dates it does so in only 10 of 15 ($p=0.15$) (Appendix~C).

\subsection{Which displaced sectors are hit}\label{sec:selection}

Political voice appears to have shaped which displaced sectors were hit (H2). Size alone did not. Among the displaced groups of Japan and Korea, the share drawing a broad measure within ten years is 27, 14 and 23 per cent by third of American imports from the challenger. By third of American employment it is 19, 14 and 29 per cent (logit coefficient 0.24, $p=0.43$, 63 groups). With so few broad measures these tests have little power, but they show no strong selection on imports or jobs. Parliamentary records point to voice that existed before displacement (Table~\ref{tab:voice} and Fig.~\ref{fig:attention}).

\emph{Britain and Germany.} The British Parliament took up German competition sector by sector. Mentions of Germany in British economic debates rose from 14 per 100,000 words in 1850-1869 to 20 in 1870-1892 and about 44 after 1893. A search for breaks in this series, without fixing the dates in advance, finds the best single break in 1871. With two breaks it finds 1871 and 1894, one year after the dye and the steel milestones. The rise is uneven across sectors (Table~\ref{tab:voice}, panel A). Germany appears in 2 of 75 sugar debates before 1880, in 5 of 23 in 1880-1885, before imports from beet countries overtook cane, and in about one in six from 1886. This debate ended in the Brussels Convention. In iron and steel the share of debates linked to Germany rises from 15 to 24 per cent, with overlapping intervals. Dyes and chemicals, where German leadership came first, were debated only 18 times in 65 years. Parliament appears to have taken up German competition where it hurt sectors with many producers, workers or colonial interests.

Growth alone did not draw attention. American income per head overtook British income around 1900 \citep{bolt2024}. Yet mentions of the United States stayed between 41 and 56 per 100,000 words of economic debate in all four periods, and the share of debates linking it to competition stayed between 0.7 and 2.1 per cent. The phrase ``American invasion'' does not occur once in the 14,383 economic debates. Trade data explain why. In a fixed panel of 17 third markets, American exports reached 75 per cent of British exports by 1913, but only 51 per cent outside Canada, against 85 per cent for Germany \citep{fouquin2016}. In manufactures, the American share of world exports rose only from 11.2 to 12.6 per cent between 1899 and 1913, the German share from 22.2 to 26.4 per cent \citep{tyszynski1951}. The United States outgrew Britain at home; Germany took its export markets (Appendix~F).

\emph{Congress, Japan and Korea.} Congress debated the sectors that would later be protected long before they lost. On the sampled sitting days of 1955-1994, attention to Japan as a competitor stayed low through the 1960s and 1970s, when Japanese textiles, steel and television sets overtook American exports. It rose in the 1980s; the best single break falls in 1980, the year of the car milestone. Attention to Korea stayed at about a tenth of that level. Two measures must be kept apart here: how much Congress talks about a sector, and how much of that talk names the challenger. The second does not separate displaced sectors that met broad measures from those that did not ($p$ between 0.11 and 0.59). The first does, and already before displacement. In the five years before the crossover, Congress mentioned the six Japanese sectors that later drew a broad measure 111 times per million words: textiles, steel, television sets, cars, semiconductors and machine tools. It mentioned the eight sectors without one 17 times, among them ships, footwear, toys and tyres ($p=0.006$). For Korea the figures are 62 and 15 ($p=0.020$).

Debate about a sector goes with the size of the American industry. We therefore regress the indicator for a broad measure on log debate volume, log American employment and log American imports from the challenger across all 28 sectors. Debate volume keeps its effect (permutation $p=0.011$). Employment loses its effect once volume is included ($p=0.33$). Imports keep a borderline one ($p=0.084$). The result is the same at the level of the 63 displaced groups. Debate volume may also reflect protection that predates displacement. Adding an indicator for the Korean sectors already protected against Japan (textiles, television sets and steel) lowers its coefficient from 0.18 to 0.14 (permutation $p=0.059$). Without textiles and steel it is 0.14 ($p=0.054$). Voice before displacement thus partly reflects earlier protection, and the evidence for it weakens to the margin of conventional significance. Displacement appears to have set the timing, and voice that existed before it, partly built by earlier protection, to have chosen the sectors.

\emph{Congress and China.} China does not fit this pattern. Its trade status was itself the subject of legislation in the 1990s. Attention in economic debates peaked with permanent normal trade relations in 2000, at 399 mentions per 100,000 words, before most Chinese crossovers. A search for breaks finds no rise after them. Across 17 sectors, those that later drew a sector-specific measure had more debate, and more debate linked to China, than displaced sectors without one, but none of the differences is significant (Table~\ref{tab:voice}, panel B). Congressional voice does not predict how heavily the first Section 301 lists of 2018 hit a sector \citep[Spearman $\rho=0.06$;][]{ustr301}. It is only weakly linked to the sector increases of 2024 ($p=0.056$). With employment and imports held constant, the sectors that drew measures had lower American imports from China ($p=0.025$). The measures went to steel, aluminium, solar cells, washing machines, telecom equipment, semiconductors and cars. Consumer-goods sectors with larger average imports from China, such as computers, furniture, toys and footwear, were spared. Lists 1 and 2 of the Section 301 tariffs, the first to take effect at 25 per cent, concentrated on machinery, electronics and vehicles. Against a challenger that displaced the whole economy, the incumbent appears to have chosen sectors by their strategic importance.

\subsection{Alignment and the form of the response}\label{sec:form}

Alignment shapes the form of each rung (H4). Before 1995 the United States mostly negotiated, with allies and others. It aimed 23 broad, trade-motivated measures at treaty allies (Japan, Korea, Taiwan until 1980, Brazil, Mexico and Thailand); 21 of them were negotiated restraints. The two unilateral ones were the trigger price for steel (1978) and the motorcycle tariff (1983). With economies outside its alliances it also mostly negotiated: two of the three broad measures aimed at Taiwan after 1980 and at Malaysia were negotiated (Appendix, Table I.1). The pattern is consistent with H4, but the variation is too small to test it. The Section 301 tariffs on Japanese electronics (1987) and on Brazilian goods (1988) were unilateral too, but covered short lists of products (IR 3). After 1995 no such measure was aimed at an ally. All five hit economies without a defence pact with the United States: four unilateral measures against China, from the Section 301 tariffs of 2018 to the tariff on electric vehicles of 2024, and the textile agreement with Vietnam of 2003. Britain's binding responses to Japan in the 1930s, in a pair without an alliance, were three unilateral measures and one agreement. This would explain why Japan's broad displacement was answered with negotiated restraints sector by sector and China's with one unilateral tariff on every group. Two changes coincide in 1995, however, and the data cannot separate them. The WTO Agreement on Safeguards outlawed negotiated restraints, and the one challenger that displaced the United States across the economy after 1995 was also its main strategic rival (Appendix~I).

Alignment does not predict whether a displacing economy meets resistance. Mexico, an ally, and Vietnam, without an alliance, both displaced the United States in many groups after 2008. Neither drew a broad measure aimed at it before 2025. Their distance from American positions in the General Assembly was 2.5 and 3.4 points.

Loans from the IMF offer a third channel of constraint, but they do not follow displacement. We use the coded conditions of 1,173 programme years in 116 countries between 1989 and 2014 \citep{stubbs2020}. Closer voting with the United States goes with a higher chance of a programme, as earlier studies found \citep{thacker1999,barro2005}. It also goes with more binding conditions. Neither link survives country fixed effects. The challengers in our data entered programmes after debt or financial crises: Mexico in 1989-1992, 1995-1996 and 1999-2000, Korea in 1998-2000, Brazil in 1999 and 2001-2004 and Vietnam in 1994-1996 and 2001-2002. At those times, the groups they had overtaken accounted for at most 2.3 per cent of American manufactured exports. Their programmes carried twice as many conditions on trade and the external sector as other programmes with the same alignment, income, growth and openness (rate ratio 2.16), but no more conditions overall. Conditions narrowed the policy space of borrowers \citep{kentikelenis2016}; they were not aimed at the sectors in which the borrowers displaced the incumbent (Appendix~J).

\section{Extensions}\label{sec:extensions}

\subsection{Further cases}\label{sec:further}

The core episodes all ended in broad resistance. We therefore test the argument on eight further pairs, chosen before looking at the responses: Japan against Britain in the 1930s; Taiwan, Hong Kong, Brazil and Mexico, which displaced American exports in a few sectors; and Thailand, Malaysia and Vietnam, usually described as having absorbed technology without displacing anyone. For the American pairs we add 19 entries to the register \citep{statutes,moore1996,dinopoulos1991}. Britain defended the markets it lost, not the one it kept. Japanese cotton piece goods overtook British goods in the Indian market in 1932, and all four binding measures, Indian duties and colonial quotas, came from that year onwards. The British home market, where Japan had not overtaken Britain, received no measure aimed at Japan. Among the American pairs, sectoral displacement drew sectoral measures, as for Taiwanese footwear in 1977 and Brazilian steel in 1984. Economies that displaced little met little outside the textile regime. Vietnam and Mexico had overtaken American exports in groups worth 6 and 7 per cent of American manufactured exports by 2023, but neither drew a broad measure aimed at it before 2025. The panel evidence for these pairs is weak: the raw rate ratio of 7.0 falls to 1.0 at given export size (Section~\ref{sec:reach}). The cases add two points. Incumbents extend measures aimed at the main challenger to its followers before these displace them, as in colour televisions, machine tools and steel. And they also use broad tariffs for purposes that have nothing to do with displacement, as in 2025. Appendix~H gives the details.

\subsection{Two exceptions}\label{sec:exceptions}

Two sets of broad measures came before displacement. Each is confined to one sector.

\emph{The textile regime.} In textiles, incumbents restrained newcomers before these displaced anyone. The register shows this for every such supplier in our data. Korea was restrained under the Long-Term Arrangement from 1963, by a bilateral agreement from 1965 and by the agreements of 1971, nine and two years before its exports in the groups concerned matched American ones. The first comprehensive American cotton agreements with Hong Kong and Taiwan, both of 1963, came two and eight years before their textile and clothing exports overtook American exports. The agreements with Mexico (1967), Brazil and Malaysia (1970) and Thailand (1972) restrained suppliers that never overtook American exports in these groups. Vietnam's clothing met a bilateral quota in 2003, three years before its clothing exports as a whole overtook American ones. Two clothing groups (SITC 841 and 842), however, had done so in 2002. Textiles were an early export sector of the low-wage suppliers in the register. The incumbent's textile industry was mature and politically organised. The regime turned the sectoral measure into a standing rule for each new supplier.

\emph{Export controls on advanced chips.} The American controls on advanced computing and chip-making equipment of 7 October 2022 (IR 5) came a year before the Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation (SMIC) was confirmed to produce chips at 7\,nm in 2023. They aimed to deny China a capability it did not yet have. They are the only broad measure among the 41 coded responses that comes before the milestone of its sector. They came in a campaign of tariffs and listings that followed China's milestones in manufacturing, solar cells and telecom equipment. And they fall in the one pair in which the challenger was a military peer. When the controls were imposed, China's distance from American positions in the General Assembly was 3.5 points, against 1.1 to 1.5 for Japan in the years of its milestones. China's national capabilities were 1.9 times those of the United States, on a measure that weights population and heavy industry. Germany's capabilities stood at 0.44 to 0.79 of Britain's in the years of its milestones. Japan's stood at 0.26 to 0.40 of America's, and Korea's at 0.15 to 0.16. In this record, technology denial before displacement was used only against a rival.

\section{Threats to validity}\label{sec:validity}

\emph{Sample.} Four core cases and 41 responses are a small sample. The cases are also linked. The core includes only successful catch-up. The further cases add negative controls. But Thailand and Malaysia displaced too little to test much. And the displacement by Vietnam and Mexico falls in a period without sectoral restraints.

\emph{Coding.} The reach scores come from one human coder. We checked them against a blind coding by four language models ($\alpha=0.87$). A model coding is not a substitute for an independent human coder. Agreement for the register (0.76) also falls short of the conventional threshold of 0.80. With the models' scores, 13 instead of 15 of the 41 responses are broad. One of them still comes before its milestone. The revision of eight scores after the second coding changes no conclusion. Several milestones rest on one or two secondary sources. Korean shipbuilding is an example: it is dated by orders to 1999, and Japan was ahead again in 2001. Some milestones depend on the measure. Chinese telecom equipment, for instance, is a near tie in total revenue in 2012 that turns on exchange rates. The target countries of several Section 201 actions come from secondary literature.

\emph{Measurement.} The crossover rule measures displacement in world exports. It therefore understates displacement in the American home market. Trade statistics also do not always capture the contested product. Antidumping cases are matched to groups through HS codes of 1988-1992. Employment is allocated to groups by tariff lines. Taiwan's exports are measured from partner imports. For Japan against Britain, the bilateral trade data cover total exports, not cotton goods. The British and Japanese series for piece goods are not fully comparable either.

\emph{Attention.} Economic debates are selected by title. The filter therefore misses economic talk under other headings. In the daily Congressional Record it captures only one in five passages that link China to competition. Before 1995 the American measure rests on 800 sampled sitting days. These carry errors from the text recognition of the scans. They are also too few for sector counts in single years. Debate measures attention, not lobbying or votes.

\emph{Identification.} After 1995, the lack of an alliance and displacement across the economy coincide in one case, China. The data cannot separate rivalry from the reach of displacement or from the WTO's ban on negotiated restraints. Above all, the evidence orders events. It does not identify the effect of displacement on policy. Inference rests on few decisions. The 26 starts in the panel for Japan and Korea come from nine measures. The further cases add little independent panel evidence. Three results narrow the claim further. In the panel, resistance rises with the challenger's relative exports rather than jumping at parity, and the data cannot separate the challenger's rise from the weakness of the American industry. Much of the raw contrast before and after the crossover reflects scale. And with the dates on which formal processes began, the placebo test loses its significance. The paperwork criterion of Section~\ref{sec:framework} is thus only partly met. Measures follow the milestone, but the processes behind some of them begin in the year or two before it. The amnesia itself is not measured. The paper shows when resistance comes, not what incumbents remember. Appendix~K documents each limit.

\section{Implications for late industrialisers}\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{The window for absorbing technology closes with success.} For a time, a latecomer can absorb foreign technology without systematic resistance. This phase appears to end when the latecomer begins to take the leader's markets in sectors that matter politically to the leader. Product duties come earlier, but they follow imports and stay narrow. A latecomer can therefore plan for two different costs: routine duties while its exports grow, and broader limits once it leads.

\emph{The warning comes early.} In the panel for Japan and Korea, broad measures became markedly more likely once a group's exports reached about a quarter of American exports (Fig.~\ref{fig:rates}). A latecomer can track this ratio sector by sector. The effect may run partly through the weakness of the leader's own exporters, not only through the latecomer's rise.

\emph{Breadth predicts reach.} On this record, how far success reaches indicates how far the response will reach. Before 1990, Korea and Taiwan displaced American exports in groups worth less than 6 per cent of American manufactured exports. They met measures in single sectors. Japan reached 24 to 36 per cent and met broad measures in six sectors. China reached 45 per cent and met a tariff on every group. Table~\ref{tab:breadth} sets out the record for all ten challengers. The table is descriptive. Its shares come from different classifications and periods. After 1995, too few measures followed for a test. Four economies are now at or above the level at which Korea and Taiwan met sectoral measures before 1990. Taiwan stood at 9 per cent in 2023, Mexico at 7 per cent, Vietnam at 6 per cent and Malaysia at 4.5 per cent. None drew a sectoral measure aimed at its displacement after 1995, when negotiated restraints became illegal. A latecomer can therefore track the share of the leader's exports it has displaced, as an early warning. But the threshold at which this share now triggers a response is not known.

\emph{Alignment shapes the form, and the rules have changed.} Before 1995 allies negotiated restraints with the incumbent. Since 1995 the WTO Agreement on Safeguards has outlawed such restraints. The measures against China after 2018 were unilateral, and the ``reciprocal'' tariffs of 2025 covered all trading partners. For latecomers today, the softer negotiated form of the past may no longer be on offer.

\emph{Convergence may stall after the response.} Total factor productivity relative to the United States peaked in Japan at 0.80 in 1991. In Korea it peaked at 0.71 in 2002. Both peaks came after the escalation documented here (Fig.~\ref{fig:tfp}). Japan fell back to 0.64 by 2023, and Korea has stayed between 0.62 and 0.70 since 2010 \citep[Appendix~L]{feenstra2015,pwt110}. We do not claim that resistance caused the stall. Domestic explanations remain strong \citep{acemoglu2006,eichengreen2012,mcmillan2014,lee2021}. In a sample of this size, the pattern cannot be told apart from regression to the mean \citep{pritchett2014}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Over four incumbents and 175 years, leading economies resisted latecomers with instruments whose reach matched the breadth of their losses. Narrow duties on single products run throughout catch-up and follow imports. Limits on whole sectors come where a large challenger meets a weak incumbent industry. They come only in some displaced sectors: those with a voice in the incumbent's legislature, or, against a strategic rival, those its government regards as strategic. Tariffs on almost everything come when displacement covers much of the economy. Alliances decide whether the incumbent negotiates or acts alone. The delay between displacement and binding response has shortened, from 17 to 37 years in free-trade Britain to zero to nine years against Japan and one to eight against China. Each episode appears to have left the next with new tools: antidumping law, Section 301, the Entity List and export controls on equipment. The textile regime and the controls of October 2022 show that incumbents can act before displacement when an industry is organised for it or a technology is held to be strategic.

The trade policy amnesia of the introduction fits this pattern. Leaders appear to have tolerated imitation and protection while the latecomer was absorbing technology, and to have turned to broader instruments as the latecomer took their markets. Japan, once itself restrained, complained about Korean shipyard subsidies almost two decades after Korea overtook it in orders. The United States, which once denied copyright to foreign authors, opened its case on Korean intellectual property in 1985, seven years before Korea led in memory chips. Such narrow, rule-based pressure runs throughout catch-up and stays below the reach of the measures that follow displacement. The memory of one's own catch-up seems to fade when one's markets are at stake. For latecomers, anticipating each rung, and the form their alliances give it, would therefore belong to development strategy.
