\title{Displacement Before Escalation: Late Industrialisation and the Timing of Incumbent Constraint, 1850--2024}

\begin{abstract}
Late industrialisers meet tariffs, quotas, export restraints and technology controls from the economies they catch up with. This paper asks when this constraint arrives and how far it reaches. It dates 18 sectoral displacement milestones in four episodes (Germany against Britain, Japan and Korea against the United States, China against the United States and the European Union) and codes 41 incumbent responses by instrument reach. The reach of the response follows the reach of displacement. Antidumping duties track imports into the incumbent's home market and run throughout catch-up. Quantity limits on whole sectors follow displacement in the markets the incumbent contests: in a panel of 1,316 manufacturing groups, they began 5.7 times as often against Japan and Korea after a group's exports overtook American exports as before. The economy-wide tariffs of 2018 followed displacement across the Chinese economy and covered every group. Displacement is close to necessary for such constraint but not sufficient: about one displaced group in five drew a sectoral instrument within ten years. Among displaced Japanese and Korean sectors, those that did were the ones Congress had debated four to six times as often before displacement; against China, the instruments went to strategic sectors. Alignment shaped the form: allies negotiated restraints, the rival met unilateral tariffs, listings and controls. Two bounded exceptions came before displacement: the multilateral textile regime, which restrained every low-wage supplier, and the export controls on advanced chips of October 2022. Eight further dyads show the same gradient. The evidence establishes a sequence, not a causal effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Every large economy that has caught up with the technological frontier has met resistance from the economy it was catching up with. Britain answered German competition with origin marking, an international sugar convention and, eventually, a general tariff. The United States answered Japan with export restraints on textiles, steel, televisions and cars, a managed-trade agreement in semiconductors and retaliatory tariffs. Korea met antidumping duties and pressure on its intellectual-property regime. China now faces tariffs on most of its exports to the United States, the listing of its national champions and controls on the equipment needed to make advanced chips.

Development economics explains how late industrialisers acquire technology and why their growth slows near the frontier. It says little about when the incumbent's reaction begins and what sets its scope. The developmental-state literature treats the leader as a fixed benchmark \citep{gerschenkron1962,johnson1982,amsden1989,wade1990,evans1995}; \citet{chang2002} identifies the pattern of incumbents restricting instruments they once used themselves, but gives it no clock. The political economy of protection explains who obtains protection and how much \citep{grossman1994}, and \citet{bown2013aer} show that import surges predict temporary trade barriers at the level of the product. Neither asks at which stage of catch-up broader constraint arrives. The question matters for policy. If incumbents constrain challengers already while they absorb foreign technology, external constraint is a condition of development from the start. If incumbents constrain only once the challenger takes their markets, constraint is a consequence of success, and late industrialisers can anticipate when it will come and in what form.

This paper answers the question with a dated, sector-level record of four episodes that span 175 years: Germany against Britain (1850--1914), Japan against the United States (1950--1990), Korea against the United States and Japan (1960--2020) and China against the United States and the European Union (1978--2024). It dates 18 sectoral displacement milestones, each the first year in which the challenger leads in the market where the incumbent sells, and codes 41 documented incumbent responses on a five-point scale of instrument reach. It then checks the record against complete registers: all 759 American and European antidumping and countervailing duty cases against the three Asian challengers, all 23 broad American instruments against them, and a panel of every manufacturing group in which a challenger exported at least 50 million dollars, displaced or not. Debates in the British Parliament (1850--1914) and the American Congress (1955--2025) provide a measure of attention that does not depend on the coding.

The central finding is that the reach of the response follows the reach of displacement. Narrow product remedies follow imports into the incumbent's home market and start long before any sectoral milestone: 314 of 414 cases against China were opened before the milestone of their sector, for most of them the manufacturing milestone of 2010, and among displaced groups the share drawing a case within five years rises from 1 to 30 per cent between the lowest and the highest third of American imports from the challenger. Binding limits on whole sectors follow displacement in the markets the incumbent contests. Thirty-six of the 41 coded responses come after the milestone of their sector, and in the panel of groups, targeted broad instruments against Japan and Korea began 5.7 times as often after a group's exports overtook American exports as before. The economy-wide Section 301 tariffs of 2018 came when the groups China had overtaken accounted for 45 per cent of American manufactured exports; they covered all 813 groups, displaced or not.

Two further results qualify the gradient. First, displacement is close to necessary for broad constraint, but far from sufficient. Only about one displaced group in five drew a broad instrument within ten years. Which ones did is predicted by political attention that existed before displacement. Congress debated the displaced Japanese sectors that later drew a broad instrument six times as often in the five years before displacement as the displaced sectors that did not ($p=0.006$); for Korea the ratio is four ($p=0.020$). How often Congress named the challenger does not separate the two groups, and controls for American employment and imports do not remove the result. In the British record, Germany became a parliamentary issue in sugar and steel, sectors with many producers and colonial interests, while German leadership in dyes went almost undebated. Against China, whose displacement covered the economy, the sector-specific instruments went to steel, aluminium, solar cells, telecom equipment, semiconductors and cars, not to the consumer goods with the largest imports or the most debate. Second, alignment shapes the form of constraint. Before 1995 the United States negotiated 21 of its 25 targeted broad instruments against treaty allies as restraints; after 1995 every targeted, trade-motivated broad instrument hit an economy without a defence pact with it. Because the WTO outlawed negotiated restraints in the same year, the data cannot separate rivalry from the change in rules.

Two bounded exceptions remain, and both are informative. The multilateral textile regime restrained every new low-wage supplier in our data before it displaced American producers, or without its ever doing so, from Korea in 1962 to Vietnam in 2003. The American export controls on advanced computing and semiconductor equipment of October 2022 preceded any Chinese lead in the sector; they were aimed at the only challenger in our data that was also a military peer. Eight further dyads, chosen before looking at the responses, confirm the gradient out of sample: Britain answered Japanese displacement in Asian cotton markets after 1932 with duties and quotas in India and its colonies, not at home; Taiwan and Brazil met restraints in the few sectors they displaced; Thailand and Malaysia, which displaced almost nothing, met no targeted broad instrument outside the textile regime.

The paper contributes in three ways. It adds a clock to the historical account of incumbent constraint \citep{chang2002} and indexes it to a measurable event rather than to threat perception \citep{mastanduno1992}. It extends the product-level evidence on temporary trade barriers \citep{bown2013aer} to the sector and the economy and shows that each level of instrument tracks a different market. And it identifies when the political demand for protection modelled by \citet{grossman1994} is activated: at displacement, in sectors with a standing voice. The claim is about sequence. The paper does not estimate the effect of displacement on policy, and it does not claim that incumbent constraint caused the later slowdown of convergence in Japan and Korea.

\section{Literature}\label{sec:lit}

Five literatures each describe one side of the relation between displacement and constraint and hold the other side fixed.

\emph{The developmental state.} The canon explains how late industrialisers substitute institutions for missing markets: universal banks in Germany \citep{gerschenkron1962}, administrative guidance and controlled technology imports in Japan \citep{johnson1982}, performance-conditioned subsidies in Korea \citep{amsden1989}, governed markets in Taiwan \citep{wade1990} and embedded autonomy more generally \citep{evans1995}. In these accounts the incumbent is a benchmark, not an actor. Johnson's account ends in 1975, before the main American response to Japan; Amsden and Wade treat American pressure as part of the environment their planners navigated. \citet{chang2002} goes furthest: today's rich countries used protection and imitation on their way up and later asked others to give them up. His account does not say at which point of catch-up incumbents begin to restrict, or whether that point recurs.

\emph{Convergence.} \citet{eichengreen2012} show that most of the slowdown of fast-growing economies is a slowdown of productivity growth, and \citet{acemoglu2006} model the switch from investment-based to innovation-based growth near the frontier. The causes they consider are domestic. Whether external constraint coincides with the slowdown, or merely accompanies it, is not asked.

\emph{The political economy of protection.} \citet{grossman1994} explain the cross-section of protection by the organisation and stakes of domestic industries. The closest test of a sequence is \citet{bown2013aer}, who show that recent bilateral import growth raises the probability of a temporary trade barrier; \citet{bown2013jie} relate new barriers to the business cycle. Their unit is the product and the year, their window two decades and their instruments those that WTO law permits. This paper moves the test to the sector, the dyad and the economy, observes the intermediate stage between imports and broad constraint, and extends the window to 175 years, long enough to include changes in the instruments themselves.

\emph{Technology denial.} \citet{mastanduno1992} distinguishes a narrow strategic embargo from broader economic warfare in the Cold War, and \citet{lind2025} argue that the controls on China since 2022 differ in kind from those of the Cold War, because they target the upstream tools of a civilian industry. This literature dates controls by strategic threat perception. It does not index them to measurable displacement.

\emph{Power, alignment and rules.} Hegemonic stability theory derives the openness of the trading system from the distribution of power \citep{krasner1976}, and \citet{gowa1993} show that states trade more with allies because the gains from trade strengthen the partner's military potential. A further strand treats rules as constraint: the WTO agreements on investment measures, intellectual property and subsidies narrowed the policy space of late industrialisers \citep{wade2003}, as did the conditions attached to multilateral lending \citep{kentikelenis2016,rodrik2006}. Alignment also prices access to that lending: Security Council members and countries that vote with the United States receive more aid and more IMF programmes \citep{thacker1999,barro2005,kuziemko2006,dreher2009eer,dreher2009jde}. These accounts explain how much constraint a challenger faces and in what form, but not when the incumbent's response begins. Economic historians have documented permissive intellectual-property regimes during catch-up \citep{khan2005,moser2005,murmann2001}, but not when incumbents began to complain.

What is missing across these literatures is a dated test of the order between displacement and constraint that treats the incumbent's response as the variable to be explained. This paper provides one.

\section{Argument and predictions}\label{sec:argument}

The argument is that an incumbent responds to what it loses, in the market where it loses it, with an instrument of matching reach. Three markets matter, and each has its own instrument.

At the level of the product, the incumbent loses sales in its home market to imports. The instruments are antidumping and countervailing duties. They are administrative, petition-driven and cheap to obtain, so a few firms facing an import surge can trigger them. They should therefore start early in catch-up, long before the challenger leads any sector, and track imports from the challenger rather than its position in world markets.

At the level of the sector, the incumbent loses its position in the market where its industry sells: export markets for Britain before 1914, the world market and the home market for the United States. The instruments are quantity or price limits on a whole sector, such as voluntary export restraints, orderly marketing agreements and sectoral tariffs. They require the political mobilisation of an industry and the attention of the legislature or the executive. They should follow the sector's displacement, and among displaced sectors they should go to those with political voice in the incumbent.

At the level of the economy, the incumbent loses ground across a large share of its tradable output. The instruments are tariffs on most imports from the challenger and the denial of technology to its national champions. They should follow displacement across the economy and need not discriminate between sectors.

The form of the instrument is a separate question. An incumbent can negotiate restraints with an ally that values the relationship and accepts managed trade, or act unilaterally against a rival. Form should therefore vary with alignment, while timing varies with displacement.

The argument fails if binding sectoral or economy-wide instruments systematically precede displacement in the affected sector, or if the lags between displacement and response are fully explained by administrative processing, such as the months between an antidumping petition and a duty order. It also fails at the product level if remedies track the challenger's world-market position rather than imports into the incumbent's market.

\section{Data and design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Cases}

The four core episodes are the largest cases of late industrialisation measured against a frontier incumbent. In 1913 Germany's manufactured exports reached 89 per cent of Britain's \citep{tyszynski1951}; Japan's total factor productivity reached 0.80 of the American level in 1991 \citep{pwt110}; China's income per head was 33 per cent of the American level in 2022 \citep{bolt2024}. The cases differ in political system, period, trade regime and alliance structure, which is the point: a regularity that holds across them is unlikely to be an artefact of one setting. They are not independent. Japan learned from Germany, Korea from Japan and China from all three. Learning by challengers does not explain the behaviour of incumbents, however, which is what the paper studies. Section~\ref{sec:further} adds eight dyads that were chosen to test the argument out of sample.

\subsection{Two measures of displacement}

The \emph{displacement milestone} is the first year in which the challenger leads in the market where the incumbent sells: the export market for Britain, the world market or the American home market for the United States, the world market for Japan and the European Union. The 18 milestones in Table~\ref{tab:milestones} are dated from the historical and industry literature (Online Appendix~B). Responses aimed at a sector are dated against that sector's milestone; responses aimed at the challenger's economy as a whole are dated against the case's first milestone.

The \emph{export crossover} is the first year in which the challenger's world exports in a manufacturing group reach those of the incumbent. It is computed from UN Comtrade \citep{comtrade} for three-digit SITC groups (Revision 1 for 1962--1999, Revision 3 for 1992--2009) and four-digit HS headings (2008--2023), with a threshold of 50 million US dollars and the requirement that the lead be sustained in three of the following four years. For Germany before 1914, the benchmark years of \citet{tyszynski1951} take its place. The crossover is mechanical and available for every group; the milestone is closer to the market that matters. The milestone serves the case evidence, the crossover the registers and the panel.

\subsection{Responses and their reach}

The core dataset lists 41 incumbent responses documented in the literature on the four episodes: speeches and publications, official investigations, binding measures and technology denial (Online Appendix, Table C1). Each response is scored on an instrument-reach scale (IR) from 1 to 5 that measures how far and how bindingly the instrument constrains the challenger: 1 for rhetoric without an official procedure, 2 for an investigation or complaint, 3 for a narrow binding measure such as origin marking or a duty on a single product, 4 for a binding quantity or price limit on a whole sector or a trade statute that creates new sanction powers, and 5 for tariffs on a large share of imports or the denial of technology to a sector or a national champion. In borderline cases the lower score applies. Measures that only strengthen the incumbent's own economy, such as the CHIPS and Science Act, are excluded. We call instruments scoring 4 or 5 \emph{broad}. A second coder is scoring all responses independently; agreement will be reported as Krippendorff's ordinal $\alpha$ \citep{krippendorff2013}. A response falls in the \emph{absorption phase} if it precedes the milestone of its sector; its \emph{lag} is the response year minus the milestone year.

\subsection{Registers, population and control group}

Because the 41 responses come from the literature on major disputes, three checks use complete sources instead. The Temporary Trade Barriers Database \citep{ttbd} records all 759 antidumping and countervailing duty cases of the United States and the European Union against Japan, Korea and China from 1979 to 2015. A register of broad instruments lists all 23 American instruments with IR 4 or 5 against these three challengers from 1957 to 2024: every Section 201 safeguard that ended in quantity limits or orderly marketing agreements (the USITC's own list for 1975--2016), the bilateral restraint agreements in textiles, steel, cars, machine tools and semiconductors, the Section 232 and 301 tariffs of 2018, the listing of Huawei, the export controls of 2022 and the tariff on Chinese electric vehicles of 2024, 31 instrument--target pairs in all. Finally, a panel follows every manufacturing group in which a challenger exported at least 50 million dollars in some year (1,316 group series for Japan, Korea and China), with the state ``displaced'' from the group's export crossover onwards. Poisson models with year effects and standard errors clustered by group compare the rate at which instruments start before and after the crossover (Online Appendix~D).

\subsection{Political attention}

Two text sources measure attention independently of the coding. For Britain we use all debates of the House of Commons and the House of Lords recorded in Historic Hansard from 1850 to 1914 \citep{hansard}, 281,577 in all, of which 15,126 are economic debates by a fixed title filter. For the United States we use the Congressional Record \citep{crec}: the daily edition from 1995 to 2025 in full, and for 1955--1994, when only scanned volumes of the bound edition exist, 20 randomly drawn sitting days per year (800 days). We count mentions of the challenger and flag passages in which it appears within 30 words of a competition term or a sector term. We use the volume of debate on a sector as a measure of its political voice, and the share of that debate that names the challenger as a measure of attention to the challenger (Online Appendices E and I).

\emph{Germany and Britain.} German firms led the world in synthetic dyes from about 1870 \citep{murmann2001}, and by 1899 German chemical exports were 43 per cent larger than British ones \citep{tyszynski1951}. Britain's first responses were general: a Royal Commission on the depression of trade (1885), the Merchandise Marks Act of 1887, which required imports to show their origin, and the polemic \emph{Made in Germany} \citep{williams1896}. The first sector-specific binding instrument was the Brussels Sugar Convention of 1902 (IR 4), which abolished export bounties under threat of countervailing duties, 22 years after continental beet sugar overtook cane in the British market. In chemicals, the Patents and Designs Act of 1907 required patents to be worked in Britain, 37 years after the dye milestone. In steel and machinery, Germany displaced Britain in export markets only between 1899 and 1913: German exports of iron and steel rose from 35 to 91 per cent of British exports, of industrial equipment from 50 to 133 per cent and of electrical goods from 58 to 211 per cent \citep{tyszynski1951}. In these years Joseph Chamberlain launched the Tariff Reform campaign (1903). Free trade blocked a general tariff until the Import Duties Act of 1932. No British response precedes a German milestone; the lags of binding measures before 1914 run from 17 to 37 years.

\emph{Japan and the United States.} Japan was again the world's largest exporter of cotton textiles by 1951; Japanese ceilings on cotton exports to the United States followed in December 1955 and a five-year restraint on all cotton textiles in January 1957. Japan became the largest steel exporter in 1969 \citep{ohashi2005}; a voluntary restraint arrangement on Japanese and European steel took effect the same year and a trigger-price mechanism in 1978. By 1970 Japanese firms supplied about 30 per cent of the American market for medium-sized colour televisions; antidumping duties followed in 1971 and an orderly marketing agreement capping exports at 1.75 million sets a year in 1977. Japan became the world's largest car producer in 1980, and a voluntary restraint of 1.68 million cars a year began in May 1981 \citep{berry1999}. Japanese 64K DRAMs overtook American producers in 1981; the semiconductor agreement followed in 1986 and 100 per cent tariffs on 300 million dollars of Japanese electronics in 1987. All eleven binding instruments against Japan, ten American ones and the British and French invocation of GATT Article XXXV in 1955, come after the milestone of their sector, with lags of zero to nine years. The closest case is the steel arrangement of 1969, with a lag of zero and a milestone that rests on a single source.

\emph{Korea.} Korea's two responses before a milestone, the Section 301 investigation of 1985 (IR 2) and the bilateral agreement of 1986 (IR 3), concern intellectual property in general, not memory chips. Binding measures in DRAM, an antidumping order in 1993 and a countervailing duty order in 2003, follow Samsung's leadership of 1992 by one and eleven years. In shipbuilding, Korea overtook Japan in new orders in 1999, and Japan's complaints to the WTO followed only in 2018 and 2020.

\emph{China.} China overtook the United States in manufacturing value added in 2010. Binding responses followed in solar products in 2012 and 2013, Section 301 tariffs from July 2018, the listing of Huawei in May 2019 and tariffs and countervailing duties on electric vehicles in the United States and the European Union in 2024. The lags of binding measures range from one to eight years.

\emph{Tests.} Within ten years on either side of the milestone, 5 responses fall before and 26 from the milestone onwards (one-sided binomial test, $p=0.0001$); for binding responses the counts are 2 and 18 ($p=0.0002$). Shifting every milestone by a random number of years between $-10$ and $+10$ (10,000 draws) yields a mean post-milestone share of 0.61; the observed share of 0.84 occurs in 3.0 per cent of draws, the binding share of 0.90 in 1.0 per cent. The timing is therefore not an artefact of where the milestones lie in the window. Nor does it reflect the business cycle, which predicts temporary trade barriers \citep{bown2013jie}: 38 per cent of the responses fall in years in which the incumbent's output was below its Hodrick--Prescott trend, against 45 per cent of all years in the case windows.

\emph{Robustness.} Under the export crossover instead of the milestone, 28 responses follow the crossover, 3 cannot be placed because only an upper bound for the crossover exists, and 9 cannot be classified because trade statistics do not measure the contested product: DRAM is not separable from logic chips, and China's exports of integrated circuits consist largely of assembled foreign chips. One response precedes the crossover: the European Union's countervailing duties on Chinese electric cars of October 2024 (IR 4), imposed while Chinese exports of electric cars were still below German ones (32.0 against 41.1 billion dollars). The two rules thus produce one exception each, in different sectors, and neither produces a pattern of broad constraint before displacement (Online Appendix~C).

\subsection{Product remedies follow imports}

Antidumping and countervailing duty cases are routine during catch-up. Against Korea, 67 of 181 American and European cases were opened before the DRAM milestone of 1992, 35 of them ending in definitive duties; against China, 314 of 414 were opened before the milestone of their sector, for most of them the manufacturing milestone of 2010, 229 ending in definitive duties. (The database begins after almost all Japanese milestones.) Each case covers a handful of products and would score 2 or 3 on our scale. The cases follow imports. For the 127 American antidumping cases against China opened between 1996 and 2015 that carry product codes, China's share of American imports of the products concerned rose from a median of 15 per cent four years before the case to 23 per cent in the year before, and it rose in 82 per cent of the cases. This replicates, for a single challenger, the import-surge result of \citet{bown2013aer}.

The cases do not follow displacement in the world market. Matched to groups through their HS codes (1988--2015), new American cases run at 6.5 per 100 group-years after a group's crossover against 3.4 before, but the difference vanishes once the size of the challenger's exports is held constant (rate ratio 1.10, interval 0.75 to 1.60); only for Japan does it survive (2.0, interval 1.1 to 3.7). What predicts a case among displaced groups is the volume of American imports from the challenger: within five years of the crossover, 1 per cent of the groups in the lowest third of import volume drew a case, 11 per cent in the middle third and 30 per cent in the top third. American employment in the industries behind a group \citep{nberces} also predicts cases (1, 16 and 25 per cent by third), but not once imports are held constant.

\subsection{Sectoral instruments follow sectoral displacement}

The register of broad instruments places 22 of 31 instrument--target pairs after the challenger's exports of the products concerned overtook American exports; five cannot be dated and four precede the crossover. Two of the four are artefacts of measurement. The 1986 semiconductor agreement precedes the crossover in the broad SITC group, but not the DRAM milestone of 1981, and the steel safeguard of 2002 applied to all suppliers, at a time when China was a minor one. The other two are substantive and concern one sector: the Long-Term Arrangement on cotton textiles (1962) and the textile agreements of 1971 restrained Korean exports a decade before they matched American exports (Section~\ref{sec:exceptions}).

The panel adds a control group. Targeted broad instruments against Japan and Korea began at 0.24 per 100 group-years before a group's crossover and at 1.15 after it, a rate ratio of 5.7 (95 per cent interval 2.3 to 14.0) with year effects. The ratio for seven further economies is 5.8 (Section~\ref{sec:further}). Starting from displacement instead of from the response gives the other side of the result. Of the groups in which Japanese exports overtook American exports between 1963 and 1989, 22 per cent (9 of 41) drew a targeted broad instrument within ten years; for Korea the share is 20 per cent of 25 groups that crossed between 1968 and 1992, and none of 13 that crossed between 1997 and 2009. Displacement raises the rate of broad constraint several times over, but most displaced groups never meet it. Section~\ref{sec:selection} asks which ones do.

\subsection{Economy-wide instruments follow economy-wide displacement}

The breadth of displacement differs by an order of magnitude across challengers. The groups that Japan had overtaken accounted for 27 to 35 per cent of American manufactured exports in 1977--1987, and the groups China had overtaken for 45 per cent in 2018; for Korea and Taiwan the share stayed below 6 per cent before 1990 \citep{comtrade}. The response scaled with it. Korea and Taiwan met instruments in single sectors. Japan met broad instruments in six sectors between 1957 and 1987 (textiles, steel, televisions, cars, machine tools and semiconductors), among them restraints covering whole industries and retaliatory tariffs on electronics. China met the Section 301 tariffs of 2018, which covered all 813 HS groups in which it exported at least 50 million dollars, the 609 it had displaced and the 204 it had not. Other targeted broad instruments against China were too rare to estimate a panel effect (five events; rate ratio 1.13, interval 0.11 to 11.24). Of the 242 groups that China overtook after 2008, 74 per cent drew a broad instrument within ten years when the tariffs of 2018 are counted and 1 per cent when they are not. An economy-wide instrument followed economy-wide displacement and did not discriminate between sectors. Why Japan's broad displacement was answered sector by sector and China's at once is a question of form (Section~\ref{sec:form}).

\section{Which displaced sectors are constrained}\label{sec:selection}

If displacement is not sufficient, something must select the sectors that are constrained. Import volume does not: among the displaced groups of Japan and Korea, the share drawing a broad instrument within ten years is 27, 14 and 23 per cent by third of American imports from the challenger. Employment does not either: 19, 14 and 29 per cent by third (logit coefficient 0.24, $p=0.43$, 63 groups). With so few broad events these tests have little power, but they show no strong selection on the size of imports or of the domestic workforce. Parliamentary records point to political voice that predates displacement (Table~\ref{tab:voice}).

\subsection{Britain and Germany}

Aggregate attention to Germany in British economic debates rose from 14 mentions per 100,000 words in 1850--1869 to 20 in 1870--1892 and about 44 after 1893. A search for structural breaks that does not impose the dates finds the best single break in 1871 and, with two breaks, 1871 and 1894, one year after the dye and the steel milestones. The rise is uneven across sectors (Table~\ref{tab:voice}, panel A). In sugar debates, Germany is almost absent before 1880 and appears in about one in six afterwards, a debate that ended in the Brussels Convention. In iron and steel its share rises from 15 to 24 per cent, with overlapping intervals. Dyes and chemicals, where German leadership came first, were debated only 18 times in 65 years. Parliament took up German competition where it hurt sectors with many producers, workers or colonial interests.

Growth alone did not trigger attention. American income per head overtook British income around 1900 \citep{bolt2024}, but mentions of the United States stayed between 41 and 56 per 100,000 words of economic debate in all four periods, and the share of debates linking it to competition stayed near one per cent. The phrase ``American invasion'', common in the press of the time, does not occur once in the 15,126 economic debates. The trade data explain why. In a fixed panel of 17 third markets, American exports reached 75 per cent of British exports by 1913 but only 51 per cent outside Canada, against 85 per cent for Germany \citep{fouquin2016}; in manufactures, the American share of world exports rose only from 11.2 to 12.6 per cent between 1899 and 1913, the German share from 22.2 to 26.4 per cent \citep{tyszynski1951}. The United States outgrew Britain at home; Germany displaced it in its export markets.

\subsection{Congress, Japan and Korea}

On the sampled sitting days of 1955--1994, attention to Japan as a competitor stayed low through the 1960s and 1970s, when Japanese textiles, steel and television sets overtook American exports, and rose in the 1980s; the best single break falls in 1980, the year of the car milestone. Attention to Korea stayed at about a tenth of that level. At the level of the sector, two measures must be kept apart: how much Congress talks about a sector, and how much of that talk names the challenger. The second does not separate displaced sectors that met broad constraint from those that did not ($p$ between 0.11 and 0.59). The first does, and before displacement. In the five years before the crossover, Congress mentioned the six Japanese sectors that later drew a broad instrument (textiles, steel, television sets, cars, semiconductors and machine tools) 111 times per million words and the eight without one (among them ships, footwear, toys and tyres) 17 times ($p=0.006$); for Korea the figures are 62 and 15 ($p=0.020$).

Sector volume is correlated with the size of the American industry, so we regress the indicator for a broad instrument on log sector volume, log American employment and log American imports from the challenger across all 28 sectors. Volume keeps its effect (permutation $p=0.011$), employment loses its effect once volume is included ($p=0.33$) and imports keep a borderline one ($p=0.084$); at the level of the 63 displaced groups the result is the same. Among displaced sectors, broad instruments went to those with a standing voice in Congress. Displacement set the timing; voice that existed before it selected the sectors.

\subsection{Congress and China}

China does not fit this pattern. Its trade status was itself legislation in the 1990s, and attention in economic debates peaked with permanent normal trade relations in 2000, at 399 mentions per 100,000 words, before most Chinese crossovers; a break search finds no rise after them. Across 17 sectors, those that later drew a sector-specific instrument had more debate and more China-linked debate than displaced sectors without one, but none of the differences is significant (Table~\ref{tab:voice}, panel B). Congressional voice does not predict how heavily a sector was hit by the first Section 301 lists of 2018 \citep[Spearman $\rho=0.06$;][]{ustr301} and is only weakly associated with the sector increases of 2024 ($p=0.056$). With employment and imports held constant, the sectors that drew instruments had lower, not higher, American imports from China ($p=0.025$). The instruments went to steel, aluminium, solar cells, washing machines, telecom equipment, semiconductors and cars, not to consumer-goods sectors with on average larger imports from China, such as computers, furniture, toys and footwear. Lists 1 and 2 of the Section 301 tariffs, the first to take effect at 25 per cent, concentrated on machinery, electronics and vehicles. Against an economy-wide challenger the incumbent chose sectors by strategic importance, not by the voice of the domestic industry.

\section{Further cases}\label{sec:further}

The core episodes select on catch-up that ended in broad constraint. We therefore apply the same measures to eight further dyads, chosen before looking at the responses: Japan against Britain in the 1930s, four economies that displaced American exports in a few sectors (Taiwan, Hong Kong, Brazil and Mexico) and three that are usually described as having absorbed technology without displacing anyone (Thailand, Malaysia and Vietnam). For the American dyads the register is extended by 19 entries from the Statutes at Large and the Federal Register \citep{statutes,moore1996,dinopoulos1991}, and the crossover rule, the hazard and the panel are re-run. Taiwan's exports are measured by the imports of all reporters from ``Other Asia, not elsewhere specified'', and Hong Kong's exclude re-exports (Online Appendix~G).

\emph{Japan against Britain.} Japanese cotton piece goods overtook British goods in the import market of British India in 1932 (552 against 542 million yards; \citealp{hansard}, 4 July 1934), and in 1933 Japan's world exports of piece goods passed Britain's: 2,090 against 2,031 million square yards \citep{robson1957,sandberg1974}. By value, Japanese exports were still 73 per cent of British ones in 1935, because Japanese cloth was cheaper \citep{lon1945}. Displacement was regional. Across 35 third markets, Japan's total exports rose only from 23 to 33 per cent of Britain's between 1924 and 1936; in seven Asian markets they rose from 42 per cent in 1924 to 86 per cent in 1936, and in the Netherlands Indies Japan led from 1930 \citep{fouquin2016}. The response was regional too. All four binding instruments (IR 4) came from the milestone onwards: India's 50 per cent duty on non-British piece goods of August 1932, ``in consequence of the abnormal imports from Japan'', the 75 per cent duty of June 1933, the Indo-Japanese quota of January 1934 and the colonial quotas on foreign cotton and rayon goods of May 1934. Before the milestone there were only an economic mission to the Far East and India's modest preference of 1930, which also protected Indian mills against Lancashire. Mentions of Japan in British economic debates stayed between 1 and 10 per 100,000 words in 1920--1931, reached 12 in 1932 and 102 in 1933, with a single break in 1932. The British home market, where Japan had not displaced Britain, received no Japan-specific measure.

\emph{Newly industrialised economies.} Displacement in these cases was sectoral, and so were the instruments. Taiwanese footwear overtook American exports in 1970 and met an orderly marketing agreement in 1977; Brazilian steel overtook American exports in 1983 and was included in the restraint agreements of 1984. Three instruments do not follow displacement. The colour-television agreement of 1979 and the machine-tool restraint of 1986 extended instruments aimed at Japan to Taiwan, and the steel agreements of 1984 extended them to Mexico, in groups where these suppliers had not overtaken American exports: the incumbent closed the route for diversion. The 100 per cent tariffs on Brazilian goods of 1988 retaliated for the absence of pharmaceutical patents. Leaving aside the graduation of Hong Kong and Taiwan from tariff preferences in 1989, an economy-wide but rule-based step, targeted broad instruments against the seven economies began 5.8 times as often after a group's crossover as before (95 per cent interval 2.8 to 12.0), close to the ratio for Japan and Korea. Displacement again was far from sufficient: a sectoral broad instrument followed within ten years in 4 of 9 displaced groups for Brazil, all of them steel, in 1 of 24 for Taiwan and in none of 9 for Hong Kong outside the textile regime.

\emph{Negative controls.} Thailand and Malaysia never displaced American exports in more than a sliver of trade: at their peak, the groups they had overtaken accounted for 2 and 6 per cent of American manufactured exports. Apart from the quotas of the textile regime, no targeted broad American instrument was aimed at either. Vietnam and Mexico are harder cases. By 2023 Vietnam had overtaken American exports in 101 groups worth 6 per cent of American manufactured exports, and Mexico had reached 7 per cent. Neither drew a targeted broad instrument before 2025, and none of the 283 groups that Thailand, Malaysia, Mexico, Vietnam, Taiwan and Brazil overtook between 2009 and 2023 drew one within the period observed. This was a period in which Article 11 of the WTO Agreement on Safeguards had outlawed the sectoral restraints of the earlier cases. The broad tariffs of 2025 on Mexico and Brazil were justified by migration, drugs and the prosecution of a former Brazilian president, and the ``reciprocal'' tariffs of April 2025 covered all trading partners and were justified by bilateral deficits, not by displacement \citep{statutes}.

The further cases confirm the gradient: sectoral displacement (Taiwan, Brazil) drew sectoral instruments, and marginal displacement (Thailand, Malaysia, Hong Kong outside textiles) drew none. They add two qualifications. Incumbents extend instruments aimed at the main challenger to its followers before these displace them, and they use broad tariffs for purposes unrelated to displacement.

\section{Alignment and the form of constraint}\label{sec:form}

Displacement dates the response; alignment shapes its form. We code every broad instrument as negotiated (a voluntary restraint, an orderly marketing agreement, a bilateral or multilateral arrangement) or unilateral (a tariff, a statute, a listing, an export control) and match each instrument--target pair to the state of the dyad in that year: a defence pact in the Alliance Treaty Obligations and Provisions data \citep{leeds2002}, the distance between ideal points estimated from votes in the UN General Assembly \citep{bailey2017} and the ratio of composite national capabilities from the Correlates of War project \citep{nmc}.

Before 1995, 21 of the 25 targeted, trade-motivated broad instruments that the United States aimed at treaty allies (Japan, Korea, Taiwan until 1980, Brazil, Mexico and Thailand) were negotiated restraints. The four unilateral ones were the trigger price for steel (1978), the motorcycle tariff (1983) and the Section 301 tariffs on Japanese electronics (1987) and on Brazilian goods (1988). After 1995 no targeted, trade-motivated broad instrument was aimed at an ally. All five hit economies without a defence pact with the United States: four unilateral instruments against China, from the Section 301 tariffs of 2018 to the tariff on electric vehicles of 2024, and the textile agreement with Vietnam of 2003. Britain's binding responses to Japan in the 1930s, in a dyad without an alliance, were three unilateral measures and one agreement. This explains why Japan's broad displacement was answered with negotiated restraints sector by sector and China's with one unilateral tariff on every group. Two changes coincide in 1995, however, and the data cannot separate them: the WTO Agreement on Safeguards outlawed negotiated restraints, and the one challenger that displaced the United States across the economy after 1995 was also its main strategic rival.

Alignment does not predict whether a displaced economy meets constraint. Among the economies that displaced the United States in many groups after 2008, Mexico (an ally, UN distance 2.5) and Vietnam (no alliance, 3.4) both drew no targeted broad instrument before 2025.

Multilateral finance offers a third channel of constraint, but it does not follow displacement. In the coded conditions of 1,173 IMF programme years in 116 countries between 1989 and 2014 \citep{stubbs2020}, closer voting with the United States goes with a higher probability of a programme, as \citet{thacker1999} and \citet{barro2005} found, and with more binding conditions; neither association survives country fixed effects. The challengers of this paper entered programmes after financial crises (Mexico 1989 and 1995, Korea 1997, Brazil 1998--2004, Vietnam 1994--1996 and 2001--2002), when the groups they had overtaken accounted for at most 2.3 per cent of American manufactured exports. Their programmes carried twice as many conditions on trade and the external sector as other programmes with the same alignment, income, growth and openness (rate ratio 2.16), but no more conditions overall. Conditionality narrowed the policy space of borrowers \citep{kentikelenis2016}; it was not aimed at the sectors in which they displaced the incumbent (Online Appendix~J).

\section{Two bounded exceptions}\label{sec:exceptions}

Two sets of broad instruments came before displacement. Each is confined to one sector, and each has its own logic.

\emph{The textile regime.} From the Short-Term Arrangement of 1961 and the Long-Term Arrangement on cotton textiles of 1962 to the Multifibre Arrangement of 1974 and its phase-out in 2005, incumbents institutionalised the restraint of new low-wage suppliers before these displaced anyone. The register shows this for every such supplier in our data. Korea was restrained under the Long-Term Arrangement of 1962 and the agreements of 1971, ten and two years before its exports in the groups concerned matched American ones. The first comprehensive American cotton agreements with Hong Kong and Taiwan (both 1963) came two and eight years before their textile and clothing exports overtook American exports; those with Mexico (1967), Brazil and Malaysia (1970) and Thailand (1972) restrained suppliers that never overtook American exports in these groups; and Vietnam's apparel met a bilateral quota in 2003, three years before its apparel exports overtook American ones. Textiles were the first sector that every late industrialiser entered, with a mature and politically organised industry in the incumbent. The regime turned the sectoral instrument into a standing rule applied to every entrant.

\emph{Export controls on advanced chips.} The American controls on advanced computing and semiconductor manufacturing equipment of 7 October 2022 (IR 5) preceded the confirmation of SMIC's 7\,nm production in 2023 by a year and were designed to deny China a capability it had not yet acquired. They are the only broad instrument among the 41 coded responses that precedes the milestone of its sector, and they came in a campaign of tariffs and listings that followed China's manufacturing, solar and telecommunications milestones. They also fall in the one dyad in which the challenger was a military peer. When the controls were imposed, the distance between Chinese and American positions in the General Assembly was 3.5 points on the ideal-point scale, against 1.1 to 1.5 for Japan in the years of its milestones, and China's composite capabilities were 1.9 times those of the United States, a measure that weights population and heavy industry. Germany's capabilities stood at 0.44 to 0.79 of Britain's in the years of its milestones, Japan's at 0.26 to 0.40 of America's and Korea's at 0.15 to 0.16. Technology denial, the one instrument that can be used before displacement, has so far been reserved for a rival.

\section{Limits}\label{sec:limits}

\emph{Sample and selection.} Four core cases and 41 responses are a small sample, and the cases are linked. The core selects on successful catch-up; the further cases add negative controls, but Thailand and Malaysia displaced too little to test much, and the displacement by Vietnam and Mexico falls into a period without sectoral restraints.

\emph{Coding.} All instrument-reach scores currently come from one coder; the second coding is in progress. Several milestones rest on one or two secondary sources, among them Korean shipbuilding (1999, by orders, with Japan ahead again in 2001), and some depend on the measure (Chinese telecom equipment: total revenue in 2012, a near tie that turns on exchange rates). The target countries of several Section 201 actions are taken from secondary literature.

\emph{Measurement.} The crossover rule measures displacement by world exports, which understates displacement in the American home market; trade statistics do not always measure the contested product. Antidumping cases are matched to groups through concordances of 1988--92 HS codes, and employment is allocated to groups by tariff lines. Taiwan's exports are measured from partner imports. For Japan against Britain, the bilateral trade data measure total exports, not cotton goods, and the British and Japanese series for piece goods are not fully comparable.

\emph{Attention.} Economic debates are selected by title, which misses economic discussion under other headings; in the daily Congressional Record the filter captures only one in five passages that link China to competition. Before 1995 the American measure rests on a sample of 800 sitting days with OCR errors, too few for sector counts in single years. Debate volume measures attention, not lobbying or votes.

\emph{Identification.} After 1995, alliance status and displacement across the economy coincide in a single case, China, so the data cannot separate rivalry from the reach of displacement or from the WTO's ban on negotiated restraints. Most fundamentally, the evidence orders events; it does not identify the effect of displacement on policy. Each of these limits is documented in the Online Appendix (Section~L).

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Across four incumbents and 175 years, the reach of constraint follows the reach of displacement. Narrow remedies against single products run throughout catch-up and follow import exposure in the incumbent's home market. Quantity limits on whole sectors come once the challenger has taken the incumbent's markets in a sector, and only in some displaced sectors: in those with a standing voice in the incumbent's legislature or, against a strategic rival, in those the incumbent's government regards as strategic. Tariffs across the economy come when displacement covers the economy. Alliance shapes whether the incumbent negotiates or acts alone. The lag between displacement and binding response has shortened, from 17 to 37 years in free-trade Britain to zero to nine years against Japan and one to eight against China, as each episode left the next with new instruments: antidumping law, Section 301, the entity list, export controls on equipment. The textile regime and the controls of October 2022 show that incumbents can act before displacement when a sector is organised to do so or a technology is held to be strategic.

The sequence also bears on convergence. Total factor productivity relative to the United States peaked at 0.80 in Japan in 1991 and 0.71 in Korea in 2002, after the escalation phase documented here; Japan fell back to 0.64 by 2023, and Korea has stayed between 0.62 and 0.70 since 2010 \citep[Online Appendix~K]{feenstra2015,pwt110}. Germany has been at or above the American level for most years since 2000. The paper does not claim that constraint caused the stall: domestic explanations remain strong \citep{acemoglu2006,eichengreen2012}, and regression to the mean is observationally equivalent in a sample of this size \citep{pritchett2014}.

For late industrialisers the implication is practical. The phase in which foreign technology can be absorbed without systematic resistance ends with success, when the challenger begins to take the incumbent's markets in sectors that matter politically to the incumbent. The reach of the response can be anticipated from the reach of that success, and its form from the challenger's place in the incumbent's alliances. Planning for that moment is part of development strategy.
