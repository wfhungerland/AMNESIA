# Displacement Before Escalation: Systematic Literature Integration Across Five Scholarly Fields

**Prepared for:** *Displacement Before Escalation: Technology Acquisition, Incumbent Response, and Convergence Limits in Four Late-Industrialising Economies*  
**Target journal:** *World Development*

---

## Framing

The paper's core empirical claim is a timing regularity: in four historical catch-up cases — Germany (1840–1914), Japan (1950–1990), South Korea (1960–1997), and China (1978–2024) — measurable technological displacement precedes rhetorical escalation and binding trade policy intervention by incumbent powers. The claim is not a general law and does not assert policy effectiveness; it asserts a recurrent sequencing pattern in the examined cases.

Five established literatures approach adjacent questions without closing the analytical loop the paper targets. The developmental state canon treats incumbent reaction as exogenous context. The convergence literature attributes deceleration entirely to endogenous forces. The trade policy political economy literature tests cross-sectional protection levels rather than temporal sequences. The technology denial literature indexes escalation timing to political-strategic variables rather than displacement metrics. The IP history literature documents weak-IP catch-up without connecting enforcement demands to displacement thresholds. Each section below maps what each literature already establishes, where the paper confirms, extends, contradicts, or fills a gap, and where overclaiming risks arise.

## A. Developmental State and Catch-Up Theory

### What the literature claims

The canonical developmental state works — Gerschenkron (1962), Johnson (1982), Amsden (1989), Wade (1990), Evans (1995), Chang (2002), Lall (1992) — collectively establish that late industrialisers succeed through deliberate institutional substitution. Gerschenkron identified universal banks as the German substitute for the absent capital markets that Britain had generated organically. Johnson demonstrated how MITI coordinated industrial structure in Japan through administrative guidance, selective credit allocation, and the deliberate management of "excessive competition." Amsden (1989) showed that Korean state subsidies were conditional on export performance, disciplining firms in ways that a purely market-oriented approach would not achieve. Wade (1990) described "governed markets" in Taiwan where state agencies shaped relative prices to accelerate structural transformation. Evans (1995) formalised the concept of "embedded autonomy" — bureaucratic insulation combined with dense ties to the private sector — as the institutional prerequisite for effective developmental intervention. Chang (2002) delivered the most pointed normative version of the argument: today's developed countries used identical protectionist and technology-acquisition instruments during their own catch-up phases, and their subsequent demands that developing countries liberalise amount to "kicking away the ladder." Lall (1992) provided the micro-level technological capability framework: firms progress from operational to duplicative to adaptive to innovative capacities, and state policy can accelerate transitions.

### Confirmation

The paper's four cases draw on institutions and mechanisms well documented in this literature. The empirical substrate — MITI-licensed technology imports, Korean Heavy and Chemical Industry programme, German *Zollverein*, Chinese indigenous innovation policies — is established.

### Extension and reframing

The paper fills a structural gap that originates with Gerschenkron himself: **the incumbent is analytically absent from the entire tradition**. Gerschenkron's model is exclusively supply-side; Britain appears as a static productivity frontier against which backwardness is measured, never as a reactive agent that responds to catch-up. Johnson's coverage ends in approximately 1975, before the most intense phase of US-Japan trade friction — the 1981 automobile Voluntary Export Restraint, the 1986 Semiconductor Trade Agreement, and the 1989 Super 301 designations of Japan. Amsden (1989) and Wade (1990) mention US trade pressure as part of the external environment that Korean and Taiwanese policymakers navigated, but neither models this pressure as endogenous to displacement. Evans (1995) discusses US Section 301 pressure on Brazil's informatics reservation but treats it as a political given, not a dependent variable. Lall's hierarchical capability framework contains no analysis of when incumbents begin resisting technology transfer as those capabilities progress.

Chang (2002) comes closest to the paper's territory: he identifies the structural pattern — incumbents demand free trade after achieving leadership — and explicitly quotes Friedrich List's 1841 metaphor of the ladder. But Chang provides no temporal mechanism. He demonstrates that incumbents eventually restrict catch-up without specifying at what point in the displacement process they begin to do so, what triggers the transition from tolerance to hostility, or whether the timing follows any regularity across cases.

### Contradiction

The paper does not contradict the developmental state literature's findings. It extends their domain of inquiry into a dimension they deliberately set aside.

### Gap filled

The paper endogenises incumbent reaction, treating it as caused by displacement rather than as background noise, and provides the temporal sequencing model the entire Gerschenkron-descended tradition leaves unmapped.

### Overclaiming risk

The paper should not assert that the developmental state literature is unaware of incumbent response — Wade's revised editions discuss international pressures, and Johnson was cognisant of US-Japan friction. The precise claim is that incumbent reaction is treated as exogenous context rather than as an endogenous variable whose timing is systematically connected to displacement metrics.

## B. Middle-Income Trap and Convergence Literature

### What the literature claims

The convergence and middle-income trap literature establishes quantitative thresholds for growth deceleration. Eichengreen, Park and Shin (2012) identify bimodal slowdown points at approximately $10,000–$11,000 and $15,000–$16,000 (2005 PPP), and find that approximately **85 per cent** of the slowdown in the rate of output growth is explained by the slowdown in TFP growth — the dominant proximate source of stagnation in middle-income economies. Their subsequent (2014) paper confirms these bimodal threshold estimates with an expanded sample. Felipe, Abdon and Kumar (2012) define trap escape through time-based criteria: countries must sustain at least 4.7 per cent annual growth to escape the lower-middle-income trap and 3.5 per cent to escape the upper-middle-income trap. Acemoglu, Aghion and Zilibotti (2006) model a structural transition near the frontier: countries converging via "investment-based strategies" (imitation, large conglomerates, protected markets) must switch to "innovation-based strategies" as they approach the productivity frontier, and stall when domestic political economy blocks this switch.

The broader literature identifies predominantly endogenous causes of deceleration: talent misallocation (Agénor and Canuto 2012), within-sector resource misallocation (Hsieh and Klenow 2009, who estimate potential TFP gains of 30–50 per cent in Chinese manufacturing from reallocation alone), extractive institutions blocking creative destruction (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012), exhaustion of factor accumulation (Young 1995), and premature deindustrialisation shrinking the manufacturing productivity escalator (Rodrik 2016).

### Confirmation

The paper's observed convergence ceiling of approximately 60–75 per cent of frontier productivity is broadly consistent with the deceleration zone identified in this literature, particularly the Eichengreen et al. higher-income slowdown cluster.

### Extension

The literature attributes the convergence ceiling entirely to endogenous factors. The paper introduces a complementary exogenous mechanism: incumbent intervention — trade barriers, technology denial, IP enforcement demands — systematically coincides with the convergence zone and may reinforce rather than merely accompany the endogenous ceiling. This distinction is absent from the convergence literature. The World Bank (2024) gestures in this direction by noting that "rising geopolitical tensions and protectionism can slow the diffusion of knowledge to middle-income countries" but does not formalise the connection or test its timing.

### Contradiction

The paper implicitly challenges the dominant decomposition that attributes slowdowns entirely to domestic TFP deceleration. If incumbent intervention systematically coincides with the 60–75 per cent frontier zone and demonstrably restricts technology access, standard growth-accounting exercises may conflate cause and consequence by attributing to domestic institutional failure what is in part an exogenous restriction on technology diffusion.

### Gap filled

The convergence literature has no analysis of whether growth deceleration near the frontier is partly caused, reinforced, or accelerated by incumbent policy responses to displacement. This paper introduces that analytical dimension.

### Overclaiming risk

The 60–75 per cent frontier threshold is not a formal consensus in this literature. The Eichengreen et al. income-based thresholds are lower than 60–75 per cent of US productivity; the higher convergence ceiling appears in specific country observations (Japan, parts of Western Europe). The paper should present this as an empirical regularity documented in its four cases rather than as a confirmed finding from the convergence literature it is reproducing.

## C. Trade Conflict and Political Economy of Protection

### What the literature claims

The political economy of trade protection literature offers powerful models of who gets protection and how much, but is remarkably silent on when. Grossman and Helpman (1994) provide the field's workhorse — a static menu-auction where lobbies bid for tariffs and governments maximise a weighted sum of contributions and welfare. It predicts cross-sectional variation in protection levels across industries but contains no time dimension. Rogowski (1989) predicts which factor-owning coalitions will favour protection through Stolper-Samuelson logic, but process-tracing is structurally absent and the theory cannot distinguish between a one-month and a ten-year lag between displacement and response. Milner (1988) explains why some displaced sectors demand protection while others do not — internationalisation of firms mediates the response — but her research design compares two cross-sectional periods rather than tracing the displacement-to-policy timeline continuously within cases. Irwin (2017) provides the most comprehensive historical documentation of coincidences between displacement episodes and protectionist responses in US trade history, but as narrative rather than formal sequencing tests. Destler (1992) documents the institutional lag structure (12–18 months from petition filing to final duty determination) as an institutional feature of US trade law rather than as a testable hypothesis about the displacement-to-response interval.

The closest empirical test of a sequencing hypothesis is Bown and Crowley (2013a), who show that a one standard deviation increase in recent bilateral import growth raises the probability of an antidumping tariff by 35 per cent — technically a test of whether lagged import surges predict subsequent protection filings. Their companion paper (Bown and Crowley 2013b) documents strong countercyclical patterns: rising unemployment, real exchange rate appreciation, and declining partner GDP all predict new temporary trade barriers. These findings are framed within Bagwell and Staiger's terms-of-trade theory of cooperative trade agreements, not as tests of displacement triggering systemic incumbent escalation.

Bhagwati's "quid pro quo" foreign investment framework (Bhagwati et al. 1987) is the most theoretically congruent with the paper's argument. It explicitly models a two-period temporal structure: rising challenger exports in period one determine the probability of protection in period two, and foreign firms invest preemptively to defuse that threat. Blonigen and Feenstra (1997) confirmed this anticipatory logic empirically, finding that the threat of US protection strongly influenced Japanese FDI decisions. This sequence is viewed from the challenger's perspective rather than as a test of incumbent behaviour — but it implicitly validates the temporal regularity the paper documents.

### Confirmation

The paper is consistent with Bown and Crowley's finding that import growth predicts subsequent protection. It extends this logic from the product-bilateral level to the systemic level of great-power technological rivalry over 175 years.

### Extension

The paper introduces rhetorical escalation as an intermediate variable between displacement and binding policy — entirely absent from the existing literature. Rodrik (1994) explicitly flagged the literature's inability to explain the dynamics and timing of protection as an unresolved gap; the paper addresses this directly. The three-step sequence (displacement → rhetorical escalation → binding policy) is the paper's structural contribution to this literature.

### Contradiction

The paper does not contradict any empirical finding in this literature. It extends the temporal dimension into territory the static models cannot address.

### Gap filled

No existing work provides a cross-case historical test of whether the displacement-to-policy sequence follows a consistent pattern across major catch-up episodes. Bown and Crowley operate at the product-bilateral level; Irwin provides narrative documentation. The paper provides the systematic multi-case sequencing test.

### Overclaiming risk

A reviewer will note that Bown and Crowley (2013a) already demonstrate lagged import surges predict protection, and may argue the paper merely relabels this at a higher level of aggregation. The rebuttal must specify what the paper adds: the systemic level of analysis, the rhetorical escalation intermediate step, and the four-case historical regularity spanning 175 years. The paper should also engage directly with Destler's institutional lag evidence: if the typical AD petition takes 12–18 months, this institutional structure partly determines the observable displacement-to-policy interval and must not be conflated with the strategic timing the paper theorises.

## D. Technology Denial and Export Controls

### What the literature claims

The technology denial literature centres on the Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls (CoCom, 1949–1994). Mastanduno (1992) provides the authoritative analysis, distinguishing between a narrow "strategic embargo" targeting items of direct military significance — the approach consistently preferred by European allies — and a broader "economic warfare" strategy seeking to impose cumulative costs on the target economy, periodically pursued by the United States under Truman (1949–53) and Reagan (1981–85). Even the broader economic warfare approach controlled finished products and specific technologies — computers, telecommunications equipment, machine tools — rather than targeting the upstream manufacturing equipment and design software needed to produce those technologies.

Cold War technology denial was further complicated by alliance frictions. The 1982 controversy over the Urengoy–Pomary–Uzhhorod pipeline — a 4,500-kilometre natural gas pipeline from Western Siberia to Western Europe — illustrates the structural limits of extraterritorial technology controls. The Reagan administration imposed sanctions on companies supplying turbine compressors and pipe-laying equipment, including European subsidiaries of American firms. European allies, led by Britain, France, and Germany, defied these extraterritorial controls openly, shipping equipment in violation of the embargo. Facing unified allied resistance, Reagan lifted the sanctions in November 1982 after five months, while the pipeline proceeded to completion in 1984 and delivered gas to Western Europe for four decades. The 1987 Toshiba-Kongsberg case — involving submarine propeller-milling machines — triggered congressional backlash but targeted an identifiable military end-use, not a broad civilian manufacturing industry. The CHINCOM "China Differential" (1952–57) imposed more restrictive controls than CoCom on a pre-industrial China with negligible absorptive capacity.

The 2022–24 US semiconductor controls represent a qualitative departure from this history. The October 2022 Bureau of Industry and Security rules simultaneously restricted advanced logic chips, semiconductor manufacturing equipment (lithography, deposition, etching tools), electronic design automation software, high-bandwidth memory, and the participation of US persons in Chinese chip-related activities. Lind and Mastanduno (2025) compare this architecture directly to CoCom and identify the critical innovation: the controls target the entire upstream toolchain of a major civilian manufacturing industry, not specific finished products or military end-use applications. Their analysis also notes that China, unlike the Soviet Union, has demonstrated the capacity to absorb and begin innovating with denied technologies, requiring the Sullivan Doctrine's shift from maintaining a "relative lead" (two technology generations) to seeking absolute denial.

### Confirmation

The paper's historical analysis of CoCom is consistent with Mastanduno's characterisation of the strategic embargo versus economic warfare distinction. The paper's identification of 2022–24 semiconductor controls as qualitatively novel is supported by Lind and Mastanduno (2025).

### Extension

No existing work in this literature analyses the timing of technology denial relative to displacement metrics. Mastanduno indexes timing to Cold War political-strategic cycles — NSC-68, détente, the Reagan build-up. Drezner (1999) indexes the severity of economic statecraft to conflict expectations. Neither approach connects technology denial initiation to measurable indicators of technological displacement. The paper would be the first systematic cross-case analysis of whether technology denial escalates in response to displacement rather than to strategic threat assessments.

### Gap filled

The upstream civilian supply chain targeting of 2022–24 lacks clear historical precedent. By documenting this analytical novelty and comparing the CoCom and contemporary architectures within a unified displacement-sequencing framework, the paper extends the technology denial literature into territory it has not mapped.

### Overclaiming risk

The claim that 2022–24 upstream targeting is unprecedented must be precisely bounded. CoCom did control some semiconductor fabrication equipment from the mid-1980s onward, particularly following the Toshiba-Kongsberg case. The novelty lies in the breadth, depth, and civilian orientation of simultaneous chokepoint targeting — equipment, software, components, and human capital — not in the mere existence of equipment controls. The paper should also acknowledge that the security-versus-competitive framing is contested: Bateman (2022) noted that the economic damage to China is disproportionate to the cited military and intelligence justifications, which itself bears on the displacement-escalation thesis.

## E. Economic History of Imitation and IP Evolution

### What the literature claims

A robust scholarly consensus establishes that major catch-up economies systematically exploited weak IP regimes during their industrialisation. Khan (2004) provides the strongest quantitative evidence for the US case: federal copyright statutes explicitly condoned piracy of foreign works from 1790 until 1891, and Khan concludes that "the United States benefited from piracy and that the choice of copyright regime was endogenous to the level of economic development." Moser (2005) uses World's Fair exhibition data to demonstrate that countries without patent laws innovated effectively across a range of industries, challenging the conventional welfare case for strong patent protection. Moser and Voena (2012) exploit the seizure of German-owned US patents under the Trading with the Enemy Act (1917) as a natural experiment, finding that eliminating patent protection in affected sectors actually increased US innovation — consistent with the view that strong IP regimes in catch-up phases retard rather than accelerate domestic innovation. Lerner (2002), reviewing 150 years of cross-country data, documents that wealthier countries have stronger IP regimes but cannot resolve the direction of causality. Boldrin and Levine (2008) argue that established industries seek IP protection precisely when their competitive advantage erodes — a mechanism structurally aligned with the paper's displacement-escalation thesis. Chang (2002) provides the broadest qualitative synthesis: today's developed countries "poached skilled workers and smuggled contraband machines from more developed countries, engaged in industrial espionage, and wilfully violated patents and trademarks" during their own catch-up phases.

The 19th-century Anglo-American copyright dispute is the most extensively documented historical case of incumbent IP enforcement demands. British authors and publishers petitioned Congress from 1837 onward; Charles Dickens lobbied personally in 1842 and 1867–68; international copyright protection was enacted only in 1891 — a 55-year lag from the first formal demand. Khan's (2004) explanation is domestic political economy: reform succeeded when American publishers found that pirate-edition competition eroded their own margins, and when American authors had become sufficiently numerous to seek protection themselves. This is consistent with the paper's framing — IP enforcement demands coincide with the catching-up country becoming a net IP producer — but Khan does not use displacement terminology or connect reform timing to trade or productivity metrics. The modern analog is the Section 301 IP enforcement mechanism: the 1984 Trade and Tariff Act created IP-specific Section 301 authority at the precise moment when Japanese and newly industrialising country technology-intensive exports had reached competitive scale; Korea and Brazil were the first Section 301 IP targets in 1985; TRIPS negotiations (1986–94) ran concurrent with peak Japanese-Korean technological competition with the United States.

### Confirmation

The paper's cross-case finding that catch-up economies systematically operated under weak IP regimes is thoroughly supported by Khan (2004), Moser (2005), and Chang (2002). The Section 301 timing evidence is consistent with the paper's sequencing thesis.

### Extension

The paper provides the systematic timing analysis this literature has not attempted: testing whether IP enforcement demands emerge at the initiation of catch-up, when the catching-up country begins exporting, or when displacement becomes measurable in frontier industries. The evidence across all four cases is most consistent with the third option, but this has never been formally tested against the alternatives. Lamoreaux and Sokoloff (1996, 1999) contribute important institutional analysis of the US patent system's role in fostering markets for technology, but their work is primarily about domestic institutional design and does not extend to international enforcement timing.

### Contradiction

The paper implicitly challenges any interpretation that treats IP reform as driven primarily by domestic political economy (the Khan explanation) rather than as a response to foreign technological displacement. The paper does not deny that domestic interests matter; it adds a complementary international trigger that the existing literature has not identified.

### Gap filled

No existing work formally connects the timing of incumbent IP enforcement demands to displacement thresholds. The Section 301 record provides the strongest circumstantial evidence, but it has not been analysed within a cross-case displacement-sequencing framework.

### Overclaiming risk

The relationship between IP enforcement demands and displacement is confounded by Cold War strategic calculations (which delayed US pressure on Japan and Korea) and by the broader Uruguay Round dynamics within which TRIPS emerged. Dutton (1984), MacLeod (1988), and Lamoreaux and Sokoloff (1996) are appropriately cited as context for domestic IP institutional evolution, but should not be cited as direct evidence for the international sequencing claim.

## Synthesis

The paper occupies a precise niche that no existing work fills: it endogenises the incumbent's response and provides temporal specification for a regularity that five adjacent literatures acknowledge only fragmentarily. The developmental state tradition, from Gerschenkron (1962) through Chang (2002), documents how catch-up economies acquire technology but treats the incumbent's reaction as exogenous. The convergence literature, from Eichengreen, Park and Shin (2012) through Acemoglu, Aghion and Zilibotti (2006), quantifies where growth stalls but attributes deceleration exclusively to domestic endogenous factors. The trade policy political economy literature, from Grossman and Helpman (1994) through Bown and Crowley (2013a), models equilibrium protection levels in static cross-section or tests macro-cyclical correlates without tracing the displacement-to-escalation sequence across historical catch-up cases. The technology denial literature, anchored by Mastanduno (1992) and updated by Lind and Mastanduno (2025), indexes escalation timing to political-strategic variables rather than displacement metrics. The IP history literature, from Khan (2004) through Moser and Voena (2012), establishes that catch-up economies exploited weak IP regimes and that enforcement demands correlate with development levels, without formally testing the displacement-trigger timing. The paper closes the analytical loop across all five dimensions with a unified sequencing framework tested against four cases spanning 175 years.

Chang, H.-J. (2002) is essential for establishing the structural pattern — incumbents restrict after achieving leadership — that the paper temporalises and formalises. Eichengreen, Park and Shin (2012) provide the bimodal TFP-deceleration evidence that the paper connects to incumbent response timing. Bown and Crowley (2013a) represent the closest existing empirical test of the displacement-to-protection sequence, from which the paper must differentiate its cross-case historical scope and rhetorical-escalation intermediate step. Mastanduno (1992) establishes the Cold War technology denial baseline against which the paper's claim of unprecedented upstream civilian targeting must be measured. Khan (2004) provides the strongest quantitative evidence for endogeneity of IP regimes to development, directly supporting the paper's imitation-as-normal-catch-up argument. Grossman and Helpman (1994) define the dominant static trade policy model whose temporal limitations the paper's dynamic sequencing approach directly addresses. Acemoglu, Aghion and Zilibotti (2006) supply the theoretical framework for strategy switching near the frontier, which the paper extends by adding exogenous incumbent intervention as a complementary mechanism alongside their endogenous institutional explanation. Irwin (2017) provides the most comprehensive historical narrative of displacement-protection coincidences in US trade history, establishing the evidentiary baseline the paper must surpass with formal sequencing evidence. Lind and Mastanduno (2025) validate the claim that 2022–24 semiconductor controls are architecturally novel relative to CoCom. Moser and Voena (2012) demonstrate that forced IP weakening enhanced domestic innovation in affected sectors, supporting the paper's argument that weak-IP catch-up is functional rather than aberrant.

## Most Likely Literature-Based Criticism

The most potent challenge a sceptical *World Development* reviewer will mount is an identification critique grounded in the convergence literature: the paper's "timing regularity" confuses sequence with causation and, more fundamentally, the displacement-escalation sequence is an artefact of observational selection on successful catch-up cases. Acemoglu, Aghion and Zilibotti (2006) already predict that catch-up economies face endogenous deceleration near the frontier as they exhaust imitation-based growth, and this endogenous deceleration coincides temporally with incumbent responses that are triggered by the same underlying displacement event — without the incumbent response being causally necessary for the convergence ceiling. Under this interpretation, both phenomena are products of a common cause (approaching the frontier), not linked sequentially. The reviewer will supplement this with a case non-independence argument: the four cases are not independent observations — Germany's catch-up informed Japan's strategy, Japan's informed Korea's, and all inform China's — raising the question of whether the "recurrent" pattern reflects a genuine cross-case regularity or a single diffusing model of state-led catch-up that produces similar-looking sequences for structural rather than law-like reasons. Finally, the reviewer will cite Bown and Crowley (2013a) as evidence that the displacement-to-protection sequence is already documented in the trade remedies literature at the product-bilateral level, questioning what the paper adds beyond aggregation.

The paper should preempt this critique through three explicit moves. First, it should disclaim causation and confine the claim to temporal sequencing, explicitly acknowledging that endogenous deceleration and exogenous intervention may be jointly operative. Second, it should treat case non-independence as a feature rather than a defect: if catch-up strategies diffuse across cases but incumbent escalation patterns nonetheless recur across all four, the regularity is more robust than if cases were entirely independent. Third, it should specify precisely what it adds to Bown and Crowley: the systemic level of analysis, the rhetorical escalation intermediate step, and the 175-year temporal scope — none of which appear in the trade remedies literature.
