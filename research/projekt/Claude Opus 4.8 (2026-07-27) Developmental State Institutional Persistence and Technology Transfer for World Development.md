# The Missing Mechanism Between Institutional Persistence and the Middle-Income Trap

*This paper argues that a manuscript linking developmental state theory, institutional persistence, the middle-income trap, and technology transfer cannot be legible to a World Development audience until it supplies a mechanism explaining why persistent institutions bind specifically at middle-income thresholds rather than uniformly across the income distribution.*

## Key Takeaways 1. The four literatures a developmental-state-and-technology manuscript must engage are individually well grounded, but the inferential chain connecting them fails at one joint: institutional persistence explains income levels while the middle-income trap describes growth dynamics, and no existing body of work bridges the two (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson 2001; Eichengreen, Park and Shin 2012).
2. The strongest available bridge is the capabilities distinction between production capacity and technological capability, which implies that colonial institutional legacies may suppress the learning organisations needed for technology absorption at middle-income complexity while leaving factor accumulation intact (Bell and Pavitt 1993; Lavopa and Szirmai 2018).
3. Five recurrent empirical claims will draw reviewer objections unless pre-empted, the most consequential being any use of the middle-income trap that does not separate a structural trap from simple regression to the mean, since roughly 85 per cent of the growth slowdown at middle income is a total-factor-productivity phenomenon rather than a factor-accumulation one (Pritchett and Summers 2014; Eichengreen, Park and Shin 2012).

## Introduction
A manuscript that connects developmental state theory, institutional persistence, the middle-income trap, and technology transfer is attempting a synthesis across four literatures that matured largely in isolation from one another. Each has its own canonical texts, its own identification standards, and its own characteristic objections. For a submission to *World Development*, the difficulty is not that any one literature is weak; it is that the joints between them carry most of the argumentative load while resting on the least settled ground. This paper maps the four clusters, specifies what each requires of the manuscript, and locates the single structural gap on which the whole argument turns.

The framing follows the development-economics conventions of the target journal. Persistence arguments in *World Development* tend to favour mechanism-tracing and conditional claims over the clean cross-country instrument (Roessler et al. 2022; Bauer, Platas and Weinstein 2022), and the journal's technology-and-development strand has long treated capability accumulation, not mere technology access, as the binding constraint (Lall 1992). Both tendencies shape what a reviewer will expect the manuscript to concede and to establish.

## What Developmental State Theory Requires: A Named Mechanism, Not a Model
The developmental state literature offers four distinct causal mechanisms for state-mediated technology acquisition, and a manuscript must commit to one rather than invoking the tradition generically. Johnson (1982) identifies a pilot-agency mechanism in which MITI administered technology acquisition directly through control of foreign-exchange allocation and licensing approval under the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Control Law of 1949 and the Foreign Capital Law of 1950. Amsden (1989) locates the mechanism in reciprocity, the disciplining of subsidies against verifiable export-performance standards, a device she later crystallised as the reciprocal control mechanism (Amsden 2001). Wade (1990) emphasises governance of the financial system, the direction of credit into priority sectors, with public enterprises such as China Steel serving as technology-diffusion anchors. Evans (1995) supplies a configuration rather than an instrument: embedded autonomy, the pairing of bureaucratic insulation with dense private-sector ties, set out through the four state roles of custodian, demiurge, midwifery and husbandry in his fourth chapter.

Each mechanism carries a different scope condition, and this is where the recent literature bites. Khan (2013) argues that identical technology policies produce divergent outcomes according to the distribution of organisational holding power, so that a state lacking the requisite political settlement cannot enforce Amsden-style performance benchmarks whatever its formal institutional design (Khan 2010). The contemporary restatements of the tradition accept industrial policy as legitimate but recast its objective as capability development and structural transformation under uncertainty rather than market-failure correction (Cherif and Hasanov 2019; Chang and Andreoni 2020; Andreoni and Chang 2019). A manuscript that treats the four mechanisms as interchangeable, or that ports the Northeast Asian model to a different political settlement without argument, forfeits the engagement a referee will demand.

## What Persistence Research Requires: Conceding Breadth, Contesting Determinism
The institutional-persistence literature is strong enough that a manuscript must concede its central finding and precise enough that it must engage its methodological disputes. The canonical chain runs from settler mortality through settlement patterns to extractive institutions and current income, identified through a cross-country instrument on a base sample of 64 former colonies (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson 2001), reinforced by the reversal-of-fortune evidence that colonisers imposed extraction where pre-colonial prosperity made it viable (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson 2002). Distinct mechanisms operate through initial inequality (Sokoloff and Engerman 2000), through land tenure identified by spatial regression discontinuity at the boundary of Peru's mining mita (Dell 2010), and through the erosion of interpersonal trust generated by the slave trades (Nunn 2008; Nunn and Wantchekon 2011).

A manuscript relying on this literature must survive two methodological challenges or it will not clear review. The first is the Albouy critique, which showed that 36 of the 64 countries in the settler-mortality sample were assigned rates from other countries, weakening the first stage when corrected (Albouy 2012), a challenge the original authors contested in reply (Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson 2012). The second is more general: persistence regressions exhibit severe spatial autocorrelation that inflates conventional significance, so that few results survive once the Moran statistic and spatial corrections are applied (Kelly 2019; Conley and Kelly 2025). The defensible posture is to concede that institutions matter broadly, to lean on the convergent evidence from Dell and Banerjee and Iyer (2005) rather than the cross-country instrument alone, and to contest determinism, since the persistence effect is itself shaped by colonial responses to pre-colonial order and by post-independence policy (Bauer, Platas and Weinstein 2022; Roessler et al. 2022). Crucially, the manuscript must contest the reduction of technology-absorption capacity to aggregate institutional quality, because capability accumulation is a distinct investment process not captured by expropriation-risk indices (Bell and Pavitt 1993; Kim 1997).

## What the Middle-Income Trap Requires: Separating a Trap From a Statistical Artifact
The middle-income trap is defined inconsistently across the literature, and a manuscript must both declare its definition and show robustness across the alternatives. The term entered the debate as a policy characterisation without a precise definition (Gill and Kharas 2007), an absence the authors later acknowledged (Gill and Kharas 2015). The first systematic operationalisation set a growth-slowdown threshold of prior seven-year growth of at least 3.5 per cent, a decline of at least 2 percentage points, and per-capita GDP above $10,000 in 2005 purchasing-power parity, and found that total-factor-productivity decline explains about 85 per cent of the slowdown (Eichengreen, Park and Shin 2012; Eichengreen, Park and Shin 2014). A duration-based definition classifies a country as trapped after 28 years in the lower-middle band or 14 in the upper, placing 35 of 52 middle-income countries in the trap (Felipe, Abdon and Kumar 2012), while a formal model derives the trap from complementarities among innovation, skill allocation and advanced infrastructure (Agénor and Canuto 2015). The current authoritative reference finds the median middle-income country stuck below 10 per cent of the United States level since 1970, with only 34 economies reaching high income since 1990 (World Bank 2024).

The decisive requirement is the separation of a structural trap from regression to the mean. Growth rates are weakly persistent, with cross-decade correlations of only 0.2 to 0.3, so apparent traps may be nothing more than fast growth reverting to the global mean (Pritchett and Summers 2014). The synthesis position rejects any unconditional trap but finds that, conditional on working-age population, macroeconomic stability and financial development, middle-income economies can indeed stall (Han and Wei 2017). A manuscript claiming that technology-transfer constraints generate a convergence ceiling must therefore show that slowdowns are more severe at middle income than mean reversion predicts, that the constraint binds specifically at that level rather than uniformly, and that the total-factor-productivity channel connects to technology rather than to some rival determinant.

## What the Technology-Transfer Literature Requires: Taking a Side in the Maskus-Lall Debate
The intellectual-property strand is organised around a disagreement with opposing empirical predictions, and a manuscript that presents only one side will read as naive. On one account, stronger protection attracts foreign direct investment and raises the volume of technology transferred, with royalty payments to affiliates rising more than 30 per cent among patent-intensive firms after reform (Maskus 2000; Branstetter, Fisman and Foley 2006). A separate vintage argument, that weak protection deters transfer of the most advanced technology, originates not with Maskus but with survey evidence showing that firms' concern about protection rises with the sophistication of the activity, approaching universality for research facilities in chemicals and pharmaceuticals (Mansfield 1994; Lee and Mansfield 1996). On the opposing account, protection without prior capability yields no benefit, because a firm below the capability threshold cannot use patented knowledge whatever its accessibility, and the historical industrialisers all relied on weak regimes during catch-up (Lall 1992; Lall 2003).

The recent literature resolves the dispute conditionally, and the manuscript should adopt that resolution. The optimal level of protection is U-shaped in income, low during absorption and rising as domestic innovation grows (Chen and Puttitanun 2005), an effect confirmed as strongly non-linear and development-stage-dependent (Kim et al. 2012; Khouilla and Bastidon 2024). The foundational distinction underneath the whole debate is that production capacity and technological capability are different resources acquired by different means, so that transfer may raise the former without touching the latter (Bell and Pavitt 1993). Any empirical claim about intellectual-property constraints must instrument for regime strength, control for absorptive capacity, and acknowledge that the standard index measures statute rather than enforcement (Ginarte and Park 1997).

## Five Claims That Will Draw Reviewer Objections
The manuscript will face five predictable objections, each avoidable. First, any claim about foreign-investment technology spillovers must control for absorptive capacity and specify the channel, since robust evidence exists only for backward linkages and shared-ownership ventures, not for horizontal, wholly-owned investment (Görg and Greenaway 2004; Javorcik 2004). Second, any persistence claim resting on the settler-mortality instrument must engage Albouy and report spatial-error diagnostics (Albouy 2012; Kelly 2019). Third, any use of the middle-income trap must distinguish a structural trap from regression to the mean (Pritchett and Summers 2014; Han and Wei 2017). Fourth, any claim for industrial-policy effectiveness must answer the picking-winners critique, to which the disciplined-subsidy and political-settlement arguments are the canonical replies (Pack and Saggi 2006; Noland and Pack 2003; Khan 2013). Fifth, any argument about patent regimes must engage both sides of the Maskus-Lall disagreement and locate its cases on the capability spectrum (Maskus 2000; Lall 1992; Chen and Puttitanun 2005).

## The Strongest Counterargument
The most serious objection is that the synthesis is unnecessary, that the four literatures can simply be cited side by side as complementary context, and that demanding a single connecting mechanism imposes a coherence the genre does not require. On this view, a *World Development* article can document a developmental state's technology policy, note that the country carries colonial institutional legacies, observe that it has stalled at middle income, and attribute the stall to technology-transfer constraints, without owing anyone a formal account of how the four link. Many published papers do precisely this and clear review.

The objection has real force for descriptive or case-study work, but it fails for a manuscript that makes a causal claim connecting persistence to a convergence ceiling, and that is the claim at issue here. Institutional persistence predicts levels: extractive institutions produce persistently low income. The middle-income trap describes a dynamic: rapid growth that decelerates at a particular income band. If persistent institutions were the binding constraint, the stall should occur at low income, not at $8,000 to $15,000, so the manuscript needs a mechanism explaining why an institutional configuration adequate for factor-accumulation-led catch-up becomes inadequate exactly when growth must shift to innovation-intensive production. The capabilities literature supplies the candidate, that colonial legacies differentially starve the learning organisations required for technology absorption while leaving production capacity intact, and the joint requirement of structural change and technological catch-up gives it empirical shape (Bell and Pavitt 1993; Lavopa and Szirmai 2018). Without that mechanism the four clusters remain adjacent rather than connected, and the paper reduces to a literature review. *The thesis fails if a manuscript can be shown to connect institutional persistence to a middle-income convergence ceiling with a mechanism that predicts a stall specifically at middle income, without recourse to a capability-accumulation channel or any functional equivalent.*

## Conclusion
The four literatures a developmental-state-and-technology manuscript must engage are each defensible on their own terms, and the positioning requirements within each cluster are well specified: name the acquisition mechanism, concede the breadth of persistence while contesting its determinism, separate a structural trap from mean reversion, and take a side in the intellectual-property debate. What the manuscript cannot borrow ready-made is the connection between historical institutional persistence and a growth stall located at middle income, because neither the persistence literature nor the middle-income-trap literature contains it. Supplying that mechanism, most plausibly through the distinction between production capacity and technological capability, is not a peripheral refinement but the manuscript's central contribution and its principal point of vulnerability at review.
