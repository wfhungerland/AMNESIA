# The East Asian miracle was built by keeping foreign money out

## Japan and South Korea restricted foreign investment on purpose, to get the technology without the owners. Today's rules make that trick almost impossible

Around 1960 an official at Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry delivered a message to IBM that no modern investment treaty would permit. According to the political scientist Stephen Krasner, the ministry warned the American giant that it would "take every measure possible to obstruct" its business in Japan unless it licensed its patents to Japanese firms and charged them no more than 5%. IBM gave in. It accepted the ministry's "administrative guidance", handed its basic computing patents to more than a dozen Japanese competitors, and in return was allowed to make machines in Japan at all. The president of the American Chamber of Commerce in Japan later called the episode the largest "white sale" on technology in history.

That is not how development is supposed to work. The modern consensus, preached by the World Bank, codified by the OECD and enforced through investment treaties, holds that foreign direct investment is among the surest routes out of poverty, and that restricting it is a self-harm to be scored against you. Yet the two cleanest catch-ups of the 20th century, Japan and South Korea, did the opposite. They kept foreign capital out, deliberately and for decades, precisely so that foreign technology would arrive in a form they could absorb. The miracle economies did not open their doors to the future. They made it knock, took the blueprints and kept the keys.

## Unbundling the package

To see why this was clever rather than merely nationalist, start with what foreign direct investment actually is. It is a bundle: capital, technology, management and, crucially, control. A multinational that builds a plant in a poor country brings its know-how, but it keeps ownership of the know-how, and it decides what the local operation is allowed to learn. For a government that wants its own firms to climb the technology ladder rather than assemble other people's designs forever, that bundle contains one ingredient it covets, the technology, and one it fears, the foreign control that can cap how much local firms ever learn.

Japan and Korea set out to prise the two apart. Rather than let foreigners own the factories, they forced them to sell the blueprints. Japan legislated capital controls in 1950 and did not eliminate them in law until 1980; over those 30 years, as the economist Mark Mason has documented, no other industrialised country denied foreign investors direct access to its market so adamantly. In place of ownership, the ministry engineered licences. It restricted competitive bidding so that Japanese buyers did not drive up the price of foreign technology against each other; it negotiated with licensors on industry's behalf; and it handed domestic firms a tax credit of 10-15% on their foreign-licence payments. Foreign companies that wanted the Japanese market were pushed to export patents in lieu of goods. Coca-Cola, Texas Instruments and the big American carmakers all spent years pressing for the access that IBM had been squeezed into buying with its patents.

Korea ran the same logic on a poorer budget. In the 1950s inward investment was barely legal and the economy leaned on American aid; when growth took off in the 1960s the government, as a 2013 OECD review puts it, favoured foreign loans and technology licensing over letting foreigners in. The numbers are stark. Foreign direct investment into Korea over 1962-72 came to just $223m, and across the two decades to 1982 it made up only 3.9% of the $9bn of long-term foreign capital the country took in, against the 10-20% typical of developing economies at the time. Korea borrowed instead, bought turn-key plants and capital goods with the proceeds, and then, in the words of its own science-policy institute, reverse-engineered the imported machinery to acquire the technology inside it. A 50% ceiling on foreign equity kept ownership local; foreign majority stakes remained effectively banned outside a few export zones; technology-transfer requirements stayed on the books until 1989. The state wanted the recipe, not the chef.

## Why it is not a growth tonic

The reflex objection is that this is just protectionism with a prettier name, and that most countries which slammed the door on foreign capital stayed poor. That is true, and it is the heart of the matter. Restriction was not the cause of the miracle; it was a bet that paid off only because two demanding conditions held. The state had to be capable enough to force the unbundling, negotiating licences, screening investors, resisting the multinationals' counter-pressure, rather than merely banning things. And a domestic industry had to exist that could actually absorb what it licensed, reverse-engineer the turn-key plant and improve on it, rather than let the blueprints gather dust. Where both held, as in Seoul and Tokyo, closure bought time for local firms to learn without a foreign owner deciding how much they were allowed to know. Where they did not, closure bought nothing but scarcity.

The strategy also carried a bill. Korea's preference for debt over equity, its way of taking foreign capital without foreign owners, loaded the economy with the short-term borrowing that detonated in the financial crisis of 1997. Japan's wall against foreign ownership hardened, as it fell, into a thicket of informal barriers and cosseted firms that dulled competition for a generation. Restricting investment is not free, and it is not a policy anyone should copy blind.

Grant all that, and the awkward fact remains. The fastest technological catch-ups on record were achieved not by welcoming foreign direct investment but by rationing it, and by insisting that technology arrive detached from control. Most economists still reckon, rightly, that FDI is good for a great many things, from jobs to management practice to plugging into supply chains. What the East Asian record contests is narrower and sharper: that openness is the best route to the specific goal of building indigenous technological capability. On that question the miracle economies are the inconvenient exception the textbook tends to skip.

## The door that is being bricked up

Here is where the history bites the present. The same institutions that now document the miracle have built a rule-set that grades its method as a defect. The OECD's FDI Regulatory Restrictiveness Index, the standard yardstick since it was extended back to 1997, scores every statutory limit on foreign ownership from zero, meaning open, to one, meaning closed, so that the joint-venture requirements and equity caps Japan and Korea used to force unbundling register purely as marks against a country. When Korea joined the OECD in 1996 it took on obligations to dismantle exactly the controls that had served it; it had, by then, already scrapped the 50% equity ceiling and abolished its technology-transfer requirements. The ladder was pulled up behind it, in part by its own hand.

China is the cautionary sequel. It ran the classic play, joint-venture requirements, equity caps and technology transfer demanded as the price of market access, and it did so late enough to be minutely scored and, unlike Japan or Korea, punished for it: forced technology transfer became a central charge in the trade war of the late 2010s, and its state technology drive, from the 863 programme to the $47.5bn semiconductor fund it launched in 2024, is now treated less as development than as threat. Whatever one makes of China's conduct, the structural point stands. The move that built the miracle, buying the blueprint while refusing the owner, is precisely the move that the modern order, from investment treaties to the World Trade Organisation's rules on trade-related investment measures, was designed to forbid.

None of this is an argument for closure. It is an argument against a reflex. Openness to foreign capital is treated as a costless virtue and its restriction as a costly vice, and the indices that rank countries encode that reflex to two decimal places. But the economies that most successfully turned foreign technology into their own did the opposite of what the ranking rewards, and they did it on purpose. A yardstick that cannot tell strategic unbundling from mere autarky is not measuring development. It is measuring compliance.

The miracle economies understood something the current rules make hard to act on: that a poor country may learn more from a licence it is forced to grant than from a factory it is invited to host. They wanted the recipe, not the chef. Today's order insists you hire the chef, thank him, and promise not to watch too closely while he cooks.
