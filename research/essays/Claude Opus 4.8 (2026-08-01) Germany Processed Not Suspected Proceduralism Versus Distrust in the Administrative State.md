# Processed, not suspected

## Germans feel their state treats them as cheats. The truth is stranger: it treats them as paperwork

A newcomer to Berlin learns the ritual fast. Within two weeks of moving in, the law requires you to register your address at the local Bürgeramt, produce a signed confirmation from your landlord, and walk away with a stamped slip, the Anmeldebescheinigung, without which you cannot open a bank account, start a job or claim a benefit. The appointment can take weeks to get. The clerk is usually correct, occasionally kind, and wholly uninterested in whether you find the process reasonable. To many who go through it, the message feels unmistakable: the German state assumes you are a problem to be logged.

That feeling is widespread, and it has hardened into a thesis. Germany, the story goes, is a country that treats its citizens as objects of supervision rather than as adults—people to be documented, licensed and corrected rather than trusted. The complaint is heard from entrepreneurs, foreign residents and Germans themselves. It is also half right, in a way that gets the important half wrong. Germany is indeed an outlier among rich democracies, but on procedure, not on suspicion. Its state buries people in forms; it does not, by the measurable evidence, believe they are cheats. The country treats its citizens as paperwork, not as suspects—and the two are easily confused.

Start with the part that is true. Ask Germans what they make of their dealings with officialdom and the answer is grim. In 2023 just 51% of recent users said they were satisfied with the administrative services they had used, against an average of 66% across the OECD, a club of mostly rich countries. That put Germany near the bottom of the Western field, miles below Finland (83%), Switzerland (81%) and the Netherlands (78%), and level with France. A newer wave nudged the German figure to 53%—still below par. Whatever the German state is doing, its users do not enjoy it.

Nor is the grievance merely a mood. The machinery really has grown heavier. According to a bureaucracy index maintained at the ESMT business school in Berlin, the volume of federal law swelled by about 60% between 2010 and 2024, from 24,775 standardised pages to 39,536. The National Regulatory Control Council, an independent watchdog, puts the direct cost of complying with German red tape at €65bn ($70bn) a year. The ifo Institute, a Munich think-tank, reckons the full drag, direct and indirect, could reach €146bn ($158bn) in lost output annually—a figure commissioned by a chamber of commerce, and so a ceiling rather than a consensus, but a startling one all the same. Digital government, which might spare citizens the queue, lags the leaders: Germany reached the United Nations' top "very high" tier only in 2024, well behind Denmark, the world's top-ranked e-government for the fourth survey running. The ifo Institute estimates that merely matching Danish digitalisation would lift German output by €96bn ($104bn) a year.

Entry into trades is gated, too. Under the Handwerksordnung, self-employment in the skilled crafts was long reserved for certified masters. A reform on January 1st 2004 cut the number of licensed trades from 94 to 41, yet the OECD still finds the administrative burden of starting a firm significantly heavier in Germany than in the average member country, and the country's Monopolkommission has repeatedly attacked the residual Meisterzwang as a barrier to competition. On procedure, in short, the indictment sticks.

### The twist

Here the story is supposed to continue in a straight line: heavy procedure, therefore a distrustful, controlling state. It does not. On every measure of whether the German state actually distrusts its citizens—rather than merely burdening them—the needle swings the other way.

Take paternalism, the itch to protect people from themselves. If Germany treated its citizens as feckless, it should regulate their vices tightly. In fact it is the *least* restrictive of 29 European countries on lifestyle rules, according to the Nanny State Index, compiled by the free-market Institute of Economic Affairs. No sugar tax, no nanny-ish food laws, comparatively light rules on tobacco, alcohol and vaping: on what its citizens put in their bodies, the German state is unusually content to let them decide.

Take trust between citizens, the soil in which high-functioning states grow. A low-trust society this is not. The share of Germans who say most people can be trusted jumped 13 percentage points between 2020 and 2025, to above 70%, according to the Pew Research Center—comfortably above America (55%) and far above France, if still shy of the Nordics. Trust in institutions follows the pattern: Germans trust the civil service markedly more than the government of the day, drawing a sharp line between a fairly respected administration and the politicians on top of it.

Even under maximum strain the picture holds. A 2021 study in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* by Katrin Schmelz, a behavioural economist, found that German compliance with covid-19 rules rested heavily on voluntary cooperation, and that enforcement could actually crowd out that goodwill where trust in government was thin. More striking still, older east Germans, raised under a state that genuinely policed them, proved *less* averse to control than west Germans, not more. A population that obeys because it chooses to, and trusts its neighbours at rising rates, is not one the state needs to treat as a den of rule-breakers—and, the evidence suggests, does not.

### Why the forms are not an accusation

How can a state be so procedurally heavy yet so substantively liberal? The answer lies in what the paperwork was built to do. Germany's administrative culture is the residue of three inheritances, none of which was designed to catch cheats.

The first is the Prussian bureaucratic state, whose top-down spirit the Weimar jurist Hugo Preuß later christened the Obrigkeitsstaat, a polity "where power flowed from the top down, and ordinary citizens were expected to obey rather than participate". The second is the 19th-century Rechtsstaat, the law-bound state, pushed forward by the liberal Robert von Mohl and hardened around 1900 into a formal legal positivism. Its temper is caught in the dictum of Otto Mayer, the father of German administrative law, in 1924: "Verfassungsrecht vergeht, Verwaltungsrecht besteht"—constitutional law passes, administrative law endures. The third is the welfare state of Otto von Bismarck, whose insurance laws for sickness (1883), accidents (1884) and old age (1889) were the world's first, and which required the state to register and verify the entire workforce, checking employers' records against insurance rolls and fining those who left workers uncovered. Frederic Howe, an American reformer dazzled and unnerved by all this in 1915, wrote that in Germany "the individual exists for the state, not the state for the individual", the state keeping "its finger on the pulse of the worker from the cradle to the grave".

That lineage explains the documentation. What it also explains, and what the grievance misses, is the purpose. The Rechtsstaat exists, in the words of one standard survey of German public administration, to protect "human dignity and individual freedom" and to prevent "arbitrary state action". Its most telling doctrine is Vertrauensschutz, the protection of legitimate expectations: grounded in the rule-of-law principle of Article 20 of the Basic Law and codified in the administrative-procedure act, it generally forbids the state from retroactively harming a citizen who relied in good faith on an official decision. The German state documents you so exhaustively in part because it has bound its own hands: the file that logs you also protects you from the official who might otherwise act on a whim. The paperwork is a leash on power as much as a ledger of people.

To be sure, the sceptic has a comeback, and a good one. Dense permission-in-advance, the argument runs, *is* a form of distrust, whatever its pedigree. A state that trusted its citizens would let them declare and act, then check later on a risk basis, rather than making everyone prove innocence at the counter first. On this view the Anmeldung and the master's certificate are institutionalised suspicion, and the satisfaction gap is simply citizens registering that they are treated as guilty until they file. The objection rightly identifies the crux: the choice between checking beforehand and enforcing afterwards.

But it does not survive the comparison it invites. If ex-ante procedure were distrust, it ought to travel with the other marks of distrust—lifestyle paternalism, low social trust, coercion-dependent compliance. In Germany it conspicuously does not: the same state that demands the registration slip leaves drinking, smoking and diet largely alone, enjoys rising interpersonal trust and secures obedience by consent. Much of the machinery, moreover, is Continental rather than uniquely German—France records an almost identical 52% satisfaction score—and its rationale is protective. Proceduralism and distrust can be pulled apart, and in Germany they come apart cleanly.

The practical upshot is that Germany's reformers have been aiming at the wrong target when they frame the problem as culture. The country does not suffer from a state that thinks too little of its citizens. It suffers from a state that makes them prove too much, too slowly, on too much paper, when Denmark has shown the same things can be done online in minutes. The fix is not a change of heart. It is a change of interface.

Germany does not need to learn to trust its citizens. It needs to stop making them queue to be believed.
