# The rage of the overtaken

## Powers that lose the technological lead follow the same script for 150 years, and always reach for it too late

In 1896 a young English journalist named Ernest Edwin Williams published a slim book that ran through six editions in a year. Its title, "Made in Germany", was meant as a warning, not a boast. Germany, Williams wrote, was copying British goods so closely as "to deceive the purchaser", selling them abroad "beneath the cost of production", and schooling its chemists and engineers while Britain dozed. "A gigantic commercial State is arising to menace our prosperity", he concluded, "and contend with us for the trade of the world."

Swap the country and the century and the pamphlet writes itself again. In September 2023 the analysis firm TechInsights pulled apart a Huawei smartphone and found, to Washington's alarm, a 7-nanometre processor built inside China by SMIC using ordinary deep-ultraviolet machines, a feat American sanctions were designed to prevent. The language that greeted it, theft, dumping, a rigged system, a threat to national security, would have been intelligible to Williams. So would the timing. It came after the fact.

Four times over the past 150 years a leading industrial power has watched a challenger catch and pass it: Britain overtaken by Germany before 1914, America by Japan in the 1980s, America and Japan by South Korea in the 1990s, and America and the European Union by China today. The cases differ in almost everything, in ideology, in the technologies at stake, in the machinery of trade policy. Yet the response barely changes. Incumbents recognise the threat late, describe it through the same escalating vocabulary, and answer with policy that arrives only once the lead is already gone. The interesting exception, America's attempt to strangle China's chip industry before it reaches the frontier, is the first genuine departure in a century and a half, and it merely proves how alien pre-emption is to the breed.

### The Victorian template

Britain's decline relative to Germany was broad and, in hindsight, plainly measurable. German steel output passed Britain's in 1893 and by 1914 was more than double it, according to figures collected by Encyclopaedia Britannica. In synthetic dyes, an industry a British chemist had founded when William Perkin stumbled on mauveine in 1856, Germany held roughly 85% of world production by 1913, on the reckoning of Johann Peter Murmann, a historian of the trade. Britain had invented the field and then surrendered it.

The rhetoric duly climbed Williams's ladder, from imitation to dumping to a critique of the whole German system of state-backed technical education. By 1903 Joseph Chamberlain had reached the top rung, casting tariff protection against German and American goods as a matter of imperial security. And then Britain did almost nothing. The Merchandise Marks Act of 1887 required imports to carry a country-of-origin label, which, as Williams tartly noted, merely advertised German wares. Chamberlain's tariff campaign, the one ambitious answer, was thrown out by voters in the general election of January 1906. Britain imposed no general protective tariff until 1932, four decades after the steel it once dominated had slipped away. Rhetoric trailed displacement by about three years; effective trade policy never came at all. This is the pattern in its slowest, feeblest form.

### America learns to say "national security"

America's answer to Japan ran the same course at higher speed, and added a weapon Britain never reached for. In 1980 Japan became the world's largest carmaker, building about 7m passenger cars to America's 6.4m; all four American car firms lost money that year. In chips, Japanese firms held some 92% of American sales of the 256K memory chip by 1985, passed America in overall semiconductor share by 1987, and by 1989 supplied the three biggest chipmakers on earth.

On cars the response was swift. A "voluntary" export restraint capping Japanese shipments at 1.68m vehicles a year was announced on May 1st 1981, hammered out between the American trade representative, William Brock, and Japan's trade minister, Rokusuke Tanaka. On chips it came in layers: an industry dumping petition in 1985, a semiconductor pact on September 2nd 1986, and, when Tokyo dragged its feet, 100% tariffs on $300m of Japanese electronics on April 17th 1987. The decisive move, though, was rhetorical. A Defence Science Board panel chaired by Norman Augustine reported in February 1987 that America's dependence on foreign chips was among the gravest matters it had ever examined. Recast as a security problem rather than a trade dispute, the issue justified what trade complaints could not: an industrial-policy consortium, SEMATECH, which drew roughly $870m of federal money by 1996. Mr Brock could restrain imports; only the Pentagon's imprimatur could underwrite a subsidy.

### Politics, not scale, sets the clock

South Korea's rise shows what really governs the speed of the response, and it is not the size of the threat. Samsung built its first 64K memory chip in November 1983, on a design licensed from America's Micron; by 1992, the year it made the world's first 64-megabit chip, it was the largest memory-chip maker on the planet, a title it held until early 2025. Hyundai's Excel reached American showrooms on February 20th 1986 at $4,995 and sold 168,882 cars in its first year, a record for an importer.

Where a powerful American firm felt the heat, Washington moved fast: a trade investigation into Korean patent and copyright law opened on November 4th 1985, and a Micron dumping complaint in 1992 produced duties within a year. Where the wounded incumbent was a fading Japanese industry, the response crawled. South Korea overtook Japan in shipbuilding around 2000, yet Tokyo did not lodge a formal complaint at the World Trade Organisation until November 2018, with a second in January 2020. The same displacement drew a one-year answer in chips and an 18-year answer in ships. What differs is not the scale of the loss but the political muscle of the industry doing the losing.

### China, and the first flicker of pre-emption

China sets off every alarm at once. It became the world's largest manufacturer in 2010, on roughly 19.8% of global output to America's 19.4%. It now makes more than 80% of the world's solar panels at every stage of production, according to the International Energy Agency. BYD passed Tesla in battery-electric car sales in the last quarter of 2023, by 526,409 to 484,507. And with that SMIC chip it showed it could inch towards the frontier despite an export blockade.

The vocabulary has run the full ladder, faster and louder than ever. Keith Alexander, then head of the National Security Agency, called Chinese cyber-espionage "the greatest transfer of wealth in history" on July 9th 2012. The American trade representative found in March 2018 that Chinese practices cost at least $50bn a year, and tariffs followed from that July. The European Commission branded China a "systemic rival" on March 12th 2019, a phrase it had never before aimed at a partner. Robert Lighthizer told Congress in May 2023 that the FBI opens a fresh China-related investigation "every ten hours".

Yet here the pattern finally bends. Most of the response was, as ever, reactive: the tariffs trailed the manufacturing crossover by eight years; Huawei went on a trade blacklist in May 2019; the CHIPS act of August 2022 threw $52.7bn at domestic chipmaking; the EU slapped duties of up to 35.3% on Chinese electric cars in October 2024. But the export controls of October 7th 2022 were different in kind. They aimed to freeze China's chip industry short of the leading edge, to deny a capability it had not yet acquired. Jake Sullivan, then national security adviser, had spelled out the logic weeks earlier, on September 16th 2022: America would no longer settle for staying a couple of generations ahead but must keep "as large a lead as possible". For the first time an incumbent tried to strike before it fell behind.

### The same four rungs, every time

Strip away the particulars and the rhetoric is astonishingly uniform. It climbs four rungs in order. First comes the charge of cheating: German counterfeits, Japanese reverse-engineering, Chinese theft. Then dumping and unfair pricing, a complaint filed in every case without exception. Then a critique of the challenger's whole model, from the Kaiser's technical schools to "Japan Inc." to the Korean chaebol to Chinese state capitalism. And finally the security frame, which alone unlocks the heaviest tools. It built SEMATECH in 1987 and the chip blockade in 2022; it failed only for Chamberlain, whose security case for tariffs the voters rejected. That the same ladder appears under a Victorian free-trade parliament, a cold-war Congress and a Brussels commission suggests it reflects something about how incumbents think, not about the politics of any one of them.

The timing tells the second half of the story. The lag from being overtaken to acting has collapsed by an order of magnitude, from a decade or more in Britain's case to a matter of weeks in China's. When BYD passed Tesla, a 100% American tariff on Chinese electric cars followed within about six months. When the SMIC chip surfaced, tighter export rules came within weeks. But faster is not earlier. The sequence, notice, denounce, act, is unchanged; what has shrunk is the delay between its steps, thanks to a machinery of trade investigations, entity lists and export controls that each episode has bequeathed to the next. Institutions built to punish Japan were turned on Korea, then refined against China.

To be sure, the export controls look like a clean refutation. If America can now act to stop displacement before it happens, the claim that incumbents are doomed to react is a description of the past, not a law. The objection has teeth. But even that pre-emptive strike was set off by mounting evidence of Chinese progress, and was followed within weeks by a thoroughly reactive tightening once the SMIC chip appeared. One anticipatory instrument, in one industry, wrapped inside a campaign of tariffs and blacklists that trailed Chinese success by years, marks the edge of the pattern rather than its collapse. The reactive rule holds; pre-emption remains the rare frontier case, the thing that happens only when the lag between overtaking and answer has shrunk almost to nothing.

Williams would recognise all of it: the wounded pride, the four-rung ladder, the policy that arrives once the race is lost. What he would not recognise is a power trying to win the next race before the starting gun. Whether that instinct spreads, or stays a one-off born of panic over a single chip, is the question the next challenger will settle. On the evidence of 150 years, do not bet on foresight.
