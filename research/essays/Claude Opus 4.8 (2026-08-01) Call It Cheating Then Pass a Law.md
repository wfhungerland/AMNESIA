# Call it cheating, then pass a law

## Every time a rising economy copies its way to the frontier, the incumbent runs the same script. China is the latest target, not the first

In 1887 Britain's parliament passed the Merchandise Marks Act, which forced imported goods to declare where they were made. The point was to shame. British manufacturers were tired of losing custom to cheaper German wares, and a country-of-origin stamp, they reckoned, would warn shoppers off the foreign stuff. It did the opposite. "Made in Germany" became a mark of quality that German exporters wore with pride. The law meant to fence out the challenger ended up advertising it.

That episode is usually filed under Victorian trade history. It deserves a wider billing, because the same drama has since played out three more times, with different casts and the same plot. When a rising economy catches up by copying, the incumbent reliably reaches for one script: call the copying imitation, then call the imitation cheating, then pass a law. Britain did it to Germany, America did it to Japan, Japan's firms did a quieter version to South Korea, and America and the European Union are doing it now to China. The charge of "unfair competition" that fills think-tank papers and ministerial speeches today is not a response to a unique emergency. It is a recurring reflex of catch-up rivalry, dressed up each time as principle.

The odd thing is how little the reflex has been measured. Economic historians have chronicled each episode in loving narrative detail, but almost no one has coded the pattern itself, its timing, its sequence, its frequency, across the cases. The result is that a predictable phase of industrial catch-up keeps being mistaken for a fresh moral crisis.

## The Victorian template

Start with the original. By the 1880s German firms were absorbing British techniques wholesale, sometimes under licence and often not; the spread of British iron-making methods to the continent, traced by Rainer Fremdling, is one well-documented channel. British opinion recast this borrowing as theft of a birthright. Ernest Edwin Williams gave the mood its bible in 1896 with "Made in Germany", a polemic that portrayed German goods as shoddy knock-offs muscling into British markets. The Merchandise Marks Act was the legislative answer.

It flopped as protection. In a 2021 study for the London School of Economics, Oliver Harvey analysed British trade by country and commodity after the Act and found no sign that it halted the advance of German manufactures; instead it damaged Britain's own entrepot trade and shipping. Mr Harvey classes it as an early non-tariff barrier, soft protectionism sold as consumer information. The deeper rivalry was real enough. Yet even here the numbers point sideways: Stephen Broadberry's sectoral work shows that when Germany and America overtook Britain, they did so mainly by shifting labour out of farming and by pulling ahead in services, not by beating British factories at their own game. The panic was about manufacturing; the overtaking was mostly elsewhere.

## America's turn to bash

Fast forward to the 1970s and swap the accents. Japan was now the copyist, and Washington the aggrieved incumbent. Chalmers Johnson's 1982 study of Japan's industry ministry lent intellectual weight to the perception that Japanese success rested on state-orchestrated absorption of foreign technology and a closed home market. Clyde Prestowitz, a trade negotiator, turned that perception into a bestseller in 1988 with "Trading Places", casting Japanese practice as reverse engineering behind a wall. Laura Tyson supplied the economists' version, marshalling market-share numbers to argue for managed trade in the industries where it hurt most.

Then came the law. The instrument was Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. The Semiconductor Industry Association filed its petition on June 14th 1985; a US-Japan semiconductor pact was signed on September 2nd 1986; and when Washington judged Tokyo's compliance wanting, President Ronald Reagan announced tariffs of up to 100% on Japanese goods on March 27th 1987. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 hard-wired the reflex into statute, creating the "Special 301" watch-list that names and shames foreign intellectual-property offenders to this day.

This dyad also produced the closest thing to a measurement. Thomas Bayard and Kimberly Elliott coded every Section 301 case concluded between 1975 and 1993, classing each as success or failure and regressing the outcomes against the target's dependence on American trade. It is a fine dataset. But notice what it counts: the enforcement actions, not the rhetoric that preceded them. The shift from "imitation" to "cheating" that justified the actions goes unmeasured.

## The exception that clarifies the rule

South Korea's rise against Japan in the 1980s and 1990s fits the first two beats and then breaks the third, which is worth dwelling on. Alice Amsden's 1989 account and Linsu Kim's later work document Korea's march from duplicative imitation to creative imitation to genuine innovation, firm by firm, in steel and electronics. The incumbent bristled. But the Japanese response was mostly private, not parliamentary: Japanese firms simply refused to license their best technology to Korean upstarts, who answered by reverse-engineering, by hiring away experienced engineers and by building their own research. Korea's formal tightening of patent law came later, and under American pressure, not Japanese.

So here the "pass a law" step is displaced. That looks like a hole in the pattern. It is better read as a clarification: when an incumbent can gatekeep through contract, it need not bother with a statute. The perception of imitation and the framing of it as illegitimate recur even when the legislative finale does not.

## The China round

Which brings us to the present, and the script's most elaborate staging. The perception is familiar: China's industrial rise is recast as intellectual-property theft and "forced technology transfer". The framing is contested in a way the earlier rounds were not. Some scholars, such as Edward Steinfeld, argued that China's plug into global supply chains yielded assembly muscle rather than deep know-how; others, including Lee Branstetter and Nicholas Lardy, documented real indigenous innovation from the mid-2000s. Legal scholars are openly split on whether the headline charge even describes something unlawful. Mark Wu warned in 2016 that China's state-capitalist structure outran the rules the World Trade Organisation was built on; Julia Ya Qin pointed out in 2019 that many of the practices denounced as forced transfer are, under existing trade law, legal; Alan Sykes argued in 2021 that much "forced" transfer is in economic terms a voluntary bargain, technology for market access.

The legislation, though, arrived with more force than ever. The office of the United States Trade Representative launched its Section 301 inquiry into China on August 18th 2017, published its findings on March 22nd 2018 and collected the first $34bn tranche of tariffs on July 6th 2018. The Export Control Reform Act became law on August 13th 2018, arming Washington to police "emerging and foundational" technologies. Robert Lighthizer, then the trade representative, framed it as defence against theft. His Victorian predecessors would have recognised the tune.

## A pattern nobody has counted

Step back and the shape is unmistakable: imitation perceived, unfair competition alleged, law enacted, in four rounds across 150 years. What is missing is arithmetic. The foundational studies, from Mr Johnson on Japan to Andrew Mertha on Chinese piracy, are narrative institutional histories, superb on motive and mechanism and silent on frequency and timing. The quantitative work, meanwhile, measures the scenery rather than the play. Petra Moser has shown, from the 17,000-odd exhibits at London's Great Exhibition of 1851, that 89% of British innovations were never patented, a lovely demolition of the idea that weak intellectual-property rules kill invention. Keun Lee and Chaisung Lim have measured Korea's catch-up through patent data. Mr Broadberry measured productivity, Mr Fremdling technology diffusion, Bayard and Elliott the enforcement cases. Not one of them codes the incumbent's rhetorical march from imitation-talk to cheating-talk to statute, on a common scale, across the dyads. The pattern is asserted everywhere and quantified nowhere.

That gap matters for more than tidiness. Ha-Joon Chang has argued that today's rich countries are "kicking away the ladder", denying latecomers the very tools, protection and selective copying, that they themselves used while climbing. The point sharpens when you remember that 19th-century America extended almost no protection to foreign inventors and authors, a fact documented by Zorina Khan. If the "unfair competition" charge were coded and timed across cases, its recurrence would be plain, and each fresh outrage would look less like a singular crime and more like a phase.

To be sure, the four episodes differ, and a sceptic can press the point hard. The instruments range from origin-marking to export controls; the responders range from parliaments to private firms; and sometimes the charge of unfair play is not rhetoric at all but an honest description of genuinely different behaviour. Grouping such unlike cases, the sceptic says, manufactures a pattern that is really just hindsight. The Korea round, with no incumbent statute, is offered as proof that the sequence frays.

The objection has teeth, and it marks the real limit: the third step is contingent, not automatic. But it does not dissolve the pattern; it locates it. The first two beats, seeing imitation and branding it as cheating, recur in every case, including Korea, whatever form the response then takes. The claim was never that each round ends in an identical law. It is that the ordered move from copying to accusation to institutional answer keeps recurring, and that we would see it clearly if anyone bothered to count. Until someone does, the burden of proof cuts against the idea that any given round is unprecedented.

The next time a minister denounces a rival's copying as an outrage without parallel, it is worth recalling the stamp that was supposed to disgrace the Germans. It sold their goods for them. The script is old; only the villain is recast.
