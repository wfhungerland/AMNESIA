# The statistics of catching up have a blind spot shaped like the West

## Modern indices can grade how permissively a country treats intellectual property. They start in 1960, which conveniently hides how the rich world really got rich

On August 23rd 1887 the British Parliament passed the Merchandise Marks Act, and with it accidentally coined a brand. The law forced imported goods to name their country of origin, so that German knives stamped "Sheffield" and German pianos passing as English could be exposed to the honest British shopper. The intended slur was the phrase "Made in Germany". Within a generation it had become a mark of quality, and the copyists of the 1880s were the engineers everyone else was copying. The label meant to shame German imitation instead advertised the moment German imitation turned into German excellence.

That story is now told as a charming irony. It is better read as data. The German catch-up of the 19th century ran, in part, on tolerated copying: a patent law of 1877 that refused to protect chemical products until the late 1960s, no unified patent regime at all before 1877, and a trading reputation built partly on other people's marks. The same is true of almost every economy that has ever caught up. And here is the awkward part: we can now measure this permissiveness, in tidy annual panels, for China, South Korea and post-war Japan; we cannot measure it for Germany, Britain or America, because the instruments that do the measuring only switch on in 1960. The numbers do not merely have a gap. The gap is shaped like the incumbents who wrote the rules.

## What the yardstick actually measures

Begin with the yardstick everyone reaches for. The standard cross-country gauge of intellectual-property regimes is the patent-rights index built by Walter Park, an economist at American University, and first published with Juan Ginarte in 1997. It scores the strength of a country's patent law from zero to five, and it has become the workhorse variable in hundreds of studies of trade, investment and growth. It is a fine index. It also does exactly one thing that matters here: it measures protection, not permission, and it begins in 1960.

Those two features together are the whole problem. Catch-up does not run on protection; it runs on its absence, on the freedom of domestic firms to copy, reverse-engineer, license cheaply and absorb the frontier before they can extend it. To turn a protection score into a permissiveness score, researchers simply invert it, a defensible trick from 1960 onwards but useless before it. And inversion quietly conflates two different things. A country with no product patents on chemicals is permissive in one way; a country whose patent law is strong on paper but unenforced is permissive in quite another. Read as a single number, "no protection" and "unenforced protection" look identical. They are not, and the distinction is precisely the mechanism through which technology gets absorbed.

For the recent catch-ups, this is a solvable problem, because the copying happened while modern statistics were switched on. Licensing flows show up directly in the World Bank's series on charges for the use of intellectual property, which tracks royalty payments country by country. Investment barriers, the lever Japan and Korea pulled hardest, are captured by the OECD's FDI Regulatory Restrictiveness Index, which scores statutory limits across 22 sectors for more than 100 countries, though only back to 1997. Governance context arrives via the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators, available from 1996. Stitch the inverted patent scores together with royalty intensity, technology-sector investment restrictiveness and a property-rights sub-index, and you have a genuine, regression-ready measure of how permissively Japan, South Korea and China behaved. The composite is buildable. It just cannot reach back past the point where the rich world had already arrived.

## Everybody did it; only some got filmed

The historical record, where it survives at all, suggests the incumbents were more permissive than their present-day sermons imply. Switzerland ran a world-class chemical and machinery industry with no patent protection until 1888, and no coverage of chemical inventions until 1907; the Netherlands abolished its patent law outright in 1869 and did without one for 43 years. The United States, now the loudest voice on intellectual-property theft, spent the whole 19th century as a piracy haven, legally reprinting British authors without payment because its copyright law protected only Americans. Charles Dickens toured the country in 1842 partly to complain about it, and got nowhere; Congress did not extend copyright to foreign authors until the Chace Act of March 3rd 1891, and even then only on condition that the books be typeset on American soil. Protection for me; free copying of you.

The pattern is not incidental to development; it is close to the method. This is the argument the economist Ha-Joon Chang made in "Kicking Away the Ladder" in 2002, and the quantitative-history literature has since filled in the mechanism. Petra Moser, now at New York University, showed using data from 19th-century world's fairs that countries without patent laws were no less inventive; they simply innovated in areas where secrecy, not patents, protected the reward. The permissiveness was a strategy, not a failing. What has changed is not the behaviour of late developers but the ability of everyone else to score it.

Consider how legible the recent cases are by comparison. Japan's Foreign Investment Law of 1950 deliberately throttled inward equity investment, not to keep foreigners out but to force them to license their technology rather than own the factories that used it; a 2005 study by Kozo Kiyota and Tetsuji Okazaki reconstructed, firm by firm, how the trade ministry screened licensing applications between 1957 and 1970. South Korea withheld product patents on pharmaceuticals and chemicals until 1987. China did the same until a patent-law amendment in 1992, ran its 863 high-technology programme on an initial commitment of 10bn yuan (about $3bn at the time) over 15 years from 1986, and in 2024 established a third state semiconductor fund worth $47.5bn. Every one of these levers leaves a paper trail in a modern database. Germany's equivalent manoeuvres left an imperial patent office that recorded 21,925 applications in 1900 and a legal text that quietly omitted chemical products; you can read the strategy, but you cannot put it in a regression next to China's.

## The blind spot is not neutral

None of this would matter if the missing data were randomly missing. They are not. The instruments encode a worldview in which protection is the norm and its absence is a deficiency to be marked against you. That worldview is enforced today through the very measures that cannot see the past: the World Bank's royalty series, the OECD's restrictiveness index, the compulsory-licensing tallies. And even those tallies are lopsided. The only systematic cross-country record of compulsory licensing, a genuine instrument of tolerated imitation, is a database assembled by Reed Beall and Randall Kuhn in 2012, and it covers 24 episodes in 17 countries between 1995 and 2011, all of them pharmaceutical. For electronics, chemicals or machinery, the sectors that actually built the Asian miracles, there is no equivalent series at all. The measurable slice of permissiveness is the slice that embarrasses developing countries over medicine prices, not the slice that explains their factories.

So the numbers do a subtle thing. They render China's copying to two decimal places while leaving the West's own copying as anecdote, folklore and the occasional charming story about a label. A cross-country study that runs its permissiveness variable from 1960 is not describing a universal law of development; it is describing the behaviour of the countries that arrived late enough to be filmed, judged against a standard set by the countries that arrived early enough to escape the camera. The morality tale writes itself, and the data quietly supply the moral.

To be sure, the sceptic has a real case. Much of the gap is archival bad luck, not conspiracy: 19th-century states simply did not collect balance-of-payments royalty data or score their own investment regimes, and no amount of ill intent is required to explain a missing series. And modern intellectual-property disputes are not obviously the same animal as Dickens being reprinted in Boston. Forced technology transfer as a condition of market access, state-directed acquisition and industrial-scale cyber-espionage differ in kind, not just degree, from a Victorian printer setting cheap type. The channel that made copying central to catch-up is real; its present-day weight, and whether today's methods cross a line the 19th century did not, is genuinely contested.

Grant all of that, and the core point survives. The problem is not that historians are careless but that the yardstick has a definition baked in: it counts the strength of protection and treats permission as protection's failure, so that the strategy which built Manchester, Zurich and the Ruhr registers, if it registers at all, as a low score. The one thing we can measure across the whole span, tariff levels and the plain text of patent law, points the same way Chang's argument does: the incumbents were more permissive on their way up than the rules they now police would allow. A measure that cannot tell "no patent" from "unenforced patent", and cannot see before 1960 at all, is not a neutral thermometer. It is a mirror that only faces one direction.

The practical fix is dull and worth doing: build the composite where the data allow it, from 1960, and hand-code the earlier cases from tariff schedules, patent statutes and capital-flow reconstructions rather than pretending a single inverted score can be stretched across the boundary. The intellectual fix is harder, because it requires the rich world to remember that the ladder it now guards so carefully was one it climbed by copying. Before you kick a ladder away, it helps to make sure nobody kept the receipts.
