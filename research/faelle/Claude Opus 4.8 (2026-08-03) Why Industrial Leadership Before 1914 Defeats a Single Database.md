# The ledgers of leadership

## Economic historians want one database that shows who led which industry before 1914. It does not exist, and the reason is more revealing than the gap

Suppose you want a single number: Germany's share of world exports of synthetic dyes in 1900. It is not an eccentric request. Dyes were the signature product of the second industrial revolution, the field in which German firms turned academic chemistry into commercial dominance, and the share is exactly the sort of figure a paper on technological leadership is built around. So you open the newest and largest historical trade database, type in the query, and get nothing at the level you need. The database knows what Germany traded with the world in total. It does not know what it sold in dyes.

That gap is not a flaw in one dataset. It is the defining feature of the entire evidence base for industrial leadership before the first world war. The striking thing about that evidence is not that it is missing. Most of it survives, in customs ledgers and in a handful of mid-twentieth-century books. What is missing is a join. No single source tells you who led a given manufacturing sector across the leading economies, and the reason has less to do with lost data than with the awkward fact that no two countries counted their goods the same way.

### A flood of data that stops at the sector

The past fifteen years have been a golden age for historical trade statistics. The Federico-Tena World Trade Historical Database, the standard reference, now covers 149 political units from 1800 to 1938, at both current and constant prices, free to download. Its own authors note that adding even a crude one-digit breakdown by sector would take a major research effort of its own. CEPII's TRADHIST assembles about 1.9m bilateral trade flows stretching back to 1827, quadrupling what was previously available before 1948. RICardo, built at Sciences Po, reconstructs some 300,000 bilateral flows across roughly 1,400 trading entities from the late eighteenth century onwards.

Every one of these is a genuine achievement, and every one is aggregate. They will tell you Britain's share of all world trade, or the flow of goods between France and Argentina, to a precision unimaginable a generation ago. They will not tell you who led chemicals, or machinery, or steel, because the commodity dimension simply is not there. For the question that matters to a historian of leadership, the richest databases in the field are silent.

### The one exception, and its catch

There is exactly one source that breaks trade down to the product level for a major economy before 1914, and it covers a single country. Wolf-Fabian Hungerland and Nikolaus Wolf have reconstructed the whole of German foreign trade, every product, every trading partner, year by year from 1880 to 1913, and reclassified it to the modern international standard at four- and five-digit detail. It is, on any measure, the most granular pre-war trade dataset in existence. It even repairs an old defect: Hamburg and Bremen sat outside the German customs area until 1888 and were missing from the official figures, a gap the new series fills.

The catch is in the phrase "single country". Nothing of the kind exists for Britain, France or the United States. A comparable dataset for Japan, built by Christopher Meissner and John Tang, covers 1880 to 1910. But for the western rivals against which German industry is usually measured, the product-level ledger has been reconstructed for one player and not the others. You can now describe German sectoral exports in fine detail; you still cannot line them up against British or American exports at the same resolution. A race needs at least two runners on the same track.

### Why books from the 1950s still win

Because no digital source spans sectors and countries at once, the backbone of cross-country sectoral history remains a small shelf of compilations put together between the 1940s and the 1960s. The indispensable one is a 1951 paper by Henryk Tyszynski in the Manchester School, written with the guidance of Arthur Lewis, later a Nobel laureate. It gives each country's share of world manufactured exports, split into iron and steel, machinery, transport equipment, textiles, chemicals and the rest, at benchmark years from 1899 to 1950. Alfred Maizels widened the picture in 1963 to cover both output and exports by sector through 1959; his volume is the most detailed of all and survives only in print. Folke Hilgerdt's 1945 study for the League of Nations reaches back to 1870.

These books endure not because nothing better has come along, but because of what their authors did that no digital project has since repeated: they forced incompatible national statistics into a single, consistent set of commodity groups. That is drudgery of a high order, and it is exactly the drudgery a modern researcher still cannot avoid. One warning is worth passing on. The world manufacturing shares often quoted for the late nineteenth century, Britain near a third, falling as America climbed to roughly a third and Germany to a sixth by 1913, come from Paul Bairoch's 1982 estimates, whose benchmark years are 1860, 1880, 1900 and 1913, and they measure output, not exports. They are frequently, and wrongly, attributed to Hilgerdt. Mixing the two is a common way to compare things that were never measured the same way.

### Three numbers that disagree

Part of the difficulty is that "market share" is not one thing but three, and they can point in different directions for the same country. An export share, the Tyszynski measure, captures competitiveness directly but is bent by trade policy, shipping costs and re-exports. A production share, the Bairoch and Hilgerdt measure, captures industrial muscle but says nothing about whether a country actually sold abroad: America was a giant producer that exported little before 1900, while Belgium was a small producer that exported heavily. A productivity level, the measure refined by Stephen Broadberry, gets closest to technological capability but yields no market share at all. A claim about who "led" an industry means little until it says which of the three it has in mind, because a country can top one ranking and trail on another.

### The real obstacle is the tariff book

Here is the crux. The binding constraint on a consistent sectoral series is not that the numbers were never recorded. It is that every state recorded them under its own tariff schedule, and nobody has reconciled those schedules at sector level. The Brussels classification of 1913 split trade into just four broad classes; the modern international standard did not arrive until the late 1940s. Hungerland and Wolf translated German data into that modern standard, but with no equivalent for Britain, France or America, comparing sector against sector across countries still means matching categories by hand.

Three further traps lie in wait. British headline figures flatter the country's competitiveness, because goods merely passing through its ports as re-exports inflate the totals; the United Nations later built its "retained imports" tables precisely to strip that out. Colonial trade drifts in and out of the world total depending on which dataset you use, a bigger problem for Britain and France than for a Germany with few colonies. And shares measured in current prices mislead wherever prices moved sharply, as steel prices did once the Bessemer and Thomas processes cut costs. None of these is fatal on its own. Together they mean that a number lifted from one source and set beside a number from another is often comparing apples with a different orchard.

### Assembling the picture

The way through is not a single source but a stack of three, each covering the others' blind spot. Start with Tyszynski and Maizels for the harmonised export shares at 1899 and 1913. Layer on the product-level national data, Hungerland and Wolf for Germany, Meissner and Tang for Japan, aggregated up to the same groups to fill the years between benchmarks. Then reach for the specialists where the broad categories blur the contest: Johann Peter Murmann on chemicals, where Germany made roughly 85% of the world's synthetic dyes by 1900; Robert Allen on steel, where German exports passed British around 1913 and Britain turned into a net importer; a 1997 study by Josef Reindl showing Germany held close to half of world electrical-engineering exports in 1913; and the cotton historians on Lancashire's slow defeat by Japan. Assembled with care, these yield a defensible series. Lifted carelessly from any one shelf, they yield a muddle.

To be sure, the digital revolution is a real leap, and the objection that it has overtaken all this deserves a hearing. For any country's slice of total world trade, the new databases now beat the old books outright; for Germany, the Hungerland-Wolf series is finer than anything Tyszynski could manage. But the objection breaks at the join. The digital databases are aggregate, so they cannot produce a sectoral share at all, and the one disaggregated source covers a single country, so it cannot produce a comparison. The instant the question becomes who led chemicals, or steel, across the four leading economies, the researcher is thrown back on the harmonised groupings only the mid-century compilers ever built. The tools have changed; the bottleneck has not.

Digitisation, in other words, has solved the easy half of the problem and left the hard half exactly where it was. The numbers for who made what, and sold it to whom, are mostly still there, waiting in the ledgers. What no one has yet rebuilt for Britain, France and America is the patient, unglamorous work of making them speak the same language. Until someone does, the field's most modern question will keep being answered out of its oldest books.
