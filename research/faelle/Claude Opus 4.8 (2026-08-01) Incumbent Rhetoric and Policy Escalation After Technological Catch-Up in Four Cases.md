# Incumbent Rhetoric and Policy Escalation After Technological Catch-Up in Four Cases

*This paper argues that incumbent powers respond to technological displacement through a consistent, reactive sequence in which rhetorical escalation follows the loss of market leadership and policy intervention follows rhetoric, a cycle that has compressed by roughly an order of magnitude over 150 years without ever becoming genuinely preventive.*

## Key Takeaways

1. In all four cases, comprehensive protective or retaliatory policy arrived only after the incumbent had already lost market leadership in at least one major sector; the lag from displacement to policy ranged from about one year (Japanese automobiles, the 1980 production crossover to the May 1981 voluntary export restraint) to about eight years (Chinese manufacturing, the 2010 crossover to the 2018 Section 301 tariffs).
2. The rhetorical taxonomy is stable across all four cases and 150 years, escalating from copying and fraud accusations through dumping claims to structural critiques and national security framing, and the security frame consistently unlocks the most aggressive instruments, as when the 1987 Defense Science Board report preceded SEMATECH and the 2022 export controls followed the Sullivan doctrine (Defense Science Board 1987; White House 2022).
3. The recognize-frame-respond cycle has compressed from roughly a decade in the British case to weeks in the most recent Chinese cases, where SMIC's confirmed 7nm production in September 2023 met tightened export controls within weeks and BYD's overtaking of Tesla in battery-electric sales in the fourth quarter of 2023 met a 100 per cent United States tariff within about six months (TechInsights 2023; European Commission 2024).

## Introduction

The claim that incumbents react to rising competitors rather than anticipating them is intuitive, but it is rarely tested across cases separated by a century and a half of institutional change. This paper examines four episodes of technological catch-up in which a leading power lost ground to a challenger: Britain confronting German industrialisation between roughly 1870 and 1914, the United States confronting Japanese catch-up between 1970 and 1990, the United States and Japan confronting Korean catch-up between 1980 and 1997, and the United States and the European Union confronting Chinese catch-up between 2005 and 2024. For each case the analysis fixes dated displacement milestones, sources the rhetoric that followed, codes that rhetoric into four categories (copying or fraud, dumping or unfair competition, structural critique, and national security framing), and dates the resulting policy interventions. The comparison then measures the intervals between milestone, rhetoric, and policy. The evidence supports a single conclusion: the sequence is stable and reactive, and although its speed has increased dramatically, its logic has not changed.

## The British Precedent of Slow Recognition and Rejected Response

Britain lost industrial leadership to Germany sector by sector, yet its policy response was the slowest and least effective of the four cases. Germany's steel production exceeded Britain's in 1893, and by 1914 Germany produced more than twice as much steel as Britain (Encyclopaedia Britannica 2024). In synthetic dyes, an industry Britain had founded with Perkin's discovery of mauveine in 1856, Germany reached a world market share of approximately 85 per cent by 1913, rising toward 90 per cent if German-owned plants abroad are counted, against Britain's 6.5 per cent (Murmann and Homburg 2001; Murmann 2003).

The rhetoric that followed displacement moved through the full taxonomy. Ernest Edwin Williams's tract Made in Germany, which ran through six editions in 1896, combined copying accusations ("his copy is often sufficiently like the British original to deceive the purchaser"), dumping claims ("German goods are often sold outside Germany at a price which is beneath the cost of production"), and a structural critique of German technical education, ending in an existential frame: "a gigantic commercial State is arising to menace our prosperity, and contend with us for the trade of the world" (Williams 1896). By 1903 Joseph Chamberlain had escalated to a security-tinged case for tariff protection against German and American competition.

The policy that followed was incremental and, on trade, absent. The Merchandise Marks Act received Royal Assent on 23 August 1887, but it required only country-of-origin labelling and, as Williams himself observed, advertised German goods rather than excluding them (Williams 1896). The Patents and Designs Act of 1907 forced patentees to manufacture in Britain, targeting German chemical patents. Chamberlain's tariff campaign, the one ambitious response, was decisively rejected at the general election of January 1906, and Britain enacted no general protective tariff until 1932. The British case establishes the baseline pattern in its slowest form: rhetoric followed displacement by about three years, and comprehensive trade policy never arrived at all.

## Japan and the Semiconductor Alarm

The American response to Japan compressed the same sequence into years rather than decades and added, for the first time, an operational national security frame. Japan surpassed the United States as the world's largest automobile producer in 1980, building about 7 million passenger cars against America's 6.4 million, and all four United States automakers recorded net losses that year (Sobel 1984). In semiconductors, Japanese firms held roughly 92 per cent of United States 256K DRAM sales by 1985, Japan overtook the United States in overall semiconductor share by 1987, and by 1989 the three largest producers in the world were all Japanese (Irwin 1996).

Rhetoric and policy tracked the two sectors on different clocks. On automobiles the response was almost immediate: a voluntary export restraint capping Japanese shipments at 1.68 million units annually was announced on 1 May 1981, negotiated between United States Trade Representative William Brock and MITI Minister Rokusuke Tanaka (Berry, Levinsohn and Pakes 1999). On semiconductors the Semiconductor Industry Association's dumping and market-closure petition of 1985 preceded the United States-Japan Semiconductor Trade Agreement of 2 September 1986, and when compliance faltered President Reagan imposed 100 per cent tariffs on 300 million dollars of Japanese electronics on 17 April 1987 (Irwin 1996). The decisive escalation was the Defense Science Board's report on defense semiconductor dependency of February 1987, chaired by Norman Augustine, which recast a trade dispute as a security problem and produced SEMATECH, a public consortium that received about 870 million dollars of federal funding through fiscal year 1996 (Defense Science Board 1987; Congressional Research Service 2016). Here the security frame did what trade framing alone could not: it justified industrial policy.

## Korea and the Selective Response

The Korean case shows that response speed depends on the political weight of the affected domestic industry, not merely on the scale of displacement. Samsung produced its first 64K DRAM in November 1983, having licensed circuit-design technology from Micron, and by 1992, the year it produced the world's first 64M DRAM, it had become the world's largest DRAM manufacturer, a position it held continuously until the first quarter of 2025 (Kim 1997; KED Global 2025). Hyundai's Excel entered the United States market on 20 February 1986 at 4,995 dollars and sold 168,882 units in its first year, and Korea overtook Japan in shipbuilding around 1999 to 2000.

Where a powerful United States firm was directly threatened, the response was fast and precise. The United States opened a Section 301 investigation into Korea's intellectual property regime on 4 November 1985, and Micron's antidumping petition of April 1992 produced antidumping duty orders on Korean DRAMs by 10 May 1993. Where the threatened incumbent was a weakened Japanese industry, the response was extraordinarily slow: Korea overtook Japan in shipbuilding around 2000, but Japan did not file its first World Trade Organization complaint against Korean shipbuilding subsidies (DS571) until 6 November 2018, followed by a second complaint (DS594) on 31 January 2020 (World Trade Organization 2024a; World Trade Organization 2024b). The same displacement produced a one-year response in semiconductors and an eighteen-year response in shipbuilding.

## China and the Comprehensive Turn

The Chinese case activates all four rhetorical categories at high intensity and, for the first time, produces a policy instrument aimed at preventing displacement rather than answering it. China surpassed the United States as the world's largest manufacturer in 2010, with manufacturing shares of about 19.8 per cent against 19.4 per cent (American Enterprise Institute 2011). China's share of every key stage of solar panel manufacturing exceeded 80 per cent by 2022 (International Energy Agency 2022), BYD surpassed Tesla in quarterly battery-electric vehicle sales in the fourth quarter of 2023 with 526,409 units against 484,507, and TechInsights confirmed SMIC's production of a 7nm chip using deep-ultraviolet lithography in the Huawei Mate 60 Pro in September 2023 (TechInsights 2023).

The rhetoric spans the taxonomy at its most explicit. The theft frame appears in General Keith Alexander's description of Chinese cyber-espionage as "the greatest transfer of wealth in history" on 9 July 2012 and in Robert Lighthizer's testimony of 17 May 2023 that the FBI opens a new China-related investigation "every ten hours" (Foreign Policy 2012; House Select Committee on the CCP 2023). The structural frame reached its most formal expression when the European Commission designated China a "systemic rival" on 12 March 2019 (European Commission 2019). The security frame culminated in National Security Advisor Jake Sullivan's statement of 16 September 2022 that the United States must maintain "as large a lead as possible", explicitly abandoning the previous doctrine of a relative technological margin (White House 2022).

Policy followed the by-now familiar reactive pattern, then broke from it once. The United States Trade Representative's Section 301 report of 22 March 2018 estimated harm of at least 50 billion dollars a year and produced tariff tranches beginning 6 July 2018; Huawei was placed on the Entity List on 16 May 2019; the CHIPS and Science Act of 9 August 2022 authorised 52.7 billion dollars for semiconductor incentives; and the European Union imposed definitive countervailing duties on Chinese battery-electric vehicles in October 2024, ranging from 7.8 per cent for Tesla's Shanghai output to 35.3 per cent for SAIC (United States Trade Representative 2018; European Commission 2024). The exception is the Bureau of Industry and Security's advanced-semiconductor export controls of 7 October 2022, which sought to freeze Chinese capability before parity at the frontier was reached. That instrument, foreshadowed by the Sullivan doctrine, is the first genuinely anticipatory move in the dataset.

## A Stable Rhetorical Ladder

The most striking cross-case regularity is that the rhetorical categories are identical across 150 years, and they appear in a consistent order. Incumbents open with narrow, legalistic charges of copying and dumping, then escalate to structural critiques of the challenger's economic model, and finally reach for national security framing. German trademark imitation and below-cost selling in the 1890s, Japanese reverse engineering and dumping in the 1980s, Korean subsidised credit in the 1990s and 2000s, and Chinese forced technology transfer and cyber-theft in the 2010s are the same first move in four costumes. The structural critique deepens over time, from German state education to "Japan Inc." to the Korean chaebol to the Chinese "systemic rival". The security frame is the decisive one, because it consistently authorises instruments that trade framing cannot: it produced SEMATECH in 1987 and the export-control regime of 2022, and it failed politically only in the British case, where Chamberlain's security argument for tariffs was rejected at the polls in 1906. The stability of this ladder across radically different political systems suggests that it reflects how incumbents process competitive threats rather than any contingent ideology.

## The Compression of the Response Cycle

The second cross-case regularity is temporal: the interval from displacement to response has shortened by roughly an order of magnitude, even as the sequence itself has held. The table below aligns the milestone, the rhetorical peak, and the policy response for each case, with the lags in years.

| Case | Sector | Milestone (year) | Rhetoric peak (year) | Policy (year) | Milestone to rhetoric | Rhetoric to policy | Milestone to policy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Britain vs Germany | Steel and chemicals | 1893 | 1896 | 1907 | 3 | 11 | 14 |
| US vs Japan | Automobiles | 1980 | 1981 | 1981 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| US vs Japan | Semiconductors | 1985 | 1985 | 1986 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| US/Japan vs Korea | DRAM (entry) | 1983 | 1985 | 1986 | 2 | 1 | 3 |
| US/Japan vs Korea | DRAM (leadership) | 1992 | 1992 | 1993 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| Japan vs Korea | Shipbuilding | 2000 | 2018 | 2018 | 18 | 0 | 18 |
| US/EU vs China | Manufacturing | 2010 | 2012 | 2018 | 2 | 6 | 8 |
| US/EU vs China | Telecom (Huawei) | 2012 | 2019 | 2019 | 7 | 0 | 7 |
| US/EU vs China | Electric vehicles | 2023 | 2023 | 2024 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| US/EU vs China | Advanced chips | 2023 | 2022 | 2023 | -1 | 1 | 0 |

Two features stand out. First, in no case did comprehensive policy precede the loss of market leadership; the British tariff campaign came after German dominance in steel and dyes and was rejected anyway, and the Section 301 tariffs against China trailed the manufacturing crossover by eight years. Second, the advanced-chips row is the single case in which rhetoric preceded the confirming milestone, because the Sullivan doctrine of September 2022 anticipated SMIC's demonstrated 7nm capability of September 2023. That inversion, and the near-simultaneous policy response, is what a genuinely preventive posture looks like, and it is confined to one sector of one case. The compression is real but it has not changed the order: institutional infrastructure built in earlier episodes, from Section 301 through the Entity List to the export-control authorities, lets the same recognise-frame-respond cycle execute faster, not earlier.

## The Strongest Counterargument

The most serious objection is that the 2022 export controls falsify the thesis by showing that incumbents can and do act preventively, so the "reactive" claim is at best a description of the past. The objection has force: the Bureau of Industry and Security's October 2022 rule was designed to deny China a capability it had not yet fully acquired, and the Sullivan doctrine that justified it explicitly rejected the old logic of a relative margin (White House 2022). If preventive action is now the norm, the sequence this paper describes is a historical artefact rather than a law.

The thesis survives this objection, but only just, and the boundary is precise. The export-control case is anticipatory in its aim, yet even it was triggered by cumulative evidence of Chinese progress and was followed within weeks by a reactive tightening once SMIC's 7nm production was confirmed in September 2023 (TechInsights 2023). One preventive instrument, in one sector, embedded in an otherwise reactive campaign of tariffs and entity listings that trailed Chinese displacement by seven to eight years, marks the edge of the pattern rather than its refutation. The honest formulation is that incumbents remain overwhelmingly reactive and have produced exactly one durable anticipatory instrument, whose emergence the thesis should predict as the limiting case at the point where response cycles compress toward zero. The thesis fails if future episodes show incumbents implementing comprehensive protective or denial policies before displacement milestones occur, as a rule rather than as a single frontier exception.

## Conclusion

Across four cases and 150 years, incumbents recognise technological threats late, frame them through an escalating and remarkably stable rhetorical ladder, and respond with instruments that trail the loss of market leadership. The rhetorical categories do not change, the reactive order does not change, and only the speed changes, compressing from a decade in Victorian Britain to weeks in the United States-China confrontation of 2023 and 2024. The one break in the pattern, the anticipatory export controls of 2022, is best read not as a refutation but as the limiting case the compression itself produces: when the interval between displacement and response approaches zero, the difference between reacting quickly and acting preventively begins to dissolve. Whether that single instrument becomes a new norm is the question the next episode of catch-up will answer.
