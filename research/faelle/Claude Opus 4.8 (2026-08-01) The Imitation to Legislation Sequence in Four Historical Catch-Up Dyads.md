# The Imitation-to-Legislation Sequence in Four Historical Catch-Up Dyads

*This paper argues that across four historical catch-up dyads the incumbent economy answered imitation-based competition with the same three-step sequence, perception of imitation, accusation of unfair competition, then legislative or regulatory response, yet the economic-history literature documents this sequence almost entirely through narrative case study rather than measurement, leaving the pattern asserted but never systematically quantified.*

## Key Takeaways

1. The three-step sequence (imitation perceived, unfair competition alleged, law enacted) recurs across all four dyads, from Britain's reaction to German goods that produced the Merchandise Marks Act 1887 to the United States Section 301 actions against Japan and later China (Williams 1896; Bayard and Elliott 1994; USTR 2018).
2. In each incumbent response the legislative step is concrete and datable: the Merchandise Marks Act 1887, the 1986 US-Japan Semiconductor Agreement that followed the Semiconductor Industry Association's Section 301 petition of 14 June 1985, the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, and the Section 301 report of 22 March 2018 followed by the Export Control Reform Act of 13 August 2018 (Harvey 2021; Flamm 1996; Destler 1992; USTR 2018; US Congress 2018).
3. The evidence base is overwhelmingly narrative: the foundational dyad studies are institutional histories, while the quantitative work measures adjacent objects such as productivity, patents, trade flows, or coded case outcomes rather than the rhetoric-to-law sequence itself, so the recurring pattern remains largely unmeasured (Johnson 1982; Amsden 1989; Broadberry 1998; Lee and Lim 2001; Bayard and Elliott 1994).

## Introduction

Late industrialisation has long been understood as a process of borrowing before building, in which relative backwardness is itself an advantage because proven technologies can be absorbed without repeating the leader's costly learning (Gerschenkron 1962; Abramovitz 1986). The mirror image of that process, how the incumbent leader reacts when a follower absorbs its technology and contests its markets, has received far less systematic treatment. This paper reads the economic-history and political-economy literature on four incumbent-follower dyads, Britain and Germany (1870 to 1914), the United States and Japan (1970 to 1990), Japan and Korea (1980 to 2000), and the United States and the European Union against China (2000 to 2025), and asks whether the incumbent response follows a common structure and whether that structure has been measured.

The reading yields a recurring sequence and a methodological gap. Incumbents move from perceiving the follower's success as imitation, to reframing that imitation as unfair competition, to enacting trade, marking, or intellectual-property legislation in response. The sequence is visible in every dyad, but it lives in the literature as a story rather than as data, which is precisely where the research opportunity lies.

## The Sequence: Imitation Perceived, Competition Contested, Law Enacted

The recurring pattern has three ordered steps that can be separated analytically even where they overlap in time. The first is perceptual: the incumbent reclassifies the follower's products from independent achievement to derivative copying. The second is normative: imitation is recast as cheating, a violation of fair practice rather than ordinary competition. The third is institutional: the incumbent legislates, whether through origin-marking rules, tariffs, managed-trade agreements, or export controls. That the leaders of one era deploy against followers the very practices, protection and selective imitation, that they themselves used while catching up is a well-documented historical irony (Chang 2002), and it is not confined to the four dyads studied here: the United States itself extended no meaningful patent or copyright protection to foreign inventors and authors through much of the nineteenth century (Khan 2005). The sequence, in other words, is a structural feature of catch-up rivalry, not a peculiarity of any single pair.

## Britain and Germany, 1870 to 1914

The Anglo-German case established the template. British manufacturers first perceived German goods as inferior imitations, a perception crystallised in the period's most influential polemic, which cast German production as a copying threat to British industry (Williams 1896). That perception hardened into a charge of unfair practice, documented in the parliamentary investigations and commercial reports of the era (Hoffman 1933). The legislative response was the Merchandise Marks Act 1887, which required imported goods to carry an indication of origin; a quantitative reassessment finds that the Act functioned as an early non-tariff barrier and soft protectionism, and that, far from halting the advance of German manufacturing exports, it damaged Britain's own entrepot trade and shipping (Harvey 2021). The underlying rivalry was real: German firms systematically absorbed British techniques, for example in iron-making, often without licence (Fremdling 2000), and the relative productivity position shifted over these decades, although sectoral analysis attributes Germany's and America's overtaking of Britain mainly to reallocation out of agriculture and to gains in services rather than to manufacturing (Broadberry 1998; Webb 1980). Contemporaries and later historians agreed that the "Made in Germany" label, intended to stigmatise, instead advertised German quality (Neuburger and Stokes 1979).

## The United States and Japan, 1970 to 1990

The US-Japan case reproduced the sequence in a high-technology setting. American perception recast Japanese success as the product of imitation, reverse engineering, and state-orchestrated market closure (Prestowitz 1988; Johnson 1982). That perception became a formal charge of unfair competition in industry after industry, with quantified market-share and subsidy claims marshalled to support managed-trade remedies (Tyson 1992). The institutional response came through Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974: the Semiconductor Industry Association filed its petition on 14 June 1985, negotiations produced the US-Japan Semiconductor Agreement signed on 2 September 1986, and, when Japan's compliance was judged inadequate, the United States announced retaliatory tariffs of up to 100 percent on 27 March 1987 (Flamm 1996; Irwin 1996). The legislative capstone was the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, whose "Special 301" provision (Section 182 of the Trade Act) institutionalised annual watch-listing of foreign intellectual-property practices (Destler 1992; Bhagwati and Patrick 1990). This dyad also produced the single most complete quantitative treatment of the enforcement instrument, a coded assessment of all Section 301 cases concluded between 1975 and 1993 (Bayard and Elliott 1994).

## Japan and Korea, 1980 to 2000

The Japan-Korea case fits the first two steps cleanly but displaces the third, which qualifies the pattern rather than confirming it mechanically. Korea's ascent rested on a documented progression from duplicative to creative imitation and then to innovation, with firm-level catch-up in electronics and steel (Amsden 1989; Kim 1997). The incumbent response, however, took the form of private technology-licensing refusal by Japanese firms rather than a statute: Japanese incumbents declined to license core technologies to Korean entrants, which pushed Korean firms toward reverse engineering, learning by hiring experienced engineers, and independent research (Kim 2003; Hobday 1995; Song, Almeida and Wu 2003). Formal intellectual-property tightening in Korea arrived largely through United States pressure rather than through a Japan-Korea legislative channel, and the quantitative literature here measures the catch-up itself, through patent-based analysis of technological regimes, rather than any incumbent rhetoric (Lee and Lim 2001). The dyad therefore shows that the "legislation" step is contingent: where the incumbent can gatekeep through private contract, it may not need a public law.

## The United States, the European Union, and China, 2000 to 2025

The China case is the sequence at its most elaborate and most contested. Perception recast Chinese industrial success as intellectual-property theft and forced technology transfer, even as some scholars argued that China's integration into global value chains yielded assembly capability rather than deep technological absorption (Steinfeld 2004), while others documented rising indigenous innovation from the mid-2000s (Branstetter and Lardy 2008). The normative charge drew on a longer normative shift in which strong intellectual-property protection had been globalised as a development prerequisite (Sell 2003), and on institutional accounts showing that weak enforcement in China reflected bureaucratic fragmentation as much as intent (Mertha 2005; Naughton 2007). The institutional response was the most forceful of the four: the United States Trade Representative initiated a Section 301 investigation on 18 August 2017, issued its findings on 22 March 2018, and imposed the first tariffs on 6 July 2018 (USTR 2018), while the Export Control Reform Act became law on 13 August 2018 to control emerging and foundational technologies (US Congress 2018). Legal scholarship is divided on whether the "forced technology transfer" charge describes practices that are actually unlawful or largely legal market bargains, which is exactly the normative contest the second step of the sequence predicts (Wu 2016; Qin 2019; Sykes 2021; Prud'homme et al. 2018).

## Narrative Saturation and the Measurement Gap

The decisive feature of this literature is not that the sequence is absent but that it has never been coded. The foundational studies are narrative institutional histories, powerful on mechanism and motive but silent on frequency, timing, and magnitude (Johnson 1982; Amsden 1989; Prestowitz 1988; Mertha 2005). The quantitative contributions, in turn, measure adjacent objects: comparative productivity (Broadberry 1998; Webb 1980), technology diffusion (Fremdling 2000), patent-based catch-up (Lee and Lim 2001), the patenting share of innovations, where for example 89 percent of British exhibits at the 1851 exhibition were never patented (Moser 2013), and the outcomes of trade-enforcement cases (Bayard and Elliott 1994). None of these codes the rhetoric-to-law sequence itself, that is, the incumbent's shift from imitation talk to unfair-competition talk to statute, across dyads on a common scale. | Dyad | Perception marker | Unfair-competition framing | Legislative or regulatory response | Dominant evidence mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Britain vs Germany (1870-1914) | German goods as inferior copies (Williams 1896) | Commercial-rivalry complaints (Hoffman 1933) | Merchandise Marks Act 1887 (Harvey 2021) | Narrative, with partial trade-series quant |
| US vs Japan (1970-1990) | Reverse engineering, closed market (Prestowitz 1988) | Managed-trade and "unfair trader" framing (Tyson 1992) | Semiconductor Agreement 1986; Omnibus Act 1988 (Flamm 1996; Destler 1992) | Narrative, with coded 301-case dataset (Bayard and Elliott 1994) |
| Japan vs Korea (1980-2000) | Duplicative then creative imitation (Kim 1997) | Licensing refusal as gatekeeping (Kim 2003) | Displaced onto US-Korea IP pressure; no Japan-Korea statute | Narrative, with patent-based catch-up quant (Lee and Lim 2001) |
| US/EU vs China (2000-2025) | IP theft, forced transfer (USTR 2018) | Contested legality of transfer (Qin 2019; Sykes 2021) | Section 301 action 2018; Export Control Reform Act 2018 (USTR 2018; US Congress 2018) | Narrative and legal, with FDI and patent quant (Branstetter and Lardy 2008) |

## The Strongest Counterargument

The strongest objection is that the "sequence" is a pattern imposed after the fact on cases too heterogeneous to share a structure. The four episodes differ in instrument (origin marking, managed trade, private licensing, export controls), in the identity of the responding actor (parliament, executive, firms), and in whether the follower's practices were genuinely illegitimate or merely competitive. On this view the recurring "unfair competition" charge is not a rhetorical move at all but sometimes an accurate description of genuinely different practices, and grouping the dyads obscures more than it reveals; the Japan-Korea case, where no incumbent statute appears, is offered as evidence that the sequence breaks. This objection has real force, and the Japan-Korea displacement shows that the third step is contingent rather than mechanical. Yet the objection strengthens rather than dissolves the thesis, because it identifies exactly the variation that a coding exercise would capture: the perception and unfair-competition steps recur even where the legislative step is displaced, and the incumbent practice of legislating against imitation it once relied on is documented across otherwise dissimilar cases (Chang 2002; Khan 2005). The claim is not that every dyad ends in identical law, but that the ordered move from imitation talk to fairness talk to institutional response recurs. *The thesis fails if a systematic coding of incumbent rhetoric and legislative timing across the four dyads shows the three steps to be temporally unordered or absent in most episodes, so that accusation neither follows imitation perception nor precedes the institutional response.*

## Conclusion

The literature on catch-up rivalry contains, without quite naming it, a recurring incumbent response sequence: imitation perceived, unfair competition alleged, law enacted. Across Britain and Germany, the United States and Japan, Japan and Korea, and the West and China, the first two steps appear in every case and the third in three of four, displaced onto a private or third-party channel only where the incumbent could gatekeep without a statute. The gap is methodological rather than substantive. The mechanism is richly narrated and the adjacent magnitudes, productivity, patents, trade cases, are well measured, but the sequence itself has not been coded on a common scale across dyads. That gap is the opening: a dataset that times and codes incumbent rhetoric against legislative response would convert a persuasive historical pattern into a testable regularity.
