# Incumbent Rhetoric and Policy Escalation After Technological Displacement: Four Cases

**Claude (2026)**

---

## Abstract

This analysis tests whether incumbent powers systematically escalate rhetoric and implement policy interventions following technological displacement by a rising competitor. Four cases are examined: Britain responding to German industrialisation (c. 1870–1914), the United States responding to Japanese catch-up (c. 1970–1990), the United States and Japan responding to Korean catch-up (c. 1980–1997), and the United States and European Union responding to Chinese catch-up (c. 2005–2024). For each case, technological displacement milestones are documented, rhetorical output is sourced and coded into four categories, policy responses are dated, and lag intervals are calculated. The cross-case synthesis identifies three robust findings: incumbents do not act preventively; rhetoric follows a stable escalation ladder from fraud accusations to national security framing across all four cases and 150 years; and the milestone-to-policy lag has compressed from roughly a decade in the Victorian period to weeks in the most recent episode.

---

## Definitions

**Technological displacement** is operationalised as measurable events including: achieving production parity with the incumbent; achieving global market-share leadership; surpassing the incumbent on quality benchmarks; and entering frontier technology nodes.

**Rhetorical escalation** denotes public official statements, industry association publications, and parliamentary or congressional records accusing the challenger of unfairness, copying, dumping, structural rule violation, or systemic rivalry.

**Policy intervention** encompasses tariffs, quotas, voluntary export restraints (VERs), technology export controls, formal trade investigations, industrial policy countermeasures, and patent law reforms linked to the dispute.

Rhetoric is coded in four non-exclusive categories: (A) Fraud/copying accusations; (B) Dumping and unfair competition claims; (C) Structural/systemic model critiques; and (D) National security framing.

## Case 1: Britain and German Industrialisation, c. 1870–1914

### 1.1 Technological Displacement Milestones

**Milestone 1 — Steel production crossover, 1893.** Germany surpassed Britain as the world's largest steel producer in 1893 (Encyclopaedia Britannica 2024a). British output grew from approximately 3.6 million tons in 1890 to 7.8 million tons by 1914 — a compound annual rate of roughly 3.3 per cent. German output grew from approximately 2.2 million tons in 1890 to 17.6 million tons by 1914, a compound rate of approximately 9 per cent (Broadberry and Burhop 2010). By 1914 Germany produced more than twice Britain's steel output (Encyclopaedia Britannica 2024a).

**Milestone 2 — Synthetic dyes, dominance consolidated by 1900.** Synthetic dyes were invented in Britain: William Henry Perkin discovered mauveine in 1856 (Wikipedia 2024a). In the early phase (1857–c. 1864) Britain and France together dominated world output. German producers — Hoechst, BASF, Bayer — rapidly displaced them. By approximately 1870 Germany held around 50 per cent of world dye output; by 1900 its share exceeded 75 per cent, and by 1914 Germany supplied approximately 75–80 per cent of world synthetic dye production (Murmann and Homburg 2001; Encyclopaedia Britannica 2024b). Germany also held a near-monopoly on patented dye processes, effectively blocking British re-entry.

**Milestone 3 — Electrical engineering, market leadership by c. 1900.** AEG and Siemens became among the world's leading electrical engineering firms by 1900. AEG's corporate history documents construction of 248 power stations by this date (Electrolux Group 2024). German firms — principally AEG and Siemens & Halske — jointly dominated the global electrical machinery market through cartel arrangements and far outpaced British competitors in both volume and patent registrations (Kocka 1999).

### 1.2 Rhetorical Documentation

**Source 1 — Royal Commission on the Depression in Trade and Industry, 1885–1886 [Categories A, B, C].** Established in August 1885 (Hansard, House of Lords, 10 August 1885), the Commission's Final Report (Command Paper C.4893, December 1886) acknowledged increasing competition from Germany and the United States in iron, steel, textiles, and chemicals. While constrained by free-trade orthodoxy, the Report documented foreign firms' price advantages and government support for technical education. Parliamentary discussions around the Commission raised early dumping concerns: Mr Newdegate, in the House of Commons on 5 August 1885, stated that "the system of free imports, to which this country had so strictly adhered, had placed her in such a position that other countries found that we had nothing to exchange" (Hansard, 5 August 1885). *Coding: C (structural critique), B (implicit dumping concern).*

**Source 2 — Ernest Edwin Williams, *Made in Germany*, 1896 [Categories A, B, C].** Williams's book, which ran through six editions in its first year, constitutes the most comprehensive rhetorical statement of the period and was widely cited in subsequent parliamentary debates. On imitation: "His copy is often sufficiently like the British original to deceive the purchaser" (Williams 1896, p. 10). On below-cost pricing: "German goods are often sold outside Germany at a price which is beneath the cost of production, for the purpose of forcing a way into the market" (Williams 1896, p. 14). On structural advantage: "The attention paid by the State in Germany to Education — and particularly to Scientific and Technical Education — is matter of common knowledge the world over" (Williams 1896, p. 23). On displacement: "A gigantic commercial State is arising to menace our prosperity, and contend with us for the trade of the world" (Williams 1896, p. 5). *Coding: A (fraud/copying), B (dumping), C (structural critique).*

**Source 3 — Hansard, Merchandise Marks Act Amendment debates, 27 January 1897 [Category A].** Parliamentary debate acknowledged that the 1887 Act was insufficient. One speaker stated: "The object of that legislation was solely confined to the prevention of fraud, to stop fraudulent marking of goods" (Hansard, 27 January 1897). The framing remained one of commercial fraud rather than strategic rivalry. *Coding: A (fraud/copying).*

**Source 4 — Joseph Chamberlain, tariff reform campaign, 1903 [Categories C, D].** Chamberlain launched his Tariff Reform campaign in May 1903 with a speech in Birmingham, arguing that free trade was destroying British industry. At Greenock in October 1903 he stated: "Sugar is gone. Silk has gone. Iron is threatened. Wool is threatened. Cotton will go! How long are you going to stand it?" (cited in Encyclopaedia Britannica 2024c). He argued that protective tariffs would "add to Britain's international security, protect manufactures threatened by new competition from the United States and Germany" (Encyclopaedia Britannica 2024c). *Coding: C (structural critique), D (national security framing).*

### 1.3 Policy Responses

| Date | Instrument | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 23 August 1887 | Merchandise Marks Act 1887 (50 & 51 Vict. c. 28) | Required country-of-origin labelling on imports (vLex 2024) |
| 1889 | Technical Instruction Act 1889 | Empowered local authorities to fund technical education |
| 1902 | Balfour Education Act | Created Local Education Authorities for secondary and technical schooling |
| 1903 | Chamberlain Tariff Reform campaign | Proposed import duties; **rejected** at January 1906 general election |
| 1907 | Patents and Designs Act amendment | Required patentees to manufacture in Britain; targeted German chemical patent-blocking |
| 1907 | Imperial College founded | Consolidated three technical institutions (Royal College of Science, Royal School of Mines, City and Guilds) |

General protective tariffs were not enacted until the Import Duties Act 1932 — nearly four decades after the steel production crossover.

### 1.4 Lag Calculations

| Milestone | Date | First Rhetoric Peak | Lag (years) | Policy Response | Lag from Milestone (years) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steel crossover | 1893 | Williams (1896) | 3 | Technical Instruction Act (1889, pre-dates crossover) | — |
| Dye dominance | c. 1900 | Williams (1896) | pre-dates | Patents Act (1907) | 7 |
| Tariff campaign | 1903 | — | — | Never enacted | ∞ |

**Summary:** Rhetoric peaked 3 years after the steel crossover. Education reform lagged the Commission's 1884 recommendations by 5 years. Comprehensive trade protection was never enacted in this period.

## Case 2: The United States and Japanese Catch-Up, c. 1970–1990

### 2.1 Technological Displacement Milestones

**Milestone 1 — Automobile production crossover, 1980.** Japan surpassed the United States as the world's largest automobile producer in 1980, manufacturing approximately 7 million passenger vehicles versus America's approximately 6.4 million (EBSCO 2024; Toyota 2024a). All four US automakers recorded net losses simultaneously for the first time: General Motors lost $762 million, Ford $1.5 billion, Chrysler $1.7 billion, and AMC $156 million (Sobel 1984). Japanese cars captured approximately 21 per cent of the US market that year (Berry, Levinsohn and Pakes 1999).

**Milestone 2 — Semiconductor DRAM market leadership, 1981–1985.** Japanese firms overtook the United States in the 64K DRAM segment by 1981, reaching approximately 70 per cent global share (Semiconductor History Museum of Japan 2024). By 1985, Japanese producers held approximately 92 per cent of US 256K DRAM sales (Irwin 1996, citing Citizens for a Sound Economy 1986). In 1987 Japan surpassed the United States in overall semiconductor market share, reaching 48 per cent versus the US's 39 per cent (Okada 2005; AMRO 2025). By 1989 the world's top three semiconductor firms were all Japanese: NEC at number one, Toshiba at number two, and Hitachi at number three (Chicago Tribune 1991, citing Dataquest).

### 2.2 Rhetorical Documentation

**Source 1 — Semiconductor Industry Association, *Japanese Market Barriers in Microelectronics*, 1985 [Categories A, B, C].** The SIA's 1984–1985 documentation, which formed the basis of a Section 301 petition filed June 1985, accused MITI of organising "unfair subsidies, improper tax advantages, and restricted domestic market" access (Irwin 1996, p. 11). The SIA noted that US producers held 83 per cent of their domestic market and 55 per cent in Europe but "only 11 per cent in Japan" (Irwin 1996, p. 11). Micron Technology's antidumping complaint against Japanese 64K DRAM producers was filed in June 1985; Intel, AMD, and National Semiconductor filed EPROM complaints in September 1985. *Coding: A (copying/misappropriation), B (dumping), C (structural market closure).*

**Source 2 — Defense Science Board Task Force Report on Defense Semiconductor Dependency, 13 February 1987 [Category D].** The Task Force, chaired by Norman Augustine (President, Martin Marietta Corporation), found that the nation's "growing dependency on foreign sources for vital semiconductor hardware and technology" was among the "most serious matters they have had the occasion to address" (Defense Science Board 1987, p. ii; UPI 1987). The report recommended what became SEMATECH. *Coding: D (national security framing).*

**Source 3 — Lee Iacocca, public statements, 1984 and 1992 [Categories B, C].** Iacocca posed an explicit colonial analogy in 1984: "What do you call a country that exports raw materials and imports finished goods? A colony" (cited in Crain's Detroit Business 2019). In January 1992 he stated: "I for one am fed up hearing from the Japanese… that all our problems in this industry are our own damn fault. We do not have idiots running General Motors, Ford and Chrysler" (Chicago Tribune 1992). *Coding: B (unfair competition), C (structural critique).*

**Source 4 — Clyde Prestowitz, *Trading Places*, 1988 [Category C].** Prestowitz, former Commerce Department Counsellor for Japan Affairs, characterised Japan as using "industry policy as its national security policy" and warned of America becoming an economic "colony-in-the-making" (Prestowitz 1988, p. 28). *Coding: C (structural/systemic critique).*

**Source 5 — James Fallows, "Containing Japan," *The Atlantic Monthly*, May 1989 [Categories C, D].** Fallows argued that Japan's economic organisation was fundamentally incompatible with free-trade assumptions and required containment analogous to Cold War strategy (Fallows 1989). *Coding: C (structural), D (security framing).*

### 2.3 Policy Responses

| Date | Instrument | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 May 1981 | Voluntary Export Restraint (automobiles) | Japan limited exports to 1.68 million units annually; negotiated by MITI Minister Rokusuke Tanaka and USTR Malcolm Brock (Toyota 2024b; GWU 2024) |
| 2 September 1986 | US-Japan Semiconductor Trade Agreement | Required Japan to halt dumping; committed Japan to opening market to 20 per cent foreign share (Reagan Library 2024a) |
| 17 April 1987 | 100% tariffs on Japanese electronics | Reagan imposed tariffs on $300 million in Japanese exports for non-compliance (Reagan Library 2024b) |
| 22 September 1985 | Plaza Accord | Coordinated dollar depreciation; yen strengthened from ¥240/$ to ¥120/$ by 1988 (Wikipedia 2024b) |
| 1987 | SEMATECH founded | Semiconductor Manufacturing Technology consortium; received approximately $870 million in federal funding through FY1996 (CRS 2016) |
| 23 August 1988 | Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act (P.L. 100-418) | Super 301, Exon-Florio Amendment (foreign acquisition screening), Toshiba-Kongsberg sanctions |
| 25 May 1989 | Japan designated Super 301 "priority country" | Triggered mandatory negotiations under Omnibus Trade Act |

### 2.4 Lag Calculations

| Milestone | Date | Rhetoric Peak | Lag (years) | Policy Response | Lag from Milestone (years) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Auto crossover | 1980 | SIA 1985, Iacocca 1984 | 0–4 | VER (May 1981) | 1 |
| DRAM leadership | 1981–85 | SIA petition (June 1985) | 0–4 | Semiconductor Agreement (Sept. 1986) | 1–5 |
| Overall semiconductor leadership | 1987 | DSB Report (Feb. 1987) | Concurrent | Omnibus Act (Aug. 1988) | 1 |

**Summary:** Automotive response was extremely rapid — one year from crossover to VER. The semiconductor cycle was slower, approximately 4–5 years from first DRAM dominance to trade agreement. The DSB's national security framing in February 1987 triggered SEMATECH and accelerated the legislative response.

## Case 3: The United States and Japan Respond to Korean Catch-Up, c. 1980–1997

### 3.1 Technological Displacement Milestones

**Milestone 1 — Samsung enters DRAM production, November 1983.** Samsung Chairman Lee Byung-chul made what Korean media called the "Tokyo Declaration" in February 1983, committing Samsung to DRAM production. Samsung produced its first 64K DRAM in November 1983, having licensed DRAM circuit design technology from Micron Technology; Sharp's technology contribution covered SRAM and ROM, not DRAM (Kim 1997, BRIE WP106; Samsung Semiconductor 2024). The technology gap closed rapidly: 256K DRAM (October 1984), 1M DRAM (July 1986), 4M DRAM (February 1988), 16M DRAM (August–September 1990), 64M DRAM — a world first — in October 1992 (Samsung Semiconductor 2024; Kim 1997).

**Milestone 2 — Samsung reaches number one in DRAM, 1992.** Samsung achieved global DRAM market share leadership in 1992 with approximately 13.5 per cent, surpassing Toshiba at 12.8 per cent, a position it has held with few interruptions since (Harvard Business School 2003; Samsung Semiconductor 2024).

**Milestone 3 — Hyundai Excel enters US market, 20 February 1986.** The Excel launched at a base price of $4,995, the lowest for any new car sold in the United States at that time. It sold 168,882 units in its first model year — an industry record for an import brand's debut (Hyundai Motor Manufacturing Alabama 2024; Wikipedia 2024c).

**Milestone 4 — Korea surpasses Japan in shipbuilding, c. 1999–2000.** South Korea overtook Japan in new vessel orders around 1999 and in overall market share by 2000, capturing approximately 40 per cent of the global shipbuilding market (ORF 2025; GlobalSecurity.org 2024).

### 3.2 Rhetorical Documentation

**Source 1 — USTR Section 301 Investigation on Korean Intellectual Property, November 1985 [Categories A, C].** On 4 November 1985, USTR initiated a Section 301 investigation after finding that "Korean laws deny patent protection for pharmaceutical and agricultural chemical products and do not provide copyright protection for computer software" (Reagan Library 1986). USTR characterised Korea's copyright protection as "virtually non-existent" (Brooklyn Law Journal 1991). President Reagan's memorandum of July 1986 formalised the diplomatic resolution. *Coding: A (fraud/copying), C (structural model critique).*

**Source 2 — Micron Technology antidumping petition against Korean DRAM producers, April 1992 [Category B].** Micron alleged systematic below-cost sales by Samsung, LG Semicon (later Hynix), and Hyundai Electronics. EDN noted that "Micron's use of the US anti-dumping laws is a key element of its business strategy in the memory business" (EDN 1997). The Department of Commerce issued antidumping duty orders on 10 May 1993 (58 Fed. Reg. 27520), finding margins of 7.61 per cent for LG Semicon and 12.64 per cent for Hyundai. *Coding: B (dumping/unfair competition).*

**Source 3 — Micron countervailing duty petition against Korean DRAM producers, November 2002 [Categories B, C].** Micron filed a CVD petition alleging that the Korean government had provided Samsung and Hynix with subsidies including "tax reductions, write-offs, debt-for-equity swaps, rollover of loans, and interest rate reductions" following the 1997–98 financial crisis (Korean Ministry of Foreign Affairs 2002). The Department of Commerce issued a CVD order in August 2003 (USITC Publication 3616). *Coding: B (unfair competition), C (structural/systemic critique).*

**Source 4 — Japan Shipbuilders' Association statement on Korean shipbuilding subsidies, January 2018 [Categories B, C].** In connection with Japan's preparation of a WTO complaint, Japanese industry associations stated that "massive fund injections and financial supports dishonoring international rules to companies in fatal state by governmental financial institutions have resulted in preserving superfluous capacity" (Marine Log 2018). *Coding: B (unfair competition), C (structural critique).*

### 3.3 Policy Responses

| Date | Instrument | Description |
|---|---|---|
| November 1985 | Section 301 investigation on Korean IP | USTR investigation on copyright and patent deficiencies |
| July 1986 | Korea-US IP Agreement | Korea agreed to strengthen patent and copyright law |
| 10 May 1993 | Antidumping orders on Korean DRAMs (58 Fed. Reg. 27520) | Duties of 7.61–12.64% on Korean DRAM producers |
| January 1999 | WTO Panel ruling (DS99) | Panel found US antidumping methodology partially WTO-inconsistent |
| August 2003 | CVD order on Korean DRAMs (USITC Pub. 3616) | Countervailing duties following Micron's 2002 petition |
| 6 November 2018 | Japan WTO complaint DS571 | Japan challenged Korean shipbuilding subsidies (WTO 2024a) |
| 31 January 2020 | Japan WTO complaint DS594 | Second Japanese complaint on Korean shipbuilding subsidies (WTO 2024b) |

### 3.4 Lag Calculations

| Milestone | Date | Rhetoric Peak | Lag (years) | Policy Response | Lag from Milestone (years) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Samsung DRAM entry | Nov. 1983 | Section 301 investigation (Nov. 1985) | 2 | Korea-US IP Agreement (1986) | ~3 |
| Samsung DRAM leadership | 1992 | Micron antidumping petition (Apr. 1992) | ~0 | AD order (May 1993) | ~1 |
| Korea shipbuilding leadership | c. 1999–2000 | Industry statements (2017–18) | ~18 | WTO DS571 (Nov. 2018) | ~18–19 |

**Summary:** The semiconductor response was fast — approximately one year from DRAM market leadership to formal antidumping orders. The shipbuilding response was extraordinarily slow — approximately 18 years from Korean displacement to Japan's formal WTO complaint, illustrating how the political power of affected domestic industries, not merely the magnitude of displacement, governs response speed.

## Case 4: The United States and European Union Respond to Chinese Catch-Up, c. 2005–2024

### 4.1 Technological Displacement Milestones

**Milestone 1 — China becomes the world's largest manufacturer, 2010.** China surpassed the United States as the world's largest manufacturing nation in 2010, when IHS Global Insight estimated China's manufacturing value-added reached 19.8 per cent of global output versus the US's 19.4 per cent (AEI 2011; CEPR/Baldwin 2024). By 2020 China accounted for approximately 35 per cent of global manufacturing output — roughly three times the US share and six times Japan's (Baldwin 2024).

**Milestone 2 — Huawei surpasses Ericsson in total revenue, 2012.** Huawei surpassed Ericsson in overall company revenue in the first half of 2012 ($16.1 billion versus $15.25 billion), and was ranked the world's largest telecom equipment maker by total revenue in 2012 by several industry analysts including Infonetics (China.org.cn 2012; RCR Wireless 2013). Caixin Global reported that by IHS Markit's narrower product-line measurement, Huawei overtook Ericsson in telecom equipment specifically by 2017 (Caixin Global 2018). Dell'Oro Group data show Huawei reaching approximately 31 per cent global telecom equipment market share by 2020.

**Milestone 3 — China achieves solar panel manufacturing dominance, c. 2012–2020.** China's share in all key manufacturing stages of solar panels — polysilicon, ingots, wafers, cells, and modules — exceeded 80 per cent as of 2022, with costs approximately 20 per cent below US levels and 35 per cent below European levels (IEA 2022, p. 4).

**Milestone 4 — BYD surpasses Tesla in quarterly battery-electric vehicle sales, Q4 2023.** BYD sold 526,409 passenger battery-electric vehicles in Q4 2023, versus Tesla's 484,507 deliveries — the first quarter in which BYD exceeded Tesla in pure battery-electric sales, excluding plug-in hybrids (InsideEVs 2024a; InsideEVs 2024b).

**Milestone 5 — SMIC's 7nm chip, confirmed August–September 2023.** TechInsights analysis of the Huawei Mate 60 Pro, released in August 2023, confirmed SMIC's production of a 7nm-class chip using deep ultraviolet (DUV) lithography, demonstrating Chinese capability to advance at the frontier despite export controls (TechInsights 2023).

### 4.2 Rhetorical Documentation

**Source 1 — General Keith Alexander, NSA Director, American Enterprise Institute, 9 July 2012 [Category A].** Alexander, speaking at the AEI event "Cybersecurity and American Power," stated that Chinese cyber espionage constituted "the greatest transfer of wealth in history," citing annual losses to US firms of $250 billion (AEI 2012; *Foreign Policy* 2012). *Coding: A (theft/fraud accusation).*

**Source 2 — USTR Section 301 Report on China's Technology Transfer Practices, 22 March 2018 [Categories A, B, C].** The USTR found China's acts, policies, and practices in technology transfer "unreasonable or discriminatory" across four domains: forced technology transfer as a condition of market access; discriminatory and non-market licensing restrictions; state-directed acquisition of US technology through investment; and cyber-enabled theft of US trade secrets. The report estimated harm at "at least $50 billion per year" (USTR 2018). *Coding: A (theft/copying), B (unfair commercial practices), C (structural model critique).*

**Source 3 — European Commission Joint Communication, "EU-China — A Strategic Outlook," 12 March 2019 [Categories C, D].** This document (JOIN(2019) 5) was the first EU communication to formally designate China as "simultaneously a cooperation partner, a negotiating partner, an economic competitor, and a systemic rival promoting alternative models of governance" (European Commission 2019, p. 1). The term "systemic rival" had no precedent in EU communications toward any partner. *Coding: C (structural/systemic critique), D (security framing).*

**Source 4 — Ursula von der Leyen, European Commission President, State of the Union Address, 13 September 2023 [Categories B, C].** Von der Leyen stated: "Global markets are now flooded with cheaper electric cars. And their price is kept artificially low by huge state subsidies. This is distorting our market" (European Commission 2023). She announced the launch of an anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese battery electric vehicles. *Coding: B (unfair subsidies), C (structural critique).*

**Source 5 — National Security Advisor Jake Sullivan, speech, 16 September 2022 [Category D].** Sullivan stated: "We previously maintained a 'sliding scale' approach that said we need to stay only a couple of generations ahead. That is not the strategic environment we are in today… we must maintain as large a lead as possible" (White House 2022). This statement explicitly reversed the prior US doctrine of maintaining only a relative technological advantage. *Coding: D (national security framing — most explicit in the dataset).*

**Source 6 — Robert Lighthizer, testimony before the House Select Committee on China, 17 May 2023 [Categories A, D].** Lighthizer testified that "the FBI has approximately one thousand investigations into attempted Chinese theft of US-based technology ongoing at any one time and opens a new investigation into Chinese espionage every ten hours" (House Select Committee 2023). *Coding: A (theft/fraud), D (national security).*

| 6 September 2012 | EU solar anti-dumping investigation initiated | Settled July 2013 on minimum import price of €0.56/Wp |
| 18 August 2017 | USTR Section 301 investigation initiated | Formal initiation per USTR press release; Federal Register notice published 24 August 2017 (USTR 2017) |

**Summary:** The China case compresses strikingly within itself. The manufacturing crossover (2010) took 7–8 years to produce comprehensive trade retaliation. By 2023–24, new displacement events triggered policy responses within months. The October 2022 BIS controls represent the dataset's clearest case of anticipatory rather than reactive policy — aimed at preventing China from reaching parity in advanced semiconductor nodes.

## Cross-Case Comparison

### 5.1 Does Rhetoric Consistently Follow Displacement?

In all four cases, significant rhetorical escalation follows rather than precedes the primary displacement milestone. The only partial exception is Sullivan's "as large a lead as possible" statement (September 2022), which preceded TechInsights' confirmation of SMIC's 7nm capability (August–September 2023) — though it was a response to cumulative evidence of Chinese semiconductor progress rather than a specific dated milestone. Table 1 summarises the milestone-to-rhetoric lags.

### 5.2 Do Policy Interventions Precede or Follow Market Leadership?

In every case, comprehensive protective or retaliatory policy was implemented *after* the challenger had already achieved market leadership in the relevant sector. Britain never enacted general tariffs against Germany at all. The United States imposed VERs on Japanese automobiles one year after Japan became the world's top producer. Section 301 tariffs against China followed the manufacturing crossover by eight years. The single genuine departure from this pattern is the October 2022 BIS semiconductor export control package, which targeted Chinese advanced chip capabilities not yet fully acquired — an instrument of technological denial rather than reaction. This represents a qualitative shift in incumbent strategy, from managing displacement after the fact to preventing its occurrence.

### 5.3 Speed of Response Across Cases

Response speed has compressed by approximately one order of magnitude over 150 years. Britain's slowest response — general tariff protection — never materialised within the period. Its education reforms arrived 5–9 years after commission recommendations. US automotive VERs took one year; semiconductor agreements 4–5 years; the Omnibus Trade Act 7–8 years from first Japanese DRAM dominance. Korean semiconductor retaliation arrived within approximately one year of Samsung's market leadership. China EV tariffs arrived within six months of BYD's Q4 2023 crossover.

This compression reflects institutional layering: each episode produced new legal instruments — Section 301 (1974, strengthened 1988), CFIUS review under Exon-Florio (1988), Super 301 (1988), Entity List (formalised 1990s), BIS advanced controls (expanded 2018–2022) — that reduced the friction between recognising displacement and implementing policy. The institutional infrastructure built in response to Japan was deployed more rapidly against Korea, and the apparatus refined against Korea and China enabled near-instantaneous responses by 2023–24.

### 5.4 Stability of the Rhetorical Taxonomy

The rhetorical taxonomy is stable across all four cases and all 150 years of the dataset (Table 2). The copying/fraud category appears in every case: German trademark imitation in the 1880s–90s, Japanese reverse engineering in the 1970s–80s, Samsung's licensed technology in the 1980s, and Chinese cyber espionage and forced technology transfer in the 2010s–20s. The dumping category appears in every case without exception. The structural/systemic model critique intensifies across cases: German state education and railway subsidies gave way to "Japan Inc." and MITI's developmental state, then the Korean chaebol and directed credit, then the Chinese "systemic rival" with state capitalism at scale. The national security frame consistently unlocks the most aggressive policy tools.

| B: Dumping/unfair | ✓ Williams (1896) | ✓ Micron (1985) | ✓ Micron (1992) | ✓ Von der Leyen (2023) |
| C: Structural/systemic | ✓ Chamberlain (1903) | ✓ Prestowitz (1988); Fallows (1989) | ✓ Micron CVD (2002) | ✓ EU Joint Communication (2019) |
| D: National security | ✓ Chamberlain (1903) — failed | ✓ DSB (1987) — succeeded | — | ✓ Sullivan (2022) — succeeded |

A pattern is visible across cases: incumbents typically begin with narrow, legalistic framing (fraud, dumping) and escalate to structural and security framing as displacement deepens and more aggressive policy instruments become politically necessary. The national security frame — absent in the Korean case at state level, failed in the British case politically — consistently unlocks the instruments that trade and commercial frames cannot justify: export controls, entity lists, investment screening, and industrial subsidy programmes framed as defence spending.

---

## Conclusion

Three findings emerge with consistency across the full dataset.

**Incumbents do not act preventively.** Across all four cases, comprehensive countermeasures arrived after market leadership was lost in at least one major sector. The October 2022 BIS semiconductor export controls are the only instrument in the dataset explicitly designed to *prevent* displacement — a structural break from 150 years of reactive behaviour, enabled by the security framing articulated in the Sullivan doctrine and the institutional infrastructure built across previous episodes.

**The rhetorical escalation ladder is stable.** The four-category taxonomy — fraud/copying, dumping, structural critique, national security framing — appears in all four cases regardless of the political system, economic structure, or historical context. The sequence within cases is also consistent: legal-commercial categories precede structural-strategic ones. This stability suggests the pattern reflects structural features of how incumbents process competitive threats rather than contingent ideological or political factors.

**The response cycle has compressed by roughly one order of magnitude.** What took a decade or more in the Victorian case took years in the Japan case, approximately one year in the semiconductor dimension of the Korea case, and weeks to months in the most recent China cases. This compression is not evidence that incumbents have learned to anticipate threats: the sequence remains the same. It reflects, rather, the accumulated institutional architecture — investigation procedures, tariff lists, entity lists, export control regimes — that makes the translate-threat-to-policy cycle faster, not earlier.
