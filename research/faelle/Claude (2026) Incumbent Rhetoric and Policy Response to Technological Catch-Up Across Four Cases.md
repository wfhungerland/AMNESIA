# Incumbent Rhetoric and Policy Response to Technological Catch-Up Across Four Cases

**Abstract**

This analysis tests the hypothesis that incumbent industrial powers frame competitor catch-up as illegitimate and respond with trade or technology restrictions following a recurrent structural pattern. It examines four episodes: Britain confronting German industrialisation (1870–1914), the United States confronting Japanese catch-up (1970–1990), the United States and Japan confronting Korean catch-up (1980–1997), and the United States and European Union confronting Chinese catch-up (2005–2024). For each case, documented statements from parliamentary debates, trade commission reports, and government investigations are mapped against technological milestones and policy interventions. The evidence supports three findings: first, rhetorical escalation consistently lags measurable technological displacement; second, terminology evolves predictably from accusations of fraud and copying toward systemic critiques of the challenger's economic model; third, policy interventions respond to established competitive displacement rather than preventing it, and in several documented instances produce effects contrary to their stated objectives.

---

## 1. Introduction

The recurring proposition that technological catch-up by emerging industrial powers provokes a characteristic sequence of rhetorical delegitimisation and defensive policy intervention is amenable to empirical evaluation. If the pattern is structural rather than contingent, it should appear across cases that differ substantially in geography, period, and institutional context. The four cases examined here span roughly 150 years, involve incumbents operating under different trade policy regimes, and address challenges from countries at varying stages of industrial development. The analytical framework maps three variables for each case: the timing and language of rhetoric directed at the catching-up country, the timing of measurable technological milestones, and the timing and content of policy responses. The central test is whether rhetoric and policy follow from technological success rather than preceding it.

## 2. Case I: Britain and German Industrialisation, 1870–1914

### 2.1 Technological trajectory

Germany's unification in 1871 accelerated an industrialisation process already underway. Between 1870 and 1913, Germany's share of world manufacturing output rose from 13 to 16 percent while Britain's fell from 32 to 14 percent (Bairoch 1982). The critical sectoral milestone was German steel production, which surpassed Britain's in 1893; by 1914 Germany produced more than twice as much steel as Britain (Britannica 1998). By 1877 Germany accounted for half of world synthetic dyestuff production; by 1913 eight leading German firms collectively produced almost 90 percent of the world supply (Wikipedia 2025a). Whereas only one-third of German exports in 1873 were finished goods, that proportion reached 63 percent by 1913, indicating structural transformation from resource exporter to sophisticated manufacturer.

### 2.2 Rhetoric: language and timing

British anxiety about German commercial competition was documented before technological parity was achieved but intensified sharply after it. The 1887 Merchandise Marks Act debates in the House of Commons focused on specific legal violations: German manufacturers were accused of false labelling, specifically the practice of marking goods as Sheffield-made when they were produced in Solingen or other German centres. Parliamentary language centred on "fraudulent practices," "false marking," and "illegitimate competition." The Act required imported goods to be marked with their country of origin, explicitly targeting "Made in Germany" as a stigmatising device (Hansard 1897).

The intended stigma failed. As German manufacturing quality improved through the 1890s, the label became commercially advantageous. Ernest Edwin Williams's 1896 bestseller *Made in Germany* acknowledged genuine German superiority directly: "German manufacturers, notably those in which artistic finish is needed, are undeniably superior" (Williams 1896). The publication was not a government report but a piece of popular economic journalism that reflected and amplified a transformation of public discourse from fraud to structural competitive threat. The timing is instructive: the "Made in Germany" press panic of 1896 arrived three years after steel parity, and thirteen years after Sheffield manufacturers had first petitioned Parliament about German competition.

By 1903, rhetoric had escalated to the systemic level. Joseph Chamberlain's Birmingham and Glasgow speeches launched the Tariff Reform campaign with an explicit industrial-decline narrative: "Sugar is gone. Silk has gone. Iron is threatened. Wool is threatened. Cotton will go!" (Chamberlain 1903, cited in Ashley 1920). The Tariff Reform League, founded the same year, deployed an emerging genre of incumbent rhetoric: the claim that Britain's adherence to free trade was asymmetric, since Germany maintained protective tariffs while simultaneously penetrating British markets (Wikipedia 2025b).

The language trajectory follows a clear arc: specific legal fraud (1887) → acknowledged superior quality requiring structural explanation (1896) → systemic indictment of the challenger's economic model and policy asymmetry (1903).

### 2.3 Policy responses and their effects

The Merchandise Marks Act (1887) was the only substantive trade response Britain deployed during the main period of German catch-up. It required country-of-origin labelling and criminalised false marking. Harvey (2021) finds no evidence the Act arrested import penetration and strong evidence it damaged Britain's entrepôt trade by complicating transit goods. The Act's principal effect was the unintended creation of the "Made in Germany" brand.

The Tariff Reform campaign, which might have provided more direct trade protection, was politically defeated in the 1906 Liberal landslide. Free-trade ideology, organised consumer interests, and the political arithmetic of working-class voters who associated tariffs with higher food prices proved insurmountable. Britain did not adopt systematic protective tariffs until the Ottawa Agreements of 1932, by which point Germany's share of world manufacturing had long exceeded Britain's. The policy gap between rhetorical escalation (1903) and substantive protection (1932) was nearly three decades, during which German industrial dominance in chemicals, electrical equipment, and precision instruments was consolidated.

### 2.4 Chronological mapping

| Event | Year |
|---|---|
| German steel parity with Britain | 1893 |
| "Made in Germany" panic in British press | 1896 |
| Tariff Reform League founded; systematic protectionist rhetoric | 1903 |
| Liberal landslide defeats Tariff Reform | 1906 |
| Ottawa Agreements introduce protective tariffs | 1932 |

**Lag from technological parity to rhetorical peak:** approximately 3 years. **Lag from parity to policy:** approximately 39 years. The policy response was not merely reactive but largely absent, consistent with the structural constraints of Britain's free-trade political economy.

## 3. Case II: The United States and Japanese Catch-Up, 1970–1990

### 3.1 Technological trajectory

Japan's export-led industrialisation achieved consumer electronics and automotive parity with the United States by the early-to-mid 1970s. The critical milestone in automobiles was quality: J.D. Power surveys and Consumer Reports assessments documented Japanese superiority in reliability over US manufacturers by approximately 1975–1978. In semiconductors, Japan's NEC, Hitachi, Toshiba, and Fujitsu collectively captured the majority of global DRAM production by 1982, displacing American producers including Intel (which exited DRAM production in 1985) and Micron. Japan's semiconductor equipment industry achieved comparable ascendancy in photolithography by 1985.

### 3.2 Rhetoric: language and timing

American trade rhetoric directed at Japan escalated in two distinct phases corresponding to two sectoral challenges.

In automobiles, political mobilisation intensified following the second oil shock (1979) and the simultaneous collapse of US auto market share. Congressional hearings in 1979–1980 featured testimony from United Automobile Workers president Douglas Fraser warning of "industrial devastation" and demanding import quotas. The language at this stage combined economic anxiety with cultural anxiety: Ezra Vogel's 1979 book *Japan as Number One* framed Japanese industrial organisation as a systemic model that the United States needed to study and emulate, or defeat. The idiom was competitive rather than criminal.

In semiconductors, rhetoric shifted toward accusations of deliberate market distortion. The Semiconductor Industry Association's June 1985 Section 301 petition to the USTR accused Japan of dumping memory chips below cost and systematically excluding US firms from the Japanese market through keiretsu procurement networks. The petition's language explicitly distinguished legal violations from general competitive pressure: "Japan's targeting of the semiconductor industry" was framed as state-directed predation inconsistent with market principles (SIA 1985, cited in Flamm 1996).

The systemic register intensified through the decade. Theodore White's 1985 *New York Times Magazine* essay characterised Japan's economic offensive as "one of history's most brilliant commercial offensives, as they go about dismantling American industry" and invoked World War II analogies explicitly. Clyde Prestowitz's 1988 *Trading Places* coined the "Japan Inc." formulation, presenting Japanese industrial policy and keiretsu networks as an integrated economic warfare system. The Structural Impediments Initiative negotiations (1989–1990) represented the culmination of this systemic critique: the United States demanded changes not to specific trade practices but to Japanese savings rates, land-use regulations, distribution systems, and retail laws — the entire institutional architecture of the Japanese economy (Tyson 1992).

The language trajectory again follows the pattern: fraud and rule violation (1985 Section 301 petition) → competitive anxiety with systemic explanation (Japan Inc., 1988) → comprehensive institutional critique (SII, 1989–1990).

### 3.3 Policy responses and their effects

**Voluntary Export Restraints on automobiles (1981–1994).** Negotiated in May 1981 at the initiative of the Reagan administration, which sought industry protection without formal tariff legislation, the VER initially limited Japanese auto exports to 1.68 million units annually. The PERC estimates the VER imposed costs equivalent to a tariff of approximately 25 percent and transferred roughly $3–5 billion annually from American consumers to Japanese producers during the peak years (Crandall 1987). Japanese automakers responded by moving upmarket — Honda launched Acura in 1986, Toyota launched Lexus in 1989, and Nissan launched Infiniti in 1989 — and by establishing US transplant manufacturing. Honda's Marysville, Ohio plant opened in 1982; Nissan, Toyota, and other manufacturers followed. The VER also opened market space vacated by Japanese export restraint for Korean manufacturers: Hyundai entered the US market with the Excel in 1986, and no VERs were ever imposed on Korean auto exports (Irwin 2017).

**The 1986 US-Japan Semiconductor Trade Agreement.** Following the Section 301 petition and a USITC investigation that found dumping margins of 10–40 percent on Japanese DRAMs, the Reagan administration negotiated the September 1986 agreement. MITI administered production discipline among Japanese firms and monitored pricing. Flamm (1996) documents that the resulting price floors generated windfall profits of approximately $1.2 billion for Japanese DRAM exporters on 1M DRAM sales in 1988 alone. US computer manufacturers paid 30–40 percent premiums over European competitors. American DRAM producers did not re-enter commodity memory manufacturing in any sustained way. Most consequentially, MITI-enforced production cutbacks created market space that Korean producers occupied: Samsung, Hyundai, and LG Semicon expanded DRAM production aggressively through 1986–1992, the very period the agreement was designed to protect American producers (Bown 2020).

**Section 301 and Special 301 mechanisms.** The 1988 Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act strengthened Section 301 and created Special 301, requiring the USTR to identify countries with inadequate intellectual property protection. Japan was placed on the Priority Watch List. The SII negotiations (1989–1990) produced Japanese commitments across seventeen structural areas. The mechanism was novel in its scope — demanding institutional transformation rather than specific trade concessions — and set a precedent applied in subsequent cases.

### 3.4 Chronological mapping

| Event | Year |
|---|---|
| Japanese automotive quality superiority documented | ~1975–1978 |
| Japan captures DRAM majority production | ~1982 |
| Intel exits DRAM production | 1985 |
| SIA Section 301 petition | June 1985 |
| US-Japan Semiconductor Trade Agreement | September 1986 |
| Automobile VER negotiated | May 1981 |
| Structural Impediments Initiative | 1989–1990 |

**Lag from automotive parity to VER:** approximately 3–6 years. **Lag from DRAM parity to Semiconductor Agreement:** approximately 4 years.

## 4. Case III: The United States and Japan Responding to Korean Catch-Up, 1980–1997

### 4.1 Technological trajectory

South Korea's semiconductor industry emerged from essentially zero capability in 1983, when Samsung made its initial 64Kb DRAM announcement. In 1984 Samsung reduced the technological gap with first-world producers from approximately a decade to four years, having licensed technology from Micron Technology. The catch-up thereafter was rapid: Samsung developed the world's first 64Mb DRAM in October 1992, achieving the top position in global DRAM market share the same year, and developed the world's first 256Mb DRAM in August 1994 (Samsung Semiconductor 2025). By 1993 Samsung had become the global leader in the memory semiconductor industry overall, displacing Japanese producers who had themselves displaced American producers a decade earlier.

### 4.2 Rhetoric: language and timing

American and Japanese rhetoric directed at Korean semiconductor producers was more muted than the Japan case and remained predominantly in the legal-violation register rather than escalating to systemic critique. This is explicable by two factors: Korea remained embedded in the US security alliance system, and the scale and technological sophistication of the Korean challenge — though remarkable — was initially narrower than Japan's across-the-board industrial advancement.

The USTR's 1985 Section 301 investigations into Korean intellectual property protection and insurance market access characterised Korea's copyright regime as "virtually non-existent" (USTR 1985, cited in infojustice.org 2013). Micron Technology's dumping complaints of the late 1980s accused Korean producers of reverse engineering proprietary DRAM designs and selling below cost to gain market share; trade press characterised Samsung's early 256Kb DRAM as a product of "extensive reverse engineering" of Micron's process (EDN 1993). The language was legal and specific: dumping margin calculations, intellectual property violations, predatory pricing — not "Korea Inc." systemic critique.

Japan's response to Korean competition was expressed primarily through the technology licensing and access disputes that characterised the Japan-Korea semiconductor supply chain relationship through the 1990s. Japanese materials and equipment suppliers maintained dominance over high-purity chemicals and photolithography equipment, giving Japanese firms leverage that was exercised through pricing rather than public rhetoric.

### 4.3 Policy responses and their effects

**Anti-dumping duties on Korean DRAMs (1993–1999).** Micron Technology filed anti-dumping petitions; the Department of Commerce issued a final duty order in May 1993 on DRAMs of one megabit and above from Korea. Duty margins varied substantially by producer (Samsung near zero, Hyundai higher). Samsung achieved de minimis status effectively by 1996. Hyundai Electronics announced plans to build a DRAM fabrication facility in Eugene, Oregon, partly to circumvent the duties — a US-produced chip would not be subject to them (Bown 2020). Korean DRAM global market share continued rising throughout the period duties were in force.

**Intellectual property pressure and GSP conditionality.** The USTR deployed GSP graduation threats to extract Korean IP protection commitments. Korea enacted copyright reforms in 1986 and strengthened patent law in 1987, partly in response to US pressure. The Brooklyn Law Review (1996) documents that Korea's copyright law evolution tracked US trade pressure closely through the late 1980s and early 1990s, with substantive reforms arriving in response to specific USTR complaints rather than autonomous domestic demand.

**Effect on the competitive trajectory.** Korean DRAM producers achieved world production leadership in 1992 and maintained it. The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis and subsequent IMF conditionality — an exogenous shock entirely unrelated to US trade policy — produced more structural adjustment in the Korean semiconductor industry than a decade of anti-dumping duties had achieved.

### 4.4 Chronological mapping

| Event | Year |
|---|---|
| Samsung-Micron technology licence agreement | 1983 |
| Samsung 64Mb DRAM: world first; #1 global DRAM market share | 1992 |
| Anti-dumping duty order on Korean DRAMs | May 1993 |
| Samsung 256Mb DRAM: world first | August 1994 |
| Samsung achieves de minimis dumping status | ~1996 |
| Hyundai announces Eugene, Oregon fab to circumvent duties | 1996 |
| Asian Financial Crisis: more structural adjustment than trade pressure | 1997 |

**Lag from DRAM technology leadership to anti-dumping order:** approximately 7 months. This is the shortest lag across all four cases, consistent with the institutional accumulation of anti-dumping infrastructure developed in prior episodes.

## 5. Case IV: The United States and EU Responding to Chinese Catch-Up, 2005–2024

### 5.1 Technological trajectory

China's technological catch-up spans multiple sectors with different timelines. In solar photovoltaics, China achieved global production dominance by approximately 2008–2010, accounting for approximately 80–95 percent of global solar module supply by 2024. In 5G telecommunications infrastructure, Huawei established global leadership by 2018–2019, accounting for 29 percent of global telecom equipment market share in early 2018, rising to 31 percent by 2024 (ITIF 2025). In electric vehicles, BYD became the world's largest EV seller in 2023. In semiconductors, China's position remains contested: SMIC produces at 7nm with significant yield limitations, while advanced logic at 3nm and below remains dependent on TSMC.

### 5.2 Rhetoric: language and timing

The US-China case exhibits the most elaborate and formally documented rhetorical escalation. The terminology has evolved through at least six identifiable stages, each corresponding roughly to a technology or trade milestone.

**"Currency manipulation" (2005–2015).** The Treasury Department's semi-annual currency reports designated China a currency manipulator from 2005 onward. The framing was macroeconomic and mercantilist rather than sector-specific, attributing Chinese export competitiveness to exchange rate policy rather than productivity.

**"Market access barriers" and "forced technology transfer" (2015–2018).** The 2015 USTR "Made in China 2025" analysis documented sector-specific industrial policy targets and licensing requirements that the USTR characterised as coercive technology transfer mechanisms. Section 301 investigations initiated in August 2017 produced the March 2018 report finding that China's technology transfer regime constituted "unreasonable and discriminatory" practices. The 2018 Section 301 report enumerated specific legal violations: mandatory joint venture requirements, foreign equity caps, and cyber-enabled trade secret theft (USTR 2018).

**"Systemic rival" (2019).** The EU-China Strategic Outlook, published 12 March 2019, marked a formal terminological shift by the European Commission, designating China simultaneously as a partner, competitor, and "systemic rival promoting alternative models of governance" (European Commission 2019). The "systemic rival" framing imported language previously used in security contexts into trade policy discourse, signalling that the challenge was characterised as structural and ideological rather than consisting of discrete rule violations.

**"De-risking" (2023).** European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen's address to the Mercator Institute for China Studies on 30 March 2023 introduced "de-risking" as the operative EU policy concept, distinguishing it from "decoupling" by accepting continued trade and investment with China while reducing exposure in strategically sensitive sectors. The speech explicitly addressed electric vehicles, solar panels, wind turbines, and advanced manufacturing as sectors requiring de-risking.

**"Overcapacity" (2023–2024).** US Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen's Beijing visit in April 2024 introduced "overcapacity" as the dominant rhetorical frame for Chinese EV, solar, and battery production, characterising the combination of state subsidies and domestic demand suppression as producing export volumes incompatible with global market equilibrium.

### 5.3 Policy responses and their effects

**Section 232 steel and aluminium tariffs (2018).** Imposed in March 2018 under national security authority, the tariffs covered $50 billion in Chinese steel and aluminium exports. Chinese retaliation targeted US agricultural exports, affecting soybean, pork, and grain producers. The tariffs remained in force through the Biden administration with modifications.

**Section 301 tariffs (2018–present).** The Trump administration imposed tariffs covering approximately $370 billion in Chinese imports across four tranches in 2018–2019. The Biden administration retained these tariffs and in May 2024 announced increases to 100 percent on Chinese EVs, 25 percent on batteries, and 50 percent on solar cells, effective September 2024.

**Huawei Entity List designation (May 2019).** The Department of Commerce added Huawei to the Entity List in May 2019, requiring US suppliers to obtain licences for exports to Huawei. The practical effect was to cut Huawei off from TSMC's advanced logic foundry services (implemented in September 2020 after rule extensions) and from Qualcomm, Intel, and Google software. Initial market reaction suggested severe damage to Huawei's smartphone business, which lost access to Google Mobile Services and Android updates.

The medium-term effects departed from the stated objective of degrading Huawei's technological capability. In August 2023, Huawei released the Mate 60 Pro smartphone equipped with the domestically manufactured 7nm Kirin 9000S chip, produced by SMIC using equipment predating the export controls. CSIS analysis (2023) found that "the October 2022 policy has thus far failed to degrade SMIC's peak technological capability." The ITIF's October 2025 report, *Backfire: Export Controls Helped Huawei and Hurt U.S. Firms*, documents that Huawei's global telecom equipment market share rose from 29 to 31 percent between 2018 and 2024, while US technology companies including Intel, Qualcomm, and Teradyne lost more than $33 billion in sales to Huawei between 2021 and 2024 (ITIF 2025). MERICS (2024) reports that Huawei received 215 billion yuan ($30 billion) in funding from central and Shenzhen government sources since 2021 to build chip fabrication capacity and support domestic semiconductor ecosystem firms — a figure substantially larger than the direct government subsidies Huawei disclosed in financial filings. Rhodium Group (2024) found that controls accelerated Chinese indigenisation of the semiconductor supply chain.

**EU anti-dumping duties on Chinese solar panels (2013–2018).** Initiated by the EU's largest solar manufacturer, SolarWorld, the anti-dumping investigation found dumping margins of 11.8–47.6 percent and resulted in provisional duties of 11.8 percent in June 2013, rising to definitive duties averaging 47.6 percent in December 2013. The measures expired in September 2018 without renewal after downstream solar installation industries lobbied against extension. SolarWorld filed for insolvency in May 2017, during the period of active tariff protection (CEPR/VoxEU 2013; Ecipe 2013). China's global solar module market share continued rising throughout and stands at approximately 80–95 percent in 2024.

**EU anti-dumping duties on Chinese EVs (2024).** Following an investigation initiated in October 2023, the EU imposed provisional anti-dumping duties ranging from 17.4 to 38.1 percent on Chinese-made EVs in July 2024 (additional to an existing 10 percent MFN tariff). The investigation found subsidies across 12 categories including preferential loans, equity injections, and preferential purchasing. China retaliated with anti-dumping investigations into EU brandy imports and restrictions on EU pork.

**CHIPS and Science Act (August 2022).** Appropriating $52.7 billion in semiconductor manufacturing subsidies and R&D funding, the CHIPS Act represented a structural departure from prior incumbent responses: offensive industrial policy aimed at rebuilding domestic capability rather than restricting the challenger. Recipients are prohibited from expanding manufacturing in China for a defined period. By March 2024, the Commerce Department had announced investments totalling approximately $160–200 billion in projected semiconductor manufacturing across more than 25 projects. The Act was explicitly a response to China's Big Fund (National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund) Phase I (established 2014) and Phase II (established October 2019) and to the "Made in China 2025" strategy (2015) — all of which predated the US response by several years.

| Event | Year |
|---|---|
| China solar manufacturing dominance achieved | ~2008–2010 |
| EU ProSun anti-dumping complaint filed | July 2012 |
| EU provisional anti-dumping duties on Chinese solar | June 2013 |
| Huawei achieves 29% global telecom equipment market share | 2018 |
| USTR Section 301 report on China technology practices | March 2018 |

| US 100% tariff on Chinese EVs | September 2024 |

**Lag from solar dominance to EU duties:** approximately 3–5 years. **Lag from Huawei 5G leadership to Entity List:** approximately 1 year. **Lag from EV market leadership to US tariff escalation:** approximately 1 year.

## 6. Comparative Analysis

### 6.1 The structural sequence confirmed

Across all four cases, the sequence is: technological displacement → rhetorical escalation → policy intervention. In no case does major policy intervention precede technological displacement. In no case does rhetoric reach its maximum intensity before the challenging country has achieved parity or superiority in the contested sector.

The lag between parity and policy has compressed from roughly three to four decades (Britain-Germany) to approximately one year (US-China EVs and 5G). This compression reflects the accumulation of institutional infrastructure — anti-dumping statutes, Section 301 mechanisms, Entity Lists — and the faster propagation of competitive intelligence. The structural dynamic, however, is unchanged.

### 6.2 The rhetorical arc

Rhetorical language follows a predictable evolutionary arc across cases. Initial accusations focus on discrete legal violations: trademark fraud (Britain-Germany), copyright infringement (US-Korea), dumping below cost (US-Japan semiconductors, US-Korea DRAMs). As technological catch-up is consolidated and cannot be attributed to rule-breaking, rhetoric escalates to systemic characterisation: "Japan Inc.," "systemic rival," "overcapacity." Each escalation corresponds to the incumbent's recognition that the challenge reflects genuine productive capability rather than market distortion.

The distinction between accusations of specific legal violations and general competitive pressure is critical for assessing the rhetorical function of incumbent discourse. Legal accusations provide actionable grounds for WTO-compatible trade remedies and carry evidentiary requirements. Systemic accusations are less legally actionable but more rhetorically effective in building domestic political coalitions for protection. The shift from legal to systemic language thus marks the transition from trade-remedy utilisation to industrial policy mobilisation.

### 6.3 Policy effectiveness

The empirical record on policy effectiveness is unfavourable to the interventionist position across all four cases.

Britain's Merchandise Marks Act inadvertently created the "Made in Germany" brand. The Tariff Reform campaign was politically defeated. Systematic British protection arrived in 1932, after German industrial dominance was consolidated (Harvey 2021).

American VERs on Japanese automobiles pushed Japanese manufacturers upmarket and into US transplant production, while opening market space for Korean entrants. The 1986 Semiconductor Agreement generated Japanese windfall profits, disadvantaged US computer manufacturers relative to European competitors, and facilitated Korean DRAM expansion. US DRAM producers did not recover commodity memory manufacturing (Flamm 1996).

Anti-dumping duties on Korean DRAMs produced circumvention through domestic production investment, achieved de minimis status within three years for the largest producer, and did not arrest Korean market share expansion (Bown 2020).

US export controls on Huawei produced the Mate 60 Pro, accelerated Chinese semiconductor supply chain indigenisation, and cost US firms over $33 billion in sales while Chinese producers filled the domestic market gap with inferior but improving domestic alternatives (ITIF 2025). EU solar anti-dumping duties expired without reversing Chinese solar dominance; SolarWorld went bankrupt during the protected period.

### 6.4 The unintended-consequences mechanism

Three recurring mechanisms produce effects contrary to stated objectives.

First, **trade barriers redirect rather than eliminate competition**. VERs pushed Japanese automakers upmarket and into transplant manufacturing. Anti-dumping duties on Korean chips prompted domestic US fab investment. Chinese tariffs redirected exports through Vietnam, Mexico, and ASEAN: China's goods trade surplus rose from approximately $992 billion in 2024 to almost $1.2 trillion in 2025 despite US tariffs reducing direct Sino-American trade volumes (NPR/AP 2026).

Second, **restrictions on one challenger create market space for the next**. The US-Japan Semiconductor Agreement directly facilitated Korean DRAM entry by constraining Japanese output and maintaining elevated prices. VERs on Japanese automobiles opened the US market to Hyundai. No systematic account of post-1986 Korean semiconductor expansion can omit the role of Japanese production restraint.

Third, **technology denial accelerates indigenous innovation**. The Huawei Entity List designation prompted the Chinese government to designate semiconductor self-sufficiency a national security priority. Through Big Fund Phase II (October 2019) and direct industrial policy, China invested substantially in domestic semiconductor capital equipment and EDA tools. Huawei's HiSilicon chip design subsidiary had prepared contingency plans for US supply disruption; the "spare tire" strategy, announced the day after the Entity List designation, was not improvised but had been in preparation for years (ITIF 2025). The historical parallel is instructive: German chemical firms, initially reliant on appropriating British discoveries, developed world-leading original research capabilities under competitive and institutional pressures in the 1870s and 1880s; by 1913 their eight largest firms collectively produced almost 90 percent of global dyestuff supply (Wikipedia 2025a).

### 6.5 Variables explaining cross-case deviation

Three variables introduce case-specific deviation from the baseline pattern.

**Political feasibility of protection.** Britain's free-trade political economy and the franchise extension of 1867 made tariff reform politically costly in ways that American institutional structures did not replicate. The United States possessed both anti-dumping infrastructure and the Section 301 mechanism, enabling more rapid deployment of trade remedies. The EU operates through the Common Commercial Policy with formal anti-dumping procedures and is more constrained by WTO commitments than the United States in unilateral action.

**Security alliance structure.** Japan and Korea accepted trade concessions — VERs, Semiconductor Agreement, GSP conditionality — that were partly enabled by their security dependence on the United States. China as a strategic competitor faces no equivalent leverage, which partially explains the escalation from negotiated agreements to unilateral technology denial.

**Scope of industrial policy capacity in the challenging country.** The MITI-keiretsu model in Japan, the chaebol-state relationship in Korea, and the party-state-enterprise system in China each provide different capacities to absorb and respond to external pressure. China's system, combining direct state ownership, national security designation of semiconductor development, and effectively unlimited fiscal capacity to support industrial champions, provides a qualitatively greater capacity to sustain catch-up under adversarial conditions than Japan or Korea commanded.

### 6.6 Testing the core hypotheses

**Hypothesis 1: Did rhetoric intensify after measurable technological success?**

Confirmed in all four cases. The timing evidence is unambiguous: British alarm peaked after German steel and chemical dominance was established; American anti-Japan semiconductor rhetoric peaked after Japanese DRAM dominance; anti-Korea dumping petitions arrived after Samsung achieved DRAM market leadership; "systemic rival" and "overcapacity" terminology emerged after Chinese solar, 5G, and EV market positions were consolidated.

**Hypothesis 2: Did interventions prevent or merely respond to catch-up?**

In no case did the principal policy intervention prevent catch-up. British measures did not reverse German industrial displacement. VERs and the Semiconductor Agreement did not restore American semiconductor or automotive competitive positions. Anti-dumping duties did not arrest Korean DRAM market share expansion. Export controls have not degraded Huawei's peak semiconductor capability and appear to have accelerated Chinese supply chain indigenisation. The only partial exception is the CHIPS Act, which is offensive industrial policy rather than defensive trade restriction and remains too recent to evaluate definitively; its stated objective is domestic capability building rather than challenger restriction, and it was itself a lagged response to Chinese industrial policy initiatives of 2014–2015.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The evidence across four cases spanning 150 years supports a structural account of incumbent response to technological catch-up. Rhetorical escalation is a lagging indicator: its intensity peaks after competitive displacement is established, not before. Language evolves predictably from specific legal accusations toward systemic characterisation of the challenger's economic model, tracking the incumbent's recognition that competitive challenge reflects genuine productive advantage. Policy interventions arrive after technological displacement is established and in documented cases produce effects contrary to stated objectives through redirection, substitution, and acceleration of indigenous innovation in the challenging country.

The compression of the lag from parity to policy intervention — from roughly forty years in the Britain-Germany case to approximately one year in the US-China EV and 5G cases — reflects institutional accumulation and faster competitive intelligence rather than any fundamental change in the underlying dynamic. The political function of rhetoric directed at catching-up competitors appears to be domestic coalition construction for protection rather than accurate description of the competitive challenge. As challengers progress from copying to genuine innovation, the legal frame of fraud and IP theft gives way to systemic indictments of state capitalism and overcapacity — preserving the rhetorical delegitimisation while updating its vocabulary.
