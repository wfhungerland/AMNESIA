# Measuring Institutional Permissiveness Toward Technology Acquisition in Catch-Up Economies

*This paper argues that institutional permissiveness toward technology acquisition can be measured quantitatively for the postwar East Asian catch-ups through a constructible composite, but not for Germany's 1840–1914 catch-up, so the routine practice of proxying permissiveness by inverting protection-strength indices fails precisely in the case that most needs it.*

## Key Takeaways

1. No existing index measures permissiveness directly; the nearest usable proxy inverts the Ginarte-Park patent-rights index, which begins only in 1960 and therefore cannot reach any of the German catch-up (Ginarte & Park 1997; Park 2008).
2. For the postwar cases a regression-ready composite is constructible from four openly available panels: inverted Ginarte-Park scores, World Bank IP-payment flows, OECD technology-sector FDI-restrictiveness scores, and the Fraser property-rights sub-index (Park 2008; World Bank 2025; OECD 2024; Gwartney, Lawson & Murphy 2023).
3. Germany 1840–1914 is served only by fragmentary structured series, namely Clemens-Williamson tariffs, Polity scores, Bloomfield capital flows, and DPMA patent counts from 1877, so its permissiveness must be hand-coded from legal texts rather than read off an index (Clemens & Williamson 2004; Marshall & Gurr 2020; Bloomfield 1968; Deutsches Patent- und Markenamt 2024).

## Introduction

Late-industrialising economies typically catch up by absorbing foreign technology before they generate it, and the institutions that tolerate imitation are as consequential for that process as the ones that later protect innovation.
The empirical study of catch-up therefore needs a measure of institutional permissiveness, meaning the degree to which a state's laws and administrative practice allowed domestic firms to copy, reverse-engineer, license on favourable terms, or otherwise absorb frontier technology.
No such measure exists as a purpose-built index, and researchers routinely substitute the inverse of a protection-strength index instead.
This paper assesses whether that substitution is defensible across the four canonical catch-up episodes, Germany (1840–1914), Japan (1950–1990), South Korea (1960–1997), and China (1978–2024), by inventorying the quantifiable sources that actually exist for each and testing how far back a credible permissiveness measure can be built.
The finding is asymmetric: the postwar cases support a genuine composite, while the German case does not, and the gap falls exactly where the inverted-index shortcut is most often invoked.

## The Measurement Problem: Permissiveness Is Not Protection Inverted

Permissiveness and protection are related but not reciprocal, and conflating them imports a specific error into catch-up research.
A protection index scores how strongly a state defends incumbent rights; the leading cross-country series, the Ginarte-Park index, rates patent-law strength on a 0 to 5 scale across five components for 110 countries from 1960 (Ginarte & Park 1997), extended to 122 countries through 2005 (Park 2008).
Its restrictions component, which captures provisions such as compulsory licensing, is the single dimension that most nearly measures tolerated imitation, and inverting it yields a defensible permissiveness proxy from 1960 onward.
The whole-index inversion is weaker, because it treats the statutory absence of product patents as equivalent to weak enforcement, whereas the two operate on absorption through different channels.
That distinction is not academic: enforcement strength diverges sharply from statutory strength in exactly the catch-up cases, so a de jure measure must be paired with a de facto one, for which the Papageorgiadis-Sofka enforcement index provides annual scores for 51 countries over 1998 to 2017 (Papageorgiadis & Sofka 2020).
Several of the most powerful permissiveness levers are, moreover, codeable directly from law rather than inferred from an index, including the exclusion of chemical and pharmaceutical product patents, a device used by Germany until the late 1960s, by Korea until 1987, and by China until its 1992 amendment, and documented as a recurrent catch-up strategy across the historical record (Chang 2002).

## What the Postwar Panels Deliver

For the three East Asian cases, permissiveness is measurable because the underlying flows were recorded contemporaneously and are now downloadable.
Patent behaviour is captured by WIPO and the national offices, with resident-to-non-resident filing ratios serving as a proxy for domestic absorption relative to foreign patenting, and by geolocated historical patent data that now extend the inventor-level record backward (Bergeaud & Verluise 2024; de Rassenfosse et al. 2019).
Arms-length technology transfer is captured directly by the World Bank series on charges for the use of intellectual property, which measures royalty and licence outflows and is regression-ready as a panel (World Bank 2025).
Investment openness, the institutional variable that Japan and Korea manipulated most deliberately, is captured by the OECD FDI Regulatory Restrictiveness Index, which scores statutory restrictions across 22 sectors and four policy categories for more than 100 countries, with an original series for 1997 to 2020 and a revised series for 2018 and 2021 to 2023 (OECD 2024).
The technology-forcing character of Japanese and Korean FDI restriction is itself the key institutional datum, since both states minimised inward equity investment precisely to compel licensing, a screening regime documented for Japan at the firm level, where the government sorted licensing applications by industry and prior experience between 1957 and 1970 (Mason 1992; Kiyota & Okazaki 2005).
Institutional-quality context is available from the Fraser Economic Freedom of the World index, which rates 165 jurisdictions annually from 2000 and quinquennially back to 1970 (Gwartney, Lawson & Murphy 2023), from the World Bank Worldwide Governance Indicators, covering more than 200 economies from 1996 (World Bank 2024), and from Polity, which reaches back to 1800 (Marshall & Gurr 2020).
State-coordinated technology programmes are also quantifiable rather than merely narrated: Japan's VLSI project ran from 1976 to 1980 on a budget of roughly USD 281 million, about half of it public, and produced over a thousand patents through a shared cooperative laboratory (Peck 1990; US Congress OTA 1991); China's 863 Programme was authorised at RMB 10 billion over fifteen years and financed at roughly RMB 11 billion across its first three plans, with the 2001 to 2005 phase alone receiving RMB 15 billion (Zhi & Pearson 2016); Korea's R&D financing shifted from a government-institute-led majority in the 1970s to a private share near 79 percent by 2020 (Statistics Korea 2024); and China's later industrial policy is traceable through the USD 47.5 billion third-phase semiconductor fund established in 2024 (Rhodium Group 2024).
These series combine into a single defensible composite from 1960, formed by inverted Ginarte-Park scores, IP-payment intensity, technology-sector FDI-restrictiveness scores, and the Fraser property-rights sub-index, each of which is open and panel-structured (Park 2008; World Bank 2025; OECD 2024; Gwartney, Lawson & Murphy 2023).

## Why Germany 1840–1914 Resists the Same Treatment

The German catch-up is the weakest case across every component, and no amount of aggregation closes the gap, because the instruments themselves start too late.
The permissiveness proxies that anchor the postwar composite are unavailable before 1960: the Ginarte-Park index does not reach the period, the FDI-restrictiveness index begins in 1997, the enforcement index in 1998, and the World Bank royalty series only in the postwar decades (Park 2008; OECD 2024; Papageorgiadis & Sofka 2020; World Bank 2025).
What survives for Germany is a handful of structured historical series that were built for other purposes: average tariff rates from the Clemens-Williamson database (Clemens & Williamson 2004), regime scores from Polity back to 1800 (Marshall & Gurr 2020), annual net capital-flow series for Britain, France, and Germany (Bloomfield 1968; Clemens & Williamson 2000), trade flows for 149 polities including Prussia (Federico & Tena-Junguito 2019), and patent counts from the Imperial Patent Office, which recorded 21,925 applications in 1900 and 49,532 in 1913 (Deutsches Patent- und Markenamt 2024), now supplemented by geolocated nineteenth-century patent data (Bergeaud & Verluise 2024).
Crucially, before the 1877 Patent Act Germany had no unified patent regime at all, so permissiveness in the earliest catch-up decades was near-maximal by construction and can be established only by coding the legal record, not by scoring an index.
Where direct measurement is impossible, the best available evidence is indirect, as in the use of world's-fair exhibition data to gauge innovation in jurisdictions that lacked patent protection (Moser 2005).
The German case thus inverts the postwar situation: the institutions are legible in law and narrative but illegible to the quantitative instruments, so any cross-case regression that includes Germany must rely on hand-coded provisions rather than an off-the-shelf score.

## The Compulsory-Licensing and Reverse-Engineering Blind Spots

Even within the well-served postwar period, the two channels most specific to catch-up permissiveness remain largely unmeasured.
Compulsory licensing, a direct instrument of tolerated imitation, is documented only for pharmaceuticals, where a verified database records 24 episodes across 17 nations covering 40 drug patents between 1995 and 2011 (Beall & Kuhn 2012), later extended in scope (Son & Lee 2018), with no equivalent panel for electronics, chemicals, or machinery.
Reverse-engineering legality, arguably the purest permissiveness variable, has no cross-country time series at all and must be coded from legal texts case by case.
These gaps matter because they fall on exactly the mechanisms through which Japanese, Korean, and Chinese firms absorbed frontier technology, so even the strongest composite understates the phenomenon it is meant to capture and does so asymmetrically across sectors.

## The Strongest Counterargument

The most serious objection is that the pre-1960 gap is overstated, because a discrete-event record already reaches deep into the nineteenth century.
Lerner's catalogue codes 177 patent-policy changes across 60 nations between 1852 and 1998, which on its face covers the German catch-up and could be spliced with the inverted Ginarte-Park series to yield a continuous cross-case measure (Lerner 2002).
On this view the inverted-index shortcut is serviceable, and the insistence on hand-coding is perfectionism.
The objection fails on the nature of what the two instruments encode.
Lerner records the direction of discrete legal shifts, strengthening or weakening, not the level or intensity of permissiveness, and the events are too few per country to support a panel, so the catalogue identifies when a regime moved but not how permissive it was at a given moment.
Inversion of a protection index compounds the problem by treating the statutory absence of product patents as interchangeable with weak enforcement, when the German chemical case shows these to be distinct absorption channels operating simultaneously (Chang 2002).
A measure that cannot separate "no product patent" from "unenforced product patent" cannot speak to the mechanism it is invoked to explain, which is why the German case still requires coding from the legal record rather than a spliced score.
*The thesis fails if a single pre-1960 cross-country index is shown to track de facto tolerance of imitation, rather than statutory protection strength, and validates against known permissive episodes such as Germany's chemical-process regime or Japan's postwar licensing screen.*

## Conclusion

Institutional permissiveness toward technology acquisition is measurable, but not uniformly, and the unevenness is systematic rather than incidental.
For Japan, Korea, and China the contemporaneous record supports a regression-ready composite from 1960, built from inverted protection scores, licensing flows, technology-sector investment restrictiveness, and property-rights context, and supplemented by quantifiable state-programme budgets and firm-level licensing data.
For Germany 1840–1914 the same instruments do not exist, the surviving series were built for other questions, and permissiveness must be recovered by coding tariffs, patent law, and capital flows directly.
The practical implication is that the common shortcut, proxying permissiveness by inverting a protection index, is defensible for the postwar cases and misleading for the pre-1960 one, so cross-case designs should build the composite where the data allow it and hand-code where they do not, rather than extending a single inverted score across a boundary the underlying instruments cannot cross.
