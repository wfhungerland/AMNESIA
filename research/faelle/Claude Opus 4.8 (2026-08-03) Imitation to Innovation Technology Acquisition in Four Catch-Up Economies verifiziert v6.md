## Abstract

This paper systematically compares technology acquisition mechanisms in Germany (c.1840–1914), Japan (c.1950–1990), South Korea (c.1960–1997), and China (c.1978–2024). For each case it identifies the principal acquisition channels, specifies their legality under domestic and international law operative at the time, provides available quantitative indicators, and documents disputes with incumbent technology-leading economies. The analysis distinguishes proven illegal acts from alleged practices and from legal-but-controversial strategies. The historically recurrent mechanisms—reverse engineering, foreign talent recruitment, IP regime design, and government-coordinated R&D—appear across all four cases. Genuinely novel elements in the Chinese case are state-directed cyber-enabled espionage and large-scale outbound acquisition of foreign technology firms. The key discontinuity between China and its predecessors is not primarily one of mechanism type but of scale, simultaneity, and the binding multilateral IP framework (TRIPS/WTO) absent for earlier industrialisers during their most aggressive catch-up phases.

## 1. Introduction

The question of how late-industrialising economies close the technology gap with frontier incumbents is among the most consequential in development economics. Abraham's (1986) concept of "social capability" and Gerschenkron's (1962) thesis on the advantages of backwardness both predict that followers can achieve rapid catch-up by absorbing externally generated knowledge. The mechanisms through which this absorption occurs, and their legal and diplomatic consequences, differ substantially across cases and historical periods.

This paper constructs a structured four-case comparison. It applies a consistent definitional framework to seven categories of technology acquisition mechanism: (1) legal licensing agreements; (2) reverse engineering of legally purchased goods; (3) weak or strategically designed intellectual property enforcement; (4) conditional market-access requirements such as mandatory joint ventures; (5) recruitment of foreign or diaspora engineers; (6) government-coordinated research consortia; and (7) documented industrial espionage (judicially confirmed). For each case, legality is assessed under domestic law and under international agreements operative at the relevant time.

Evidence standards are strict: quantitative indicators are drawn from official sources (WIPO, OECD, national statistical offices) or peer-reviewed scholarship; espionage claims are reported only where judicial confirmation exists; and allegations without evidentiary confirmation are marked explicitly as such.

## 2. Case One: Germany, approximately 1840–1914

### 2.1 Early phase (c.1840–1870): absorption without IP constraint

Germany's industrialisation began in earnest after the Zollverein customs union (1834) reduced internal tariff barriers, but the most important immediate driver was the absorption of British machinery and craft knowledge (Landes 1969). British government policy from the late eighteenth century had prohibited both the emigration of skilled artisans and the export of machinery; the artisan emigration ban was repealed on 21 June 1824 following the recommendations of the Select Committee on Artizans and Machinery, while export controls on machinery remained in place, gradually liberalised between 1825 and 1843 (Henderson 1954; Bensimon 2023). Even before the formal lifting of these restrictions, a substantial flow of British mechanics reached Prussia and the Rhineland through smuggling, fee arrangements, and deliberate recruitment. At least 15,000 British workers were employed in France by 1830, chiefly in skilled technical roles in textiles and metalworking, and the flow into the German states was comparably significant (Bensimon 2023). Industrialists including Friedrich Harkort and August Borsig employed British engineers explicitly to transfer locomotive and steam-engine construction knowledge. Krupp engaged British mechanics from Sheffield to transfer steel-casting techniques (Landes 1969; Mokyr 1990).

No unified German patent law existed before 1877. Individual German states operated disparate patent regimes, many providing minimal protection to foreign patentees. Württemberg's patent administration actively discriminated against foreign applicants (Ritter and Warmuth 2019, published as *Journal of Economic History* paper on Württemberg patent law). Under these conditions, reverse engineering of legally purchased British equipment was not merely tolerated but structurally unconstrained—it was, in the terminology of this analysis, legal under domestic law and subject to no binding international obligation.

### 2.2 Middle phase (c.1870–1914): the patent system as industrial policy

The German Patent Act of 1877 created a unified national patent system but incorporated a critical design feature: chemical product patents were excluded entirely; only *process* patents for chemical synthesis were recognised. This single provision allowed the newly formed BASF, Bayer, and Hoechst to legally imitate British and French synthetic dye molecules while independently developing alternative manufacturing routes (Beer 1959; Murmann 2003). The exclusion of chemical product patents remained in force until 1968—a 91-year window that covered the entire period in which the German chemical industry rose to global dominance.

Germany joined the Paris Convention for the Protection of Industrial Property only in 1903, twenty years after its founding. During the preceding two decades, German firms operating across the French, Belgian, and Swiss markets were not bound by the convention's national-treatment and priority provisions. The 1877 Act required that patents be "worked" domestically within a specified period or face compulsory licensing—a provision that disadvantaged foreign patent holders who did not manufacture in Germany (Machlup and Penrose 1950).

**Institutional infrastructure.** The *Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt* (Imperial Physical-Technical Institute, established 1887) provided standardised measurement and testing infrastructure that reduced the cost of quality improvement in precision manufacturing. The *Technische Hochschulen* system linked industrial firms to applied science in a model that Chandler (1990) identifies as the archetypal "cooperative managerial capitalism." BASF, Bayer, and Hoechst all employed PhD chemists through university connections long before this practice was standard elsewhere.

### 2.3 Legal assessment

Reverse engineering of British machinery before 1843 was illegal under British law for those who facilitated the export, and British authorities periodically prosecuted recruiters. For the German recipients, domestic law imposed no prohibition. The trademark counterfeiting documented before the British Merchandise Marks Act (1887) constituted fraud under both British and, in principle, German commercial law. No major German firm faced foreign judicial proceedings for technology acquisition practices. Germany acceded to no binding multilateral IP treaty during its catch-up phase that would have constrained these mechanisms.

The British reaction was primarily commercial rather than diplomatic. The 1887 Merchandise Marks Act required "Made in Germany" labelling on imports after Sheffield cutlery manufacturers complained of trademark deception; Ernest Williams' *Made in Germany* (1896) documented competitive penetration across multiple sectors, but generated no inter-governmental IP dispute mechanisms comparable to twentieth-century instruments.

### 2.4 Quantitative indicators

Reliable R&D expenditure data for pre-1914 Germany are unavailable in modern national-accounts form. Proxy indicators include: German university research expenditure growing from an estimated 0.1% of GDP in the 1860s to roughly 0.3% by 1900 (Baten and Schulz 2005); German patent filings at the *Kaiserliches Patentamt* rising from 3,212 applications in the founding half-year of 1877 (190 patents granted) to 11,882 applications by 1890, and higher still by 1900; and German exports of chemical products rising from roughly £2 million in 1870 to over £40 million by 1913, displacing British dominance (Landes 1969). Technology balance of payments data does not exist for this period, but Germany was a net technology exporter in chemicals by the 1890s.

## 3. Case Two: Japan, approximately 1950–1990

### 3.1 Early phase (c.1950–1960): foreign exchange rationing as technology lever

Postwar Japan's technology acquisition was distinguished above all by the institutional architecture through which the Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) channelled and controlled foreign technology imports. Japan's Foreign Investment Law (1950) and the Foreign Capital Law required government approval for all technology licensing agreements, giving MITI effective monopsony power in negotiations with foreign technology suppliers (Johnson 1982). Between 1950 and 1981, Japan approved approximately 42,000 foreign technology import contracts (Odagiri and Goto 1993). MITI used this authority to force down royalty rates, extract package deals, and require full disclosure of underlying know-how.

The most consequential early transaction was the licensing of the transistor from Western Electric/AT&T in 1953. Sony paid $25,000 for the licence—a fraction of AT&T's development cost—and used it to develop consumer electronics applications that AT&T had not pursued (Odagiri and Goto 1993). Texas Instruments applied for fundamental semiconductor patents in Japan in 1960; MITI refused to process the application for six years, during which Japanese firms—Nippon Electric (NEC), Hitachi, Toshiba—reverse-engineered and commercialised TI's technology. When TI's application was eventually processed and granted, MITI compelled TI to accept a domestic market-share cap (reported at 10%) and licence its patents to Japanese firms at concessional royalty rates (Girouard 1995; Princeton University/OTA 1991). IBM was permitted to establish wholly-owned operations in Japan only after agreeing to broad licensing of its patents to Japanese competitors (Johnson 1982). [The specific figures — a 10% cap, ≤5% royalty, and ~1,500 IBM patents — derive from the monograph literature (Johnson 1982) and are not independently web-verifiable.]

These arrangements were legal under Japanese domestic law, which gave MITI broad discretionary authority under the Foreign Investment Law. They were consistent with the Paris Convention (which Japan had joined in 1899) but operated in an era before the TRIPS Agreement imposed obligations on compulsory licensing and patent examination timelines. The US government criticised them bilaterally from the late 1970s onward, but no binding international rule prohibited them at the time.

### 3.2 Middle phase (c.1960–1980): systematic absorption and the patent system as competitive weapon

Japanese firms developed an approach to the patent system that further reduced foreign firms' ability to obtain or enforce broad protection. Japanese patent law at this time featured: narrow claim interpretation requiring claims to specify exact embodiments; pre-grant opposition, allowing domestic competitors to challenge and delay foreign patent applications for years; and average examination pendency of six to seven years (compared with 19 months in the United States at the time) (Girouard 1995; GovInfo/OTP 2000). The practical effect was that US and European firms obtained narrower, later-effective patents in Japan than in their home markets. A 1993 GAO survey found that 65% of US companies reported pendency, cost, narrow claims, or inability to obtain broad "pioneering" patents as "very great" problems in the Japanese system (National Academies Press 1997).

Japanese firms complemented this defensive strategy with aggressive patent flooding: filing large numbers of incremental utility model applications to surround any foreign pioneer patent with a thicket of blocking applications. By the mid-1980s, Japanese patent applications exceeded 300,000 per year, with utility model applications routinely outnumbering invention patents.

The reverse engineering of legally purchased goods was systematic and openly practised. Odagiri and Goto (1993) document how Japanese electronics firms routinely purchased, disassembled, and redesigned foreign products within the protection afforded by narrow patent claims. IBM's 360-series mainframe architecture was reverse-engineered by Fujitsu and Hitachi in processes that insiders estimated required approximately six years (FAS/IRP 2023).

**VLSI Project (1976–1980).** The most celebrated instance of government-coordinated R&D was the Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI) Technology Research Association. Bringing together Fujitsu, Hitachi, Mitsubishi Electric, NEC, and Toshiba, the project ran from 1976 to 1980 with a total budget of approximately ¥70 billion (US$281 million at prevailing exchange rates), of which the government provided roughly 40% as subsidy (Asianometry 2023; Shmj.or.jp 2023). The project generated over 1,000 patents in semiconductor manufacturing and equipment, enabling Japan to achieve technical parity with and then surpass US firms in 64K DRAM production. Japan's global semiconductor market share rose from approximately 15% in the early 1970s to 50% by the mid-1980s (Wikipedia/Semiconductor Industry in Japan 2025). SEMATECH, founded in 1987 with US government backing, was explicitly modelled on the VLSI Project.

### 3.3 Confirmed illegal activity: the IBM-Hitachi case (1982)

In June 1982, a nine-month FBI undercover operation (Operation PENGEM) concluded with the arrest of Hitachi engineers who had paid approximately $612,000 to FBI undercover agents posing as officials of a fictitious California consulting firm to obtain stolen IBM computer design materials. Hitachi Ltd. pleaded guilty on 8 February 1983 to one count of conspiracy to transport stolen property; the statutory maximum corporate fine was $10,000, which Hitachi paid (UPI Archives 1983). Two individual Hitachi employees were fined $10,000 and $4,000 respectively. IBM subsequently filed a civil suit seeking $7.5 billion (representing triple damages on its valuation of the stolen information at between $750 million and $2.5 billion); Hitachi settled the civil component separately, reportedly for approximately $300 million plus a five-year IBM inspection regime over new Hitachi products (Washington Post 1983; UPI Archives 1984). Mitsubishi Electric faced parallel charges and pleaded *nolo contendere*, also receiving a $10,000 fine.

The case was, as the Japanese press noted, legally complicated by the FBI's use of sting techniques, which are impermissible in Japan outside drug investigations. It produced guilty pleas and thus constitutes a judicially confirmed instance of industrial espionage.

### 3.4 Incumbent reaction and disputes

The US response to Japan's technology practices evolved through several phases. In 1986, the US and Japan signed the Semiconductor Trade Agreement, which set a target of 20% foreign market share in Japan; the US imposed 100% tariff sanctions on $300 million of Japanese electronics exports in April 1987 when MITI was found in non-compliance. The Structural Impediments Initiative (SII, 1989–1990) addressed systemic market access barriers. A 1994 bilateral framework agreement produced specific commitments on Japanese patent reform, including the eventual elimination of pre-grant opposition (implemented 1996) and reduction of examination pendency (US Senate Finance Committee 1989; Girouard 1995). These disputes were ultimately resolved between treaty allies with strong security interdependence; they produced no tariffs that persisted beyond the immediate semiconductor dispute.

### 3.5 Quantitative indicators

Japanese R&D expenditure rose from approximately 1.5% of GDP in the 1960s to 2.9% by 1990 (Wikipedia/R&D in Japan 2023). Cumulative royalty and licence fee payments from Japan to foreign technology suppliers are estimated at roughly $10 billion between 1950 and 1980 (Odagiri and Goto 1993). Japanese patent filings reached approximately 414,000 per year by the early 1990s (WIPO 2024). Japan transitioned from net technology importer to net exporter in its technology balance of payments around 2003.

## 4. Case Three: South Korea, approximately 1960–1997

### 4.1 Early phase (c.1960–1975): directed imitation under authoritarian developmentalism

South Korea's catch-up began under explicit state direction. Park Chung-hee's government used the National Investment Fund to channel a large share of available investment credit (reported at approximately 62%) to designated Heavy and Chemical Industries (HCI) from 1973 to 1979 (Kohli 2004). [The precise 62% figure is monograph-sourced (Kohli 2004) and not independently web-verifiable; the broad direction of NIF credit toward HCI is well documented.] The *chaebol* conglomerates—Samsung, Hyundai, LG, Daewoo—were the designated vehicles for technology absorption.

The principal acquisition mechanism in the early phase was reverse engineering of legally purchased products combined with structured licensing. Samsung's electronics division reverse-engineered Sanyo and Panasonic microwave ovens beginning in 1976, learning assembly sequences and component sourcing before approaching Sanyo for a formal technology licensing arrangement in 1978 (Kim 1997). This sequence—imitate first, license later—was a characteristic Korean pattern that economised on royalty payments while building absorptive capacity before entering formal agreements.

South Korea's Patent Law (1961) initially provided 12-year patent terms (versus 17 years in the United States), excluded pharmaceutical and chemical product patents, and permitted broad compulsory licensing. No pharmaceutical product could be patented; only the manufacturing *process* was protectable. Utility model (petty patent) applications outnumbered invention patents—reaching a patent-to-utility-model ratio of roughly 1:6 in 1984—reflecting the dominance of incremental improvement claims over pioneering inventions during the absorptive phase (WIPO Economics of IP in Korea 2010). Korea joined the Paris Convention in 1980, nearly two decades after its Patent Law was enacted.

### 4.2 Middle phase (c.1975–1990): diaspora engineering and the semiconductor trajectory

The most distinctive feature of South Korea's middle catch-up phase was systematic recruitment of Korean-American engineers from US semiconductor firms. The Korea Institute of Science and Technology (KIST, founded 1966) and the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST, founded 1971) provided institutional anchors, but the decisive talent flows came through direct recruitment from Silicon Valley (Issues in Science and Technology 2023). Samsung Electronics established a Silicon Valley design centre and actively recruited Korean-American engineers who had accumulated manufacturing knowledge at Intel, National Semiconductor, Mostek, and Fairchild. Kim Choong-Ki, trained at KaIST and later at US institutions and Fairchild Semiconductor, returned to Korea and played a central role in establishing the country's semiconductor manufacturing capabilities (IEEE Spectrum 2023). These movements were entirely legal under both US and Korean law.

Samsung's entry into DRAM manufacturing illustrates the blended nature of these mechanisms. It obtained the 64K DRAM design in 1983 chiefly through a paid technology-transfer arrangement with Micron Technology—which, needing cash and a low-cost second source, agreed to teach Samsung to produce the chip—supplemented by reverse engineering of the acquired design; Samsung developed its first 64K DRAM in November 1983 (Kim 1997). Reverse engineering became the dominant mode for the following 256K DRAM generation, which Samsung designed largely independently to reduce licensing costs. These channels were legal under Korean domestic law and, prior to TRIPS, raised no binding international obligation. Texas Instruments pursued patent-infringement actions against Asian chip makers in the mid-1980s: in January 1986 it accused nine semiconductor manufacturers, including Samsung, of infringing its DRAM patents, and the US International Trade Commission subsequently found that Samsung had sold chips in the United States without TI licences and barred the unlicensed imports. Samsung settled by taking a TI licence and, under a broad cross-licensing agreement renewed in 1996, paid TI more than US$1 billion in royalties over the following decade (Chicago Tribune 1996; WIPO 2010). These were civil commercial patent disputes, not criminal proceedings.

**Government research consortia.** The Electronics and Telecommunications Research Institute (ETRI) coordinated major technology programmes. The 4M DRAM development project (1986–1989) brought together Samsung, Hyundai, and LG with government funding, producing a commercially deployable 4M DRAM chip and demonstrating Korea's transition from pure imitation to adaptive engineering (Kim 1997).

### 4.3 US bilateral pressure and IP reform

The United States placed Korea on the Special 301 Priority Watch List in 1989 and repeatedly thereafter. The 1985–1987 bilateral IP negotiations under Section 301 of the US Trade Act of 1974 produced: extension of pharmaceutical patent protection (product patents from 1987); extension of patent terms from 12 to 15 years (subsequently 20 years to align with TRIPS); tightening of copyright enforcement; and commitments on pipeline protection for pharmaceuticals (ResearchGate/Pharmaceutical Patents 2009; Washington University Law Review 2003). These reforms were coerced by bilateral US pressure rather than arising from domestic IP constituency demand—an observation consistent with Maskus's (2000) characterisation of IP reform in developing economies as externally driven.

No judicially confirmed criminal case of industrial espionage was brought against Korean firms or individuals during the catch-up period. The Texas Instruments and subsequent patent disputes were commercial civil matters resolved through settlement and cross-licensing.

### 4.4 Quantitative indicators

South Korean R&D expenditure rose from 0.81% of GDP in 1981 to approximately 1.9% by 1990 and reached 5.2% of GDP in 2022 — the second-highest ratio in the OECD after Israel (6.0%), with the two economies approaching parity on 2024 preliminary data (Statista 2024; OECD MSTI; NSF NCSES 2024). Korean patent filings reached approximately 287,954 in 2023 (WIPO 2024). By the mid-1990s, Samsung and Hynix had achieved technology parity with US and Japanese DRAM producers; Korea became a net technology exporter in memory semiconductors by the early 2000s.

## 5. Case Four: China, approximately 1978–2024

### 5.1 Early phase (c.1978–1995): open-door policy and conditioned FDI

Deng Xiaoping's "reform and opening" generated a fundamentally different acquisition logic from its predecessors. China was a much larger economy at the outset of catch-up than Korea or Japan, and it possessed state capacity to impose systematic conditions on foreign capital inflows across all major sectors simultaneously (Naughton 2007). The Catalogue for the Guidance of Foreign Investment classified industries as "encouraged," "restricted," or "prohibited." In the restricted category, foreign equity was typically capped at 49–50%, requiring a mandatory Chinese joint-venture partner. Automotive joint ventures (Volkswagen-SAIC from 1984; General Motors-SAIC from 1997) and telecommunications equipment manufacturing were the most prominent examples (Branstetter 2018; USCC 2018).

Chinese firms used joint-venture structures as technology transfer vehicles. The technology transfer provisions typically required the foreign partner to bring specific production processes, manufacturing equipment, and training programmes. Whether this constituted "forced" transfer is contested as a matter of legal characterisation: foreign firms entered voluntarily in pursuit of market access, and no domestic law mandated the handing over of specific IP. Branstetter (2018), in testimony before the US-China Economic and Security Review Commission, argues that the combination of regulatory conditions, restricted market access, and informal administrative pressure constituted de facto coercion even where no single provision was formally illegal under WTO rules. The 2018 USTR Section 301 Report characterised these practices as violating WTO commitments on forced technology transfer; the WTO dispute settlement mechanism has not yet produced a definitive ruling on this specific question.

China's first Patent Law was enacted in 1984 and initially excluded pharmaceutical and chemical product patents. These exclusions were progressively removed in 1992 (pharmaceuticals), and subsequent revisions in 2000, 2008, and 2020 expanded protection under external pressure. China joined the Paris Convention in 1985 and the WTO in December 2001, at which point TRIPS obligations became binding. This is the critical institutional discontinuity separating China from its predecessors: the most aggressive period of Chinese technology acquisition—approximately 1995–2020—occurred under binding multilateral IP obligations that did not constrain Germany (no Paris Convention until 1903), Japan (catch-up substantially complete before TRIPS 1995), or Korea (IP reforms imposed before full TRIPS compliance).

### 5.2 Middle phase (c.1995–2010): R&D programmes and systematic talent acquisition

China launched a series of state-directed R&D programmes: the 863 High Technology Programme (1986, expanded substantially with 10 billion RMB in the 1990s), the Torch Programme (1988, commercialisation focus), the 973 Basic Research Programme (1997), and subsequently Made in China 2025 (announced 2015), backed by government guidance funds whose aggregate is commonly estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars [the frequently cited ~$347 billion figure is an analyst estimate, not an official total, and is not independently web-verifiable].

The Thousand Talents Programme, launched in 2008, recruited overseas researchers—predominantly Chinese nationals or diaspora scientists working at US and European institutions—with packages comprising salaries of $75,000–$150,000 and research funding of $500,000–$1,000,000 per recruit, plus laboratory infrastructure in China (Wikipedia/Thousand Talents Plan 2024; ERIC 2022). By 2016, approximately 60,000 individuals had been recruited across approximately 200 talent programmes at various administrative levels. The programme itself was legal under both Chinese and—for participants not bound by non-disclosure agreements—US law. The FBI opened investigations into participants who were simultaneously employed at US national laboratories or research universities without disclosure, and prosecuted cases where intellectual property was removed; the programme's legal status is thus mixed, depending on specific participant conduct.

### 5.3 Confirmed illegal activity: court-confirmed espionage cases

Several cases of Chinese state-linked technology theft have resulted in judicial convictions in US courts.

**Dongfan "Greg" Chung** (Rockwell International/Boeing): The first trial conviction under the Economic Espionage Act of 1996. Chung, a former Rockwell and Boeing stress engineer, was convicted in July 2009 of six counts of economic espionage for transmitting materials related to the Space Shuttle and Delta IV rocket to Chinese aviation officials beginning in the late 1970s. Sentenced in February 2010 to 188 months (approximately 15 years and 8 months), this represented the first judicially confirmed case of systematic Chinese state-directed economic espionage against US aerospace (FBI 2010; FindLaw 2011).

**Yanjun Xu** (GE Aviation): The first Chinese government intelligence officer extradited to the United States. Xu Yanjun, a deputy division director of the Jiangsu office of China's Ministry of State Security, was arrested in Belgium in April 2018 while attempting to receive GE Aviation composite fan-blade technology. Convicted November 2021 on all four counts (conspiracy and attempt to commit economic espionage; conspiracy and attempt to steal trade secrets); sentenced November 2022 to 20 years in federal prison. The Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the conviction in August 2024 (DOJ 2022; VOA News 2022; Yahoo News 2024).

**United Microelectronics Corporation (UMC)/Fujian Jinhua**: The case presented a nuanced outcome. UMC (a Taiwanese foundry) pleaded guilty in October 2020 to one count of receiving and possessing a stolen trade secret from Micron Technology and agreed to pay a $60 million fine—the second largest in any criminal trade secrets prosecution in the United States—and cooperate with the ongoing prosecution of Fujian Jinhua (DOJ/UMC Plea Agreement 2020; SEC Filing 2020). However, in February 2024, US District Judge Maxine Chesney found that prosecutors had failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Fujian Jinhua had misappropriated Micron's trade secrets and acquitted Fujian Jinhua of all charges (South China Morning Post 2024; UMC/Wikipedia 2025). The UMC guilty plea stands; the Fujian Jinhua acquittal does not retroactively invalidate it.

**PLA Unit 61398 indictment (2014)**: Five officers of the People's Liberation Army's Unit 61398 were indicted in Pittsburgh by a federal grand jury on charges of computer hacking and economic espionage targeting Westinghouse Electric, US Steel, and other firms. The indictment was unprecedented as the first criminal charge by any government against foreign military personnel for commercial hacking. The defendants remain in China and have not stood trial; this case therefore constitutes an indictment without conviction, not a judicial confirmation (Wikipedia/PLA Unit 61398 2024).

### 5.4 Alleged but unconfirmed practices

The IP Commission's estimate (IP Commission 2013, updated 2017) of $225 billion to $600 billion in annual US losses to Chinese IP theft has been contested by the Government Accountability Office, which stated that developing independent estimates was "not feasible," and by researchers who note the methodology's reliance on unverifiable assumptions (Niskanen Center 2018). Broad intelligence community assessments of systematic Chinese state-directed cyber-theft remain classified and thus do not constitute public judicial evidence. The US Department of Justice's China Initiative (2018–2022), which brought approximately 80 prosecutions, produced mixed results: a number of cases ended in acquittals or dropped charges, including the prosecution of Fujian Jinhua noted above and the Huawei criminal technology-theft charges related to the "Tappy" robot, which were eventually dropped (DOJ various).

### 5.5 Quantitative indicators

China's R&D expenditure rose from 0.9% of GDP in 2000 to approximately 2.7% in 2024 (2.68% on preliminary NBS data), with total spending reaching 3.61 trillion yuan ($496 billion) (NBS 2025; OECD 2025). OECD data indicate Chinese R&D spending grew by roughly 10% annually in real terms over 2010–2024, among the fastest of any major economy. Chinese patent filings by origin reached approximately 1.64 million in 2023—the largest absolute figure in the world—but 96.7% of China-origin patent families were single-office (domestic-only) filings. Measured as origin share of all patent families, Japan accounted for 18.6% of the world total, China 11.8%, the Republic of Korea 8.2% and Germany 7.3%; although China ranks first in the absolute number of patent families, it ranks only third for foreign-oriented (multi-office) families, with a share roughly half that of the United States (WIPO 2024). Chinese charges for the use of intellectual property (payments) rose from low single-digit billions of dollars around 2000 to $45.8 billion in 2024, making China one of the world's largest IP royalty payers in absolute terms—though still behind Ireland and the United States—while remaining a substantial net technology importer (World Bank/Trading Economics 2024).

## 7. Analytical Synthesis

### 7.1 Historically recurrent mechanisms

Three mechanisms appear across all four cases regardless of era: **reverse engineering of legally purchased goods**, **deliberate design of patent regimes to favour domestic absorption**, and **government coordination of technology diffusion** (whether through consortia, directed credit, or institutional channelling). These are structural features of successful catch-up industrialisation, not historically specific anomalies. Their ubiquity was emphasised by Chang (2002) in his account of how incumbent powers withdrew access to the same "ladders" they had used.

**Recruitment of foreign and diaspora engineers** appears in all four cases, with varying institutional forms: British artisans in German workshops (1820s–1840s); Japanese engineers visiting Bell Labs; Korean-Americans returning from Silicon Valley; Chinese researchers recruited through talent programmes.

### 7.2 Genuinely new mechanisms in the Chinese case

Two mechanisms have no clear historical antecedent.

**Cyber-enabled trade secret theft** employing state-linked hacking infrastructure—as exemplified by the PLA Unit 61398 indictment (2014) and subsequent APT41 charges—constitutes a qualitatively new category of acquisition. Remote network intrusion to extract proprietary manufacturing specifications and business intelligence was technologically impossible for earlier industrialisers. The 2014 indictment was the first criminal charge by any government against foreign state military personnel for commercial hacking; it has no analogue in the German, Japanese, or Korean catch-up experiences.

**Large-scale strategic acquisition of foreign technology firms** as a state-coordinated programme was unavailable to earlier industrialisers lacking China's capital account resources and the existence of global capital markets. ChemChina's $43 billion acquisition of Syngenta (announced 2016, completed 2017) and Midea Group's €4.5 billion (about $5 billion) acquisition of Kuka Robotics (2016) illustrate a mechanism that accelerates technology access by purchasing the organisational knowledge embedded in frontier firms rather than licensing or reverse engineering specific products. Germany, Japan, and Korea all engaged in outbound FDI, but not on a scale or with a degree of state direction comparable to post-2013 China.

### 7.3 Differences in scale versus differences in type

The scale discontinuity is substantial. China's $45.8 billion in annual royalty payments, 1.64 million annual patent filings, and total R&D spending of $496 billion (2024) dwarf any comparable metric in the earlier cases at equivalent stages of catch-up. China's absolute GDP at the start of its catch-up dwarfed Germany's, Japan's, or Korea's, so this scale difference is partly mechanical. More analytically significant is the simultaneity: China deployed all historically recurrent mechanisms—licensing, reverse engineering, IP design, conditional market access, talent recruitment, R&D consortia—simultaneously and at industrial scale, across virtually all advanced manufacturing sectors, within a single policy architecture.

The legal context is the most consequential difference of type. Germany's entire catch-up preceded any binding multilateral IP obligation. Japan and Korea conducted their most aggressive technology absorption phases under minimal multilateral constraint. China is the only case in which significant acquisition occurred after WTO/TRIPS accession, creating a structural tension between the methods historically used by all catch-up economies and the rules China had formally accepted. This tension underlies the scale of the US and allied response—tariffs, export controls, criminal prosecutions, FDI restrictions—which has no equivalent in the responses to German, Japanese, or Korean catch-up.

### 7.4 The incumbent response gradient

The severity of incumbent responses has escalated across cases in ways that exceed what scale differences alone explain.

Britain's response to Germany was commercial labelling requirements and popular polemic (*Made in Germany*, 1896). The US response to Japan was bilateral managed-trade agreements, a single episode of criminal prosecution (IBM-Hitachi, $10,000 fine), and patent system reform through negotiation. The US response to Korea was Section 301 bilateral pressure producing IP law reform. The US response to China has involved: $370+ billion in tariffs (2018–present); the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act (FIRRMA, 2018); the Export Control Reform Act (ECRA, 2018); entity-list restrictions on Huawei, SMIC, and several hundred other Chinese firms (over 600 China-based parties listed by 2023); criminal prosecutions producing a 20-year prison sentence; and an unprecedented allied coordination (EU FDI screening mechanisms, Japan/Netherlands semiconductor equipment restrictions). The DOJ's China Initiative (2018–2022) brought roughly 80 China-related prosecutions across a range of statutes (economic espionage, trade-secret theft, and — in many academic cases — grant fraud and false statements).

This escalation reflects not only China's greater absolute scale but also its violation of a treaty-based IP order that did not exist for earlier industrialisers, the dual-use military-civil nature of many targeted technologies, and the geopolitical context of strategic competition absent from US-Japan or US-Korea relations.

---

## 8. Conclusion

All four late industrialisers deployed structurally similar portfolios of technology acquisition mechanisms: domestically legal reverse engineering, strategically weak IP regimes, government-mediated technology consortia, and foreign talent recruitment. This historical regularity does not trivialise differences between cases. The Chinese case introduces genuinely novel cyber-enabled mechanisms, deploys all historical mechanisms simultaneously at unprecedented scale, and does so under binding multilateral IP obligations that constrained none of its predecessors during equivalent catch-up phases. The policy implication drawn by Chang (2002)—that incumbent powers "kick away the ladder" they climbed—retains partial validity: the ladder's rungs are substantially the same. But the legal altitude at which China stands when it reaches for them is higher than any previous climber faced.
