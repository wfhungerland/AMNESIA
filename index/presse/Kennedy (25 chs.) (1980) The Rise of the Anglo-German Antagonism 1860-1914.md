(1) The deepest driver of the antagonism was the structural shift in relative economic power as Germany's industry overtook Britain's.

The drift of Britain and Germany into war in 1914 followed above all from a structural change in their relative economic power. Two peoples bound by dynastic, cultural, religious and commercial ties nonetheless became antagonists within two generations. Direct trade rivalry mattered less than the deeper fact that Germany's industry was overtaking Britain's. Britain produced more than twice Germany's steel at the start of the period and had lost that lead by 1914. Shifting material power did not act through diplomacy alone but through many simultaneous channels. Trade relations, naval building, colonial friction, the press, the political parties and the courts each carried the strain. Kennedy alternates "structural" Parts on economy, parties, opinion, religion and monarchy with narrative Parts on diplomacy. The founding of the Reich in 1871 marked less of a break in the relationship than later hindsight implies. Antagonism hardened only from the 1880s, through Bismarck's colonial bid and the failure of alliance talks. Weltpolitik, the navy laws and Weltmacht ambitions after 1897 turned rivalry into fixed enmity. Domestic pressures — the "social question", armaments finance and mass patriotism — fused with foreign policy. Appeals to shared race, religion and dynasty could not offset the rising chauvinism on both sides. The Anglo-Russian entente of 1907 completed Britain's encirclement of Germany in German eyes. Kennedy joins the "primacy of foreign policy" to the "primacy of domestic politics" rather than choosing between them. The war thus emerged from a relationship transformed at every level, not from any single decision.

Introduction

Chapter in Kennedy "The Rise of the Anglo-German Antagonism, 1860-1914", pp. xi–xiii.
Modus: KAPITEL

3 Take-aways
(1) The book's guiding question is why two friendly, connected peoples became enemies by 1914.
(2) Post-1945 archival access allows a whole-relationship study that interwar historians could not attempt.
(3) A single integrated account, spanning structure and diplomacy, fills a gap left by specialised studies.

Claim Register
C01: The organising question is why Britain and Germany, long connected, drifted to war in 1914. Evidence: the opening sentence framing dynastic, cultural, religious and economic ties. Source: p. xi. Relevance: sets the book's problem.
C02: Interwar historians addressed the question mainly from published documents and memoirs. Evidence: the roll-call of Meinecke, Ritter, Rothfels, Fay, Sontag, Langer, Gooch and Taylor. Source: p. xi. Relevance: positions the work against prior scholarship.
C03: No recent full-length study treated the relationship as a whole before this book. Evidence: the noted absence of an integrated account despite many specialised works. Source: p. xi. Relevance: states the contribution.

Two peoples who had never fought each other, and whose ties were dynastic, cultural, religious and economic, drifted apart and went to war in 1914. That paradox frames the entire investigation. Nearly every major interwar historian of modern Europe felt bound to explore it. Meinecke, Ritter, Rothfels, Schmitt, Fay, Sontag, Langer, Gooch, Taylor and Woodward all took it up. They worked chiefly from published documents and diplomatic memoirs available before 1939. Only after 1945 could historians reach the full array of official and private archives. Many later studies then treated single aspects — the naval race, the cultural bonds — in isolation. No recent full-length study had examined the Anglo-German relationship as a whole. That gap is the one this book sets out to fill. The method joins structural analysis to diplomatic narrative across fifty-five years. Economy, parties, press, religion and monarchy receive treatment alongside high politics. The aim is an explanation, not a chronicle, of the antagonism's rise. The opening question returns, answered, in the Conclusion.

Ch. 1: "Anglo-German Relations in the 1860s"

Chapter in Kennedy "The Rise of the Anglo-German Antagonism, 1860-1914", pp. 3–21.
Modus: KAPITEL

3 Take-aways
(1) In the 1860s Britain regarded the German states with sympathy, not suspicion.
(2) A liberal, Protestant and commercial Germany fitted British preferences against autocratic France and Russia.
(3) The baseline of goodwill shows the antagonism was made later, not inherited.

Claim Register
C01: British opinion in the 1860s viewed German unification largely with favour. Evidence: sympathy for a liberal, Protestant Germany against French and Russian power. Source: Ch. 1, pp. 3–10. Relevance: fixes the starting point of the relationship.
C02: Commercial and dynastic ties reinforced the political affinity. Evidence: the Coburg marriage connection and dense trade links. Source: Ch. 1, pp. 10–18. Relevance: identifies the bonds later strained.
C03: The Schleswig-Holstein and unification crises did not yet produce lasting estrangement. Evidence: British non-intervention and continued goodwill. Source: Ch. 1, pp. 18–21. Relevance: dates antagonism after the 1860s.

Britain in the 1860s looked on the German states with broad sympathy rather than rivalry. A liberal, Protestant, commercially dynamic Germany suited British sentiment and interest. Against autocratic France and Russia, a strong Germany seemed a stabilising force in Europe. Dynastic ties, above all the Coburg connection, reinforced the political affinity. Dense trade and cultural exchange bound the educated classes of both nations. The Schleswig-Holstein crisis exposed British reluctance to intervene on the Continent. Palmerston's bluster gave way to non-intervention when Prussia and Austria acted. Prussia's victories were watched with interest but not yet alarm. Many Britons admired Prussian administration, universities and military reform. German unification under Prussia was largely welcomed as a liberal-national achievement. No settled hostility yet coloured the relationship at any level. The decade thus establishes a baseline of goodwill against which later change stands out. Antagonism, on this account, was constructed after the 1860s rather than inherited from them. The chapter grounds the book's claim that enmity was historically made.

(1) The Reich's founding was a European watershed but not yet a break in Anglo-German relations.

Claim Register
C01: 1870-1 marked a decisive watershed for central Europe yet not for the Anglo-German relationship. Evidence: continuity of diplomatic practice and mutual assessment across the war. Source: Ch. 2, pp. 22–24. Relevance: dates the antagonism after unification.

The founding of the Second Reich was the most decisive central-European event for centuries. From the standpoint of Anglo-German relations, however, it marked no comparable break. Diplomatic practices and assumptions on both sides continued along their earlier lines. Each government's assessment of the other changed little across the Franco-Prussian War. Bismarck used the isolation and defeat of France to weld a German nation under Prussia. Gladstone's government deplored the bloodshed but refused intervention beyond protest. British caution over Continental commitments persisted as before. Admiration for German success coexisted with unease at France's humiliation. The annexation of Alsace-Lorraine troubled some British observers without producing estrangement. Later generations, conscious of 1914 and 1939, have exaggerated 1870-1 as a turning point. Kennedy warns against this retrospective teleology. The decade to Disraeli's fall in 1880 shows rivalry still latent, not active. Continuity, not caesura, best describes the relationship in these years. The chapter reinforces the argument that antagonism hardened only afterwards.

Ch. 3: "Anglo-German Economic Ties"

Chapter in Kennedy "The Rise of the Anglo-German Antagonism, 1860-1914", pp. 41–58.
Modus: KAPITEL

3 Take-aways
(1) Deep, old and mutually vital commercial links bound Britain and Germany before rivalry set in.
(2) The early trade relationship was highly unbalanced in Britain's favour.
(3) These bonds complicate any account that reduces the antagonism to commercial competition.

Claim Register
C01: Anglo-German commerce was ancient, dense and important to producers and consumers on both sides. Evidence: continuity from the Hanse through the Zollverein era. Source: Ch. 3, pp. 41–44. Relevance: establishes the depth of economic interdependence.
C02: Early trade was markedly unbalanced toward Britain. Evidence: in the early Zollverein years Germany took about one-third of all British exports to Europe. Source: Ch. 3, pp. 44–46. Quantitative support: roughly one-third of European exports. Relevance: shows Britain's initial dominance.
C03: Strong economic bonds persisted alongside later political estrangement. Evidence: continued mutual dependence into the pre-war years. Source: Ch. 3, pp. 46–58. Relevance: qualifies the "trade rivalry" thesis.

Commercial links between Britain and Germany reached back to the Hanse and beyond. They survived Dutch trading supremacy, mercantilism and the wars of 1793–1815. After Waterloo the trade resumed at an even more hectic pace than before. To producers, merchants and customers on both sides the commerce was highly significant. It was also markedly unbalanced in Britain's favour in the early decades. In the early Zollverein years Germany took about one-third of all British exports to Europe. That share exceeded the combined exports to France, Russia, Scandinavia, Spain and several others. German merchants, confined largely to a European field, depended on British imports. Britain supplied manufactures, colonial goods and capital that Germany then lacked. These bonds tied the two economies tightly together well before political rivalry emerged. Even as Germany industrialised, mutual dependence remained substantial into 1914. The depth of these ties complicates any purely competitive reading of the antagonism. Economic interdependence and economic rivalry coexisted throughout the period. The chapter supplies the material baseline for the later chapters on trade rivalry.

Party alignments in Britain and Germany shaped each nation's view of the other. British Liberals long admired Prussian reform, education and Protestant liberalism. Conservatives were more ambivalent, weighing German power against imperial interest. As Bismarckian Germany turned protectionist and authoritarian, Liberal sympathy cooled. In Germany, National Liberals had once looked to free-trading Britain as a model. Conservatives and agrarians grew hostile as tariff and naval questions sharpened. The rising Social Democrats viewed Britain through the lens of class and free trade. Ideological affinity, more than raw national interest, often drove these attitudes. Domestic party competition thus entered foreign perception from the start. Each side read the other partly through its own internal quarrels. Attitudes to Germany became entangled with British debates over free trade and reform. Attitudes to Britain became entangled with German debates over constitutionalism and empire. The chapter shows the relationship refracted through party politics on both sides. It advances the book's integration of domestic and external causes.

A cheap mass press transformed how each nation imagined the other. Sensational coverage of naval, colonial and commercial disputes hardened popular images. Newspapers on both sides traded in suspicion, alarm and national self-assertion. Organised pressure groups gave that sentiment shape and direction. Navy leagues, colonial societies and patriotic associations mobilised opinion. In Germany such bodies pressed for fleet and empire against Britain. In Britain, press scares dramatised the German naval and commercial threat. Public opinion thus became an active force rather than a passive backdrop. Governments increasingly acted within, and were provoked by, this charged climate. Editors and lobbyists could inflame crises that diplomats sought to contain. The machinery of opinion helped convert elite rivalry into mass hostility. Perceptions, once fixed in print, proved hard to reverse. The chapter treats opinion as part of the antagonism's causal structure. It prepares the later analysis of orchestrated patriotism.

Both Britain and Germany after 1871 were constitutional monarchies of contrasting character. Historians treat one as moving toward democracy and the other toward "Caesarism". Yet in both, monarchs and their court circles helped formulate policy toward the other. Victoria, though lacking her German cousin's prerogatives, wielded great actual influence. Her deep knowledge of German affairs informed successive British governments. Her permanence, as ministries came and went, enhanced her real weight. Wilhelm II held, in theory, supreme control over external policy. His passivity and volatility, however, repeatedly undercut that authority. The German court thus generated unpredictability rather than steady direction. Dynastic visits and family ties could soothe or inflame relations by turns. Personal rulers injected temperament into what diplomats hoped to keep calm. Kennedy corrects the assumption that the more autocratic monarch had the greater sway. The interplay of courts and cabinets forms another layer of the antagonism. The chapter shows personality as a genuine, if secondary, causal force.
