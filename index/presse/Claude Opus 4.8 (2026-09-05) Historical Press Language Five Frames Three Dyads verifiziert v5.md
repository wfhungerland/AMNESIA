# Historical press language across three hegemonic rivalry dyads

This report catalogs attested press vocabulary across three challenger-incumbent dyads and five analytical frames, drawing on digitized newspaper archives, secondary historiography, and Google Books Ngram frequency proxies. Terms are organized by dyad/period, then by frame. For each term: attested example with year and publication where available, frequency assessment (dominant / common / marginal), and semantic peculiarities.

## DYAD 1: UK → GERMANY (1870–1913)

### Frame 1 — Unfair competition

**"Made in Germany"** — The single most recognizable press phrase of the entire Anglo-German commercial rivalry. Originated as a compulsory labeling requirement under the Merchandise Marks Act of 1887 (50 & 51 Vict. c. 28). Became a press sensation after E.E. Williams's serialization in the *New Review* (early 1896, five installments) and book publication (1896; six editions in one year). Reviewed in *The Spectator* (21 November 1896), *Reynolds's Weekly Newspaper* (12 January 1896), *Pall Mall Gazette* (16 January 1896), *Lloyd's Weekly Newspaper* (22 November 1896). Williams wrote that the phrase became "raw material for a jape at the pantomime," indicating mass cultural saturation.
Frequency: DOMINANT. The defining phrase of the entire discourse.
Semantic peculiarities: Originally intended as a stigma to deter consumers from buying German goods, the phrase underwent a remarkable semantic inversion — by c. 1907, German manufacturers were citing it as a badge of quality. In 1890s British press context, it simultaneously connoted fraud, cheapness, imitation, and existential economic threat.

**"Dumping"** — Entered press vocabulary primarily through the Tariff Reform debates from 1903 onward. The Treasury produced a formal report, "The Conditions and Effects of 'Dumping'" (7 July 1903, Cab. 37/65/42). Lansdowne used precursor language in the House of Lords (15 June 1903). Used extensively in Tariff Commission hearings (Report of the Tariff Commission, vol. 4, *The Engineering Industries*, 1909). German steel was the primary referent.
Frequency: COMMON from 1903 onward. Before 1903 the concept existed but the term was not standard.
Semantic peculiarities: In Edwardian usage, "dumping" referred specifically to state-sponsored cartels (Kartelle) selling stockpiles abroad below domestic prices, narrower than the modern WTO definition. A noted irony: the 1903 Treasury report observed that cheap "dumped" German steel benefited British shipbuilders, who then sold ships back to Germany.

**"Commercial invasion" / "trade invasion"** — Widespread metaphor across late Victorian periodicals framing German exports as military-style aggression. Williams (1896) and surrounding press discourse used this language extensively.
Frequency: COMMON. Identified as one of "two strands of metaphor" most prevalent in discussions of British economic decline (alongside life-cycle metaphors).
Semantic peculiarities: Part of a pervasive system of military metaphor applied to trade, paired with "commercial defeat." The militarization of trade language both reflected and reinforced the conflation of economic and strategic competition with Germany.

**"Unfair competition" / "unfair trade"** — Standard vocabulary in parliamentary and quality press coverage throughout the period. Used in the 1887 Select Committee reports, the 1890 Select Committee on the Merchandise Marks Act, and the 1886 Royal Commission on Trade and Industry. The *Economist* (November 1887) described the Merchandise Marks Act as "directed against fraud."
Frequency: COMMON. Standard parliamentary and quality press term.
Semantic peculiarities: Encompassed both outright trademark fraud (German goods stamped "Sheffield") and the broader practice of selling goods at lower prices through state-supported cartels. The boundary between legitimate competition and "unfair" practices was fiercely contested between free traders and protectionists.

**"Chief commercial rival"** — *Saturday Review* (24 August 1895, "Our True Foreign Policy"). `[v1: verbatim attested wording is "Our chief rival in trade and commerce today is not France but Germany" (Saturday Review, 24 August 1895, p. 17). The earlier draft rendering "chief competitor in trade and transport" conflated this sentence with the article's separate line "We English have always waged war against our competitors in trade and transport." Both phrases appear in the 1895 Saturday Review discourse, but the "trade and commerce" version is the one attached to the "chief rival" claim.]*
Frequency: COMMON to DOMINANT. The framing of Germany as Britain's primary trade rival was widespread across the political spectrum by the mid-1890s.

### Frame 2 — National security threat

**"The German Menace"** — Per Patrick Longson's PhD thesis (*The Rise of the German Menace*, University of Birmingham, 2014), this phrase crystallized after the Kruger Telegram Crisis (January 1896). Attested across weekly and monthly reviews, broadsheet newspapers, "yellow dailies," magazines, and popular literature. Kipling invoked the "language and ideology of the 'German Menace'" in his poem "The Rowers" published in *The Times* (22 December 1902). The *Quarterly Review* published "The German Peril" (1908, pp. 264–98).
Frequency: DOMINANT from c. 1896, peaking 1906–1914. Became "an increasingly powerful force in British popular and political culture."
Semantic peculiarities: The term encompassed stereotypes of German "covetousness, autocracy and efficiency." Originally conceived as an imperial/colonial threat (German encroachment on British spheres — Kiao Chou 1897, Baghdad Railway, Pacific expansion); only after c. 1904–1908 did the domestic invasion threat become primary.

**"The German Peril"** — *Quarterly Review* (1908, pp. 264–98), article title.
Frequency: COMMON. Close synonym of "German Menace," used more in quality periodicals.

**"We want eight and we won't wait!"** — Coined by the Unionist/Conservative opposition in 1909 during the "dreadnought panic" / "acceleration crisis." Popularized by the Conservative press, especially Leo Maxse's *National Review*. The demand was for eight new dreadnoughts against Germany's building programme.
Frequency: DOMINANT (1909). One of the most famous political slogans of the Edwardian era.
Semantic peculiarities: A rare example of a press/political slogan that directly shaped policy. The Liberal government eventually ordered eight dreadnoughts via a "face-saving formula" (four immediate, four contingent). Churchill later quipped: "The Admiralty had demanded six ships; the economists offered four; and we finally compromised on eight." *(Beide Elemente — Slogan und Churchill-Zitat — durch International Churchill Society und Cambridge/Seligmann bestätigt.)*

**"Invasion scare" / invasion literature** — A recognized sub-genre. Key works: Erskine Childers, *The Riddle of the Sands* (1903); William Le Queux, *The Invasion of 1910* (serialized in the *Daily Mail* from 19 March 1906, commissioned by Alfred Harmsworth/Lord Northcliffe). The *Daily Mail* marketed the serial by dressing its London newspaper vendors as Prussian soldiers in pickelhaube helmets (per historian Sir Richard Evans). Le Queux also wrote *Spies of the Kaiser*.
Frequency: DOMINANT (1903–1914). *The Invasion of 1910* sold over 1 million copies, was translated into 27 languages. Generated spy hysteria: by 1909, "German tourists consulting railway timetables or buying phrase books had been reported to the police as spies."
Semantic peculiarities: Northcliffe insisted Le Queux rewrite invasion routes to pass through towns with high *Daily Mail* readership rather than the strategically plausible Essex route recommended by Field Marshal Lord Roberts. This reveals the commercial motivation behind the scaremongering. *(Serialisierungsdatum, 27 Sprachen, 1 Mio. Exemplare und Pickelhaube-Verkäufer bestätigt.)*

**"Naval race" / "naval scare" / "dreadnought panic"** — Intensified after the German Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900. The "acceleration crisis" of 1908–1909 was the peak of press alarm. *The Times* was a key venue.
Frequency: DOMINANT (1908–1912).

**"Huns"** — The *Daily Mail* is credited as an early English-language adopter, drawing on Kaiser Wilhelm II's own 1900 "Hunnenrede" during the Boxer Rebellion.
Frequency: MARGINAL before 1914 (became DOMINANT during WWI). In 1900–1913, used but not yet the standard dehumanizing epithet it later became.

### Frame 3 — Economic decline anxiety

**"National efficiency"** — Per G.R. Searle, *The Quest for National Efficiency* (1971), a major cross-party political movement from 1899–1914, especially intense 1899–1905. Led by Lord Rosebery, supported by Fabians (Sidney and Beatrice Webb), Liberal Imperialists. Searle sums up the ideology as an attempt to "discredit the habits, beliefs and institutions that put the British at a handicap in their competition with foreigners and to commend instead a social organization that more closely followed the German model" (Searle 1971, p. 54). The Duke of York used it in December 1901 to exhort England to "Wake Up!" (Searle attests the headline "Wake Up, England!", p. 34). `[v4: Gegen Searle (1971) im Volltext geprüft. Die frühere Fassung „discredit the old-fashioned habits, customs and institutions that put the British at a handicap in competition with the world, especially with Germany" war ein nicht wörtliches Mischzitat und wurde durch Searles tatsächlichen Wortlaut (S. 54, Abschnitt „The German and Japanese Models") ersetzt; Searle schreibt allgemein „foreigners" und nennt das „German model" gesondert. Zusammensetzung der efficiency group (Rosebery, Sidney und Beatrice Webb, Haldane, Hewins, H. G. Wells, Fabianer, Liberal Imperialists) sowie die Doppelrolle Deutschlands (Modell und Bedrohung) und Japan als sekundäres Vorbild sind gegen Searle bestätigt.]`
Frequency: DOMINANT (1899–1905), then COMMON (1905–1914). Central to Edwardian political discourse.
Semantic peculiarities: Germany served a dual role — both as the terrifying competitor AND as the model to emulate. German technical education, state organization, and industrial planning were held up as exemplary. The term encompassed military reform, educational reform, social welfare, public health, and eugenics.

**"Industrial supremacy" (loss thereof) / "commercial supremacy"** — Williams (1896): "The Industrial Supremacy of Great Britain has been long an axiomatic commonplace; and it is fast turning into a myth." Also: "The industrial glory of England is departing, and England does not know it." Press discussions of declining trade figures, especially in chemicals and steel. The Royal Commission on Trade and Industry (1886) was an early institutional expression.
Frequency: COMMON. The anxiety was pervasive but contested — free traders argued the statistics were being misread.
Semantic peculiarities: Two dominant metaphor systems: (a) MILITARY — "commercial invasion," "commercial defeat"; (b) LIFE-CYCLE — "eclipse of a senescent Britain by 'youthful' industrial powers." The life-cycle metaphor implied inevitability and biological determinism.

**"Wake Up!" / "Wake Up, England!"** — The Duke of York's December 1901 speech. Kipling's *The Five Nations* (1903). Both followed the humiliations of the Boer War (1899–1902).
Frequency: COMMON (1901–1905). Linked to Boer War failures exposing broader systemic weaknesses.

**"Race deterioration" / "physical deterioration" / "degeneration"** — Prompted by discovery that many Boer War recruits were physically unfit. Led to the 1904 Inter-Departmental Committee on Physical Deterioration. Connected to eugenics movement (Eugenics Education Society founded 1907).
Frequency: COMMON (1899–1914). A subset of the national efficiency discourse.
Semantic peculiarities: Combined social Darwinist, neo-Malthusian, and eugenic concerns. Physical unfitness of recruits was taken as evidence of broader national degeneration, with Germany as the implicit standard.

**Note on "British Disease":** This term did NOT exist in the 1870–1913 period. It is a 1970s coinage referring to stagflation and industrial relations problems. Anachronistic if applied to the Victorian/Edwardian era.

### Frame 4 — Civilizational/system rivalry

**"Prussian militarism" / "Prussianism"** — Widespread throughout the period. The concept of Prussia as "an army with a state rather than a state with an army" was commonplace. Carried strong connotations of autocracy, discipline, subordination of individual liberty to the state, and aggressive expansionism.
Frequency: COMMON (1870–1913), becoming DOMINANT during and after the Boer War.
Semantic peculiarities: Characterized as "Anglocentric and Prussophobic terms." Functioned as antonyms to British "liberalism" and "free institutions." Framed the rivalry as a contest between liberty and autocracy.

**Free Trade vs. Protectionism as civilizational framing** — Central to the Tariff Reform debate launched by Joseph Chamberlain's Birmingham Town Hall speech (15 May 1903). Covered extensively in *Manchester Courier* (28 September 1903), *Gloucester Chronicle* (23 October 1903), *Edinburgh Evening News* (12 December 1903), *Western Times* (28 January 1910, 27 August 1913).
Frequency: DOMINANT (1903–1914). The defining political controversy of Edwardian Britain.
Semantic peculiarities: Free trade was presented not merely as economic policy but as a moral and quasi-religious commitment — Williams called critics "fanatical Free-Traders." For Liberals, free trade was inseparable from British identity, liberty, and civilization. Protectionism was associated with continental autocracy. The tariff debate thus became a proxy for civilizational self-definition.

**"Teutonic" characterizations** — Widely used as a descriptor for German character, institutions, and culture in both positive and negative senses (positive: thoroughness, efficiency, scholarship; negative: heaviness, pedantry, militarism). *Fun* magazine (4 February 1871) used "Vaterlanders" as a colloquial term.
Frequency: COMMON. Standard press adjective throughout the period.
Semantic peculiarities: Underwent semantic darkening. In the 1870s–1880s, "Teutonic" could invoke shared Anglo-Saxon/Teutonic racial kinship. By the 1900s, it increasingly connoted otherness and threat.

**"Germaniam esse delendam" / "Germania est delenda" ("Germany must be destroyed")** — *Saturday Review*, later 1890s. `[v4: Gegen die jetzt vorliegende Standardquelle Kennedy (1980) geprüft. Frequency: MARGINAL but highly significant. The *Saturday Review* was a quality weekly, not mass-circulation. These editorials were extreme but widely quoted, including in German diplomatic circles.
Semantic peculiarities: The allusion to Cato the Elder's "Carthago delenda est" explicitly framed the rivalry as an existential civilizational struggle, an eliminationist framework.

### Frame 5 — Imitation/technology theft

**"Imitation" / "imitations"** — Central to Williams's *Made in Germany* (1896): "One special cause of complaint against him is that his imitations are inferior to the models, though... his copy is often sufficiently like the British original to deceive the purchaser." Williams added: "the German imitation is not as a rule inferior in all respects. In the matter of artistic finish it is often — one may say, as a rule — decidedly better."
Frequency: DOMINANT within the "Made in Germany" discourse of the 1890s.
Semantic peculiarities: Carried a double register — both "inferior copy" and "rival that may actually be superior." Williams's honest acknowledgment that German imitations were often artistically superior was a notable departure from simple deprecation.

**"Fraud" / "fraudulent marking" / "false description"** — Central to the Merchandise Marks Act debates. Sheffield cutlers were particularly vocal — German goods from Solingen were regularly stamped "Sheffield" for export to the American market. *British Trade Journal* (March 1886): "Sheffield Merchants and German Goods." The *Economist* (November 1887) described the Act as "directed against fraud." The 1887 Select Committee, 1890 Select Committee, and 1897 Hansard debates all used this language.
Frequency: COMMON (1886–1900). The specific charge of trademark fraud was the legal foundation of the Merchandise Marks Act.
Semantic peculiarities: Tension between "fraud" (outright deception) and "competition" (legitimate if unwelcome). One MP noted in 1897: "They never proceeded straight in this legislation of theirs against fraud. They always wanted to insinuate that the rogue was abroad, whereas he was generally found at home."

**"Copying" / "piracy" (of designs and methods)** — German firms were accused of copying Sheffield steel marks, Birmingham hardware designs, and various British manufacturing methods. Williams (1896) discussed German copying practices extensively.
Frequency: COMMON in trade press and sectoral publications; less prominent in the general press.
Semantic peculiarities: "Piracy" referred primarily to trademark and design piracy rather than patent theft. The copying accusation became less tenable as German quality improved — by the 1900s, Germany was increasingly the innovator, especially in chemicals, electrical engineering, and optics.

**"Cheap and nasty" / "cheap imitations"** — Common pejorative for German goods in the 1880s–1890s. German goods from Solingen sold as "Sheffield" products.
Frequency: COMMON (1880s–1890s), declining as German quality improved.
Semantic peculiarities: Increasingly contradicted by reality. The persistence of the "cheap" frame despite evidence of improving quality reveals its ideological rather than empirical function.

**Cross-cutting note for Dyad 1:** The dominant frame shifted chronologically from UNFAIR COMPETITION (1887–1900) → NATIONAL SECURITY THREAT (1900–1914), with ECONOMIC DECLINE ANXIETY as a constant undertone. The Boer War (1899–1902) was the pivotal turning point. Key publications by role: *Daily Mail* (mass-market scaremongering), *The Times* (authoritative naval race coverage), *Saturday Review* (extreme anti-German editorials), *National Review* under Leo Maxse (naval alarmism). Key secondary sources quoting original press language: Paul Kennedy, *The Rise of the Anglo-German Antagonism, 1860–1914* (1980); G.R. Searle, *The Quest for National Efficiency* (1971); Longson, *The Rise of the German Menace* (PhD thesis, Birmingham, 2014).

## DYAD 2: USA → JAPAN (1980–1995)

### Frame 1 — Unfair competition

**"Japan Inc."** — Used throughout the 1980s in all major American publications. Encapsulated the idea that Japan's government and private sector operated as a single coordinated entity. Derived from Chalmers Johnson's analysis of MITI. Appeared in WSJ, NYT, newsweeklies, and editorial pages.
Frequency: DOMINANT. Appeared in virtually all publications discussing Japan's economy.
Semantic peculiarities: Carried connotations of conspiratorial coordination between government (especially MITI) and corporations. Sometimes used neutrally but predominantly pejorative in press context. Collapsed the distinction between private enterprise and state direction — itself a substantive analytical claim embedded in a two-word label.

**"Unfair trade" / "unfair trade practices"** — Standard phrase throughout the 1980s. PBS/Econofact: "Japan, which seemed to be steadily displacing American firms in industry after industry through 'unfair trade.'" EBSCO Research Starters: "American politicians and media often accused it of unfair trade practices."
Frequency: DOMINANT. Standard vocabulary of trade coverage.

**"Dumping"** — Used heavily in semiconductor dispute coverage. The 1986 U.S.–Japan Semiconductor Trade Agreement was explicitly an anti-dumping pact. Reagan "negotiated a semiconductor pricing pact with Japan that prevented the Japanese from dumping product into the U.S. market" (IndustryWeek, citing Clyde Prestowitz). DRAM chip pricing was central.
Frequency: COMMON, especially intense 1985–1987 during semiconductor disputes.
Semantic peculiarities: Moved from a technical trade-law term to general press vocabulary during this period.

**"Voluntary export restraints" (VERs)** — Ubiquitous in trade coverage from 1981 onward. Auto VERs began in 1981, limiting Japanese car exports to 1.68 million units per year. PBS: "Voluntary Export Restraints on Japanese autos were equivalent to a tariff rate exceeding 60 percent." Cato Institute described them as "hardly voluntary, backed as they are by the threat of legislation."
Frequency: DOMINANT in trade/business press; COMMON in general press.
Semantic peculiarities: Scare-quote usage of "voluntary" was itself a press convention, highlighting the euphemistic nature of the term.

**"Trade deficit"** — The most frequently cited statistic in Japan coverage. `[v2 — KORREKTUR: Die frühere Fassung nannte ein "bilaterales Handelsdefizit von $171,2 Mrd. für 1987". Bestätigt via US-MoFA/Commerce-Bluebook und Chicago Fed.]* The bilateral U.S.–Japan deficit (on the order of ~$56–60 billion in the late 1980s) was the single most-cited economic framing statistic. Constant citation in NYT, WSJ, Washington Post.
Frequency: DOMINANT. The single most-used economic framing term.

**"Closed markets" / "protectionist"** — EBSCO: "many US politicians and journalists accused the Japanese government of being protectionist and unfairly excluding American-made goods."
Frequency: COMMON to DOMINANT.

**"Level playing field"** — Common metaphor in trade rhetoric throughout the period.
Frequency: COMMON.

**"Agents of influence"** — Title of Pat Choate's 1990 book. *Sun-Sentinel* (27 January 1991) reviewed it under the headline "THE 'JAPAN LOBBY' SCORES AN ECONOMIC PEARL HARBOR." Described 200+ former U.S. government officials lobbied by Japanese corporations.
Frequency: MARGINAL to COMMON. Primarily associated with Choate's book but entered general discourse.

**"Adversarial trade"** — Term used in Michael Crichton's *Rising Sun* (1992): "the Japanese have invented a new kind of trade — adversarial trade, trade like war, trade intended to wipe out the competition."
Frequency: MARGINAL. Primarily literary/opinion.

**"Predatory pricing"** — Used in semiconductor and electronics coverage, especially regarding DRAMs and television sets.
Frequency: COMMON, particularly in business press.

**"Structural Impediments Initiative" (SII)** — Formal trade negotiation term (begun under Bush Sr., 1989–1990) that appeared frequently in news coverage.
Frequency: COMMON in business/policy press.

### Frame 2 — National security threat

**"Economic Pearl Harbor"** — Attested in *Sun-Sentinel* headline (27 January 1991): "THE 'JAPAN LOBBY' SCORES AN ECONOMIC PEARL HARBOR." The National WWII Museum confirms the phrase was used in this period.
Frequency: COMMON. Appeared in opinion pieces, editorials, and political rhetoric rather than hard news leads.
Semantic peculiarities: Deliberately collapsed the distinction between military and economic competition. Later revived by Warren Buffett (2008) in financial crisis context.

**"Buying up America" / "buying America"** — Used to describe Japanese acquisitions of U.S. real estate and companies. Key events: Mitsubishi Estate's purchase of Rockefeller Center (1989), Sony's purchase of Columbia Pictures (1989), Bridgestone's acquisition of Firestone.
Frequency: DOMINANT. One of the most emotionally charged phrases of the era.

**"Japan Invades Hollywood"** — *Newsweek* cover, 9 October 1989, describing Sony's purchase of Columbia Pictures.
Frequency: COMMON. The "invasion" metaphor was widespread.
Semantic peculiarities: Military metaphor applied to a business acquisition — typical of the period's conflation of economic and military language.

**"Containing Japan"** — James Fallows, *The Atlantic Monthly*, May 1989. FAIR noted the cover featured "a hungry-looking sumo wrestler eyeing the globe." The Cold War "containment" allusion was widely noted. *(Titel, Datum und Sumo-Cover bestätigt via FAIR.)*
Frequency: COMMON. Deliberately imported from George Kennan's containment doctrine against the Soviet Union.
Semantic peculiarities: Implied Japan was an analogous strategic threat to the USSR — a remarkable framing for a treaty ally.

**"The Danger from Japan"** — Theodore H. White, *New York Times Magazine*, 28 July 1985. An 8,000-word essay: "Today, 40 years after the end of World War II, the Japanese are on the move again in one of history's most brilliant commercial offensives, as they go about dismantling American industry." *(Lead-Zitat und Datum bestätigt via FAIR und Discourse Magazine.)* *Washington Post* (1 August 1985) published a rejoinder titled "Low Point in Japan-Bashing" ("Japan-bashing has just about hit its low point with an unfortunate New York Times Magazine cover story by Theodore H. White called 'The Danger from Japan'"). *[v2: Titel und Datum 1.8.1985 gegen das WaPo-Archiv bestätigt.]*
Frequency: COMMON. Hugely influential, widely discussed.
Semantic peculiarities: White explicitly recast economic competition as a continuation of WWII. "Commercial offensive" language was military-derived.

**"The Coming War with Japan"** — George Friedman and Meredith Lebard's 1991 book. *Newsweek* (1998 retrospective): "the theme — that the United States and Japan would ultimately start shooting at each other — seemed farfetched. But the fact that such a book could be respectably published reflected the widespread belief."
Frequency: MARGINAL. Extreme position but "respectably published" and reviewed in major outlets.

**Toshiba-Kongsberg scandal language (1987)** — On 1–2 July 1987, members of Congress smashed a Toshiba radio/boombox with sledgehammers on the **U.S. Capitol lawn** (not, as an earlier draft had it, "in front of the White House"). *[v2: Ort — Capitol lawn/grounds — durch UPI (1.7.1987), Washington Post, Foreign Affairs (Packard 1987) und Wilson Center bestätigt.]* Terms used in coverage: "national security breach," "endangering the security of the free world," "dual-use technology." *Christian Science Monitor* (26 October 1987) noted the "unsettling double standard" — intense anger at Japan vs. minimal response to Norway's equal role (Kongsberg).
Frequency: COMMON. Major news event covered extensively 1987–1988.
Semantic peculiarities: The Toshiba scandal was unique in directly linking Japanese corporate behavior to military security, making it a crossover between economic and military threat frames.

### Frame 3 — Economic decline anxiety

**"Japan as Number One"** — Title of Ezra Vogel's 1979 book (*Japan as Number One: Lessons for America*), which set the frame for the entire decade. Widely referenced in press throughout the 1980s.
Frequency: DOMINANT. Became a catchphrase cited constantly.
Semantic peculiarities: Originally intended as a challenge to America to reform, it was popularly received as a declaration of Japanese supremacy.

**"Trading Places"** — Title of Clyde Prestowitz's 1988 book: *Trading Places: How We Are Giving Our Future to Japan and How to Reclaim It*. Used as both book-title reference and general metaphor.
Frequency: COMMON.

**"Competitiveness" / "competitiveness crisis"** — Central framing term. The 1980s preoccupation with whether America could "compete" was institutionalized in bodies like Prestowitz's Economic Strategy Institute and the President's Commission on Industrial Competitiveness (1985).
Frequency: DOMINANT. Pervaded business and political press.

**"Rust Belt"** — Standard term for the deindustrializing Midwest. Directly connected to the Japan competition narrative through auto industry decline. Detroit was ground zero for anti-Japanese sentiment (the Vincent Chin murder, 1982).
Frequency: DOMINANT.
Semantic peculiarities: While not exclusively Japan-related, "Rust Belt" was frequently invoked in Japan trade coverage.

**"Imperial overstretch"** — Paul Kennedy's concept from *The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers* (1987). Kennedy argued that America, like previous great powers, was declining through military overextension relative to economic capacity. A bestseller; Kennedy became a frequent media commentator.
Frequency: COMMON. Widely referenced 1987–1990, often alongside Japan's rise.
Semantic peculiarities: Provided a historical/theoretical framework for decline anxiety. Press used it as shorthand for American decline.

**"Deindustrialization"** — Used in academic and press contexts. Connected to works like Magaziner and Reich's *Minding America's Business* (1982).
Frequency: COMMON. More common in features/opinion than daily news.

**"Declining America" / "American decline"** — General framing in late 1980s press.
Frequency: DOMINANT. Pervasive theme in newsweeklies and editorial pages.

### Frame 4 — Civilizational/system rivalry

**"The Revisionists" / "revisionist school"** — Label applied to Chalmers Johnson, Clyde Prestowitz, James Fallows, and Karel van Wolferen. They challenged the mainstream view that Japan was converging toward Western-style capitalism and argued Japan practiced a fundamentally different form of economic organization.
Frequency: COMMON. Standard label in press discussions of Japan policy, 1988–1993.
Semantic peculiarities: Contested label — the revisionists "reject the claim that they are 'Japan bashers.'" McCargo used "revisionists" as "less polarizing."

**"Japan bashing"** — The meta-term for the entire discourse. Both "Japan bashing" and "Japan-bashing" (hyphenated) were used. EBSCO confirms it "became popular" in the 1980s.
Frequency: DOMINANT. Ubiquitous as both description and accusation.
Semantic peculiarities: Initially used by defenders of Japan to delegitimize criticism; later adopted more broadly. Its existence as a meta-term is itself a distinctive feature of this discourse.

**"Developmental state"** — Chalmers Johnson's key concept from *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* (1982). Adopted by press.
Frequency: COMMON. Used in business press and opinion pages.

**"Keiretsu"** — Japanese term for interlocking corporate groups, adopted directly into English-language press. Used to describe "insider capitalism."
Frequency: COMMON. Standard business press vocabulary by late 1980s.

**"Anglo-American model" vs. "Japanese model"** — The revisionists "asserted that, in contrast to the open-market capitalism of the 'Anglo-American' model, Japan practiced a unique form of state-directed insider capitalism."
Frequency: COMMON. Standard framing in analytical/opinion pieces.

**"MITI"** — The Ministry of International Trade and Industry became press shorthand for state-directed capitalism. Theodore White (1985): "MITI defines strategies; Japanese private enterprise follows through with zest." *(White-Zitat bestätigt via Discourse Magazine.)*
Frequency: DOMINANT in Japan economic coverage.

**"The Japan That Can Say No"** — 1989 book by Shintaro Ishihara and Akio Morita. Referenced widely in American press as evidence of Japanese assertiveness.
Frequency: COMMON (1989–1991).

**"Soft authoritarianism"** — Chalmers Johnson's characterization of Japanese governance.
Frequency: MARGINAL. Primarily in policy/opinion pieces.

**"The Enigma of Japanese Power"** — Karel van Wolferen's 1989 book title, widely reviewed.
Frequency: MARGINAL as a specific phrase, but contributed to the "inscrutable Japan" framing.

### Frame 5 — Imitation/technology theft

**"Cheap copies" / "cheap imitations"** — EBSCO: journalists "suggested that the Japanese lacked creativity and had gained economic success by producing cheap copies of American products or slight improvements on American ideas."
Frequency: COMMON. Especially dominant in the 1950s–1970s; by the 1980s this was transitioning as Japan's quality became undeniable, but persisted in political rhetoric.
Semantic peculiarities: Major semantic shift — "Made in Japan" went from denoting cheap imitation (1950s–1960s) to denoting superior quality (1980s). The old frame persisted in rhetoric even as consumer behavior contradicted it. Akio Morita titled his 1986 autobiography *Made in Japan* as a deliberate reclamation.

**"Reverse engineering"** — Used to describe Japan's method of acquiring technology. Standard business press vocabulary.
Frequency: COMMON.

**"Lacking creativity" / "lacked originality"** — EBSCO: journalists accused Japan of "lack of originality." Widespread stereotype.
Frequency: COMMON. Recurrent characterization in editorial/opinion.
Semantic peculiarities: Coexisted uneasily with simultaneous admiration for Japanese innovation in manufacturing processes (kaizen, just-in-time, total quality management).

**"Industrial espionage"** — Mentioned in press as one of the fears raised about Japan in the 1980s.
Frequency: MARGINAL to COMMON. More common in opinion/commentary than hard news.

**"Dual-use technology"** — Prominent in Toshiba-Kongsberg coverage (1987). The scandal involved "militarily critical dual-use technologies."
Frequency: COMMON (1987–1988).

**"Copycat"** — Used informally, particularly in editorials and letters to the editor.
Frequency: MARGINAL. Less formal than "reverse engineering."

**"Slight improvements on American ideas"** — Specific characterization used by journalists and politicians. A nuanced version of the "copycat" charge.
Frequency: COMMON.

**Cross-cutting note for Dyad 2:** The most distinctive feature of the period's press language was the systematic use of WWII military metaphors for economic competition ("offensive," "invasion," "Pearl Harbor," "containment," "war"). Theodore White explicitly connected 1985 trade competition to 1945 surrender. A double standard was noted: the *Christian Science Monitor* (October 1987) observed that identical behavior by Norway (Kongsberg) generated almost no press anger, suggesting racial/historical factors amplified anti-Japanese language. The entire vocabulary became nearly extinct after Japan's bubble burst; by the mid-1990s, "Lost Decade" replaced the threat frame. An opinion poll after Reagan left office found Japan had replaced the Soviet Union as the greatest perceived threat to the U.S.

## DYAD 3: USA → CHINA (2000–2015)

### Frame 1 — Unfair competition

**"Currency manipulation" / "currency manipulator"** — First major press/legislative wave: 2003–2005 (Schumer-Graham bill introduced 2005 proposing 27.5% tariffs on Chinese goods). Continued through 2006 (Grassley-Baucus shifted language from "manipulation" to "misalignment"), 2010 (Currency Exchange Rate Oversight Reform Act), 2011 (passed Senate 63–35), 2012 (Romney vowed to name China a "currency manipulator" on his first day in office), and 2013 (re-introduced). Covered extensively in WSJ, NYT, Washington Post, Bloomberg.
Frequency: DOMINANT. One of the most heavily used press terms of the period.
Semantic peculiarities: The Treasury Department repeatedly declined to formally label China a "currency manipulator" under Bush and Obama, creating a recurring press narrative ("Treasury again refuses to label China a currency manipulator"). The formal designation only came in August 2019 under Trump (first since Clinton labeled China in 1994). The gap between political rhetoric and Treasury's refusal was itself a major recurring press story. Associated terms: "undervalued currency," "currency misalignment" (introduced as softer alternative), "unfair competitive advantage," "pegging."

**"The China Price"** — *BusinessWeek* (Bloomberg Businessweek), 6 December 2004, landmark cover story. Opening: "They are the three scariest words in U.S. industry." Meaning: "Cut your price at least 30% or lose your customers." The article covered the U.S.–China Economic and Security Review Commission hearings in Akron, Ohio (23 September 2004). Authors: Pete Engardio, Dexter Roberts, Brian Bremner. Later became title of Alexandra Harney's 2008 book *The China Price: The True Cost of Chinese Competitive Advantage*. *(Zitat, Datum, Akron-Hearing bestätigt via Bloomberg.)*
Frequency: COMMON. Peaked 2004–2006, then declined.
Semantic peculiarities: A genuinely novel press coinage — a three-word metonym for total competitive dislocation. Implied not just lower prices but an existential ultimatum to American manufacturers.

**"Unfair trade practices"** — Standard congressional and press formulation. Senator Stabenow (2013): "when countries like China cheat by manipulating currency, that's not competing — it's cheating." "Cheating" became an increasingly common informal equivalent, especially in populist discourse.
Frequency: DOMINANT throughout the period.

**"Dumping"** — Used primarily in trade-specific coverage, particularly anti-dumping duties in steel, solar panels, and paper industries.
Frequency: COMMON.

**"Trade deficit"** — Used in virtually every article on U.S.–China trade. $315 billion U.S. trade deficit with China in 2012 was frequently cited. "Record trade deficit" language recurred annually.
Frequency: DOMINANT.

**"Level playing field" / "playing by the rules"** — Ubiquitous congressional and press metaphor. Senator Graham (2013): "Manufacturing jobs in South Carolina and across the country are being destroyed because the Chinese and others continue to defy the rules of international trade."
Frequency: DOMINANT.

**"Race to the bottom"** — Used in labor/environmental context.
Frequency: COMMON.

**"Trade war"** — Used occasionally as a hypothetical or warning during this period. "Trade tensions" and "trade friction" were more common.
Frequency: MARGINAL in 2000–2015. Became dominant only 2017–2018.

### Frame 2 — National security threat

**"Strategic competitor"** — Condoleezza Rice, *Foreign Affairs* ("Campaign 2000: Promoting the National Interest," Jan/Feb 2000): "This means that China is not a 'status quo' power but one that would like to alter Asia's balance of power in its own favor. That alone makes it a strategic competitor, not the 'strategic partner' the Clinton administration once called it." The 2000 Republican platform identified China as a "strategic competitor." *(Verbatim-Zitat und Zuschreibung bestätigt via Foreign Affairs.)*
Frequency: COMMON. Peaked 2000–2001, then shelved after 9/11 as Bush moved toward engagement. Not revived until post-2015.
Semantic peculiarities: Deliberately chosen to contrast with Clinton's "strategic partner." The rapid abandonment of the term after 9/11 reveals how the War on Terror displaced China-focused security language.

**"China threat" / "China threat theory"** — Bill Gertz, *The China Threat: How the People's Republic Targets America* (Regnery, 2000). The Chinese counter-term 中国威胁论 (Zhongguo Weixielun) became increasingly used in English-language coverage.
Frequency: COMMON in policy press; DOMINANT in think-tank discourse.
Semantic peculiarities: In the early 2000s, "China threat" was primarily military; by the mid-2000s, increasingly economic; post-2008, mixed economic/ideological.

**"Pivot to Asia" / "rebalance to Asia"** — Hillary Clinton, "America's Pacific Century," *Foreign Policy*, October 2011 (used "pivot" three times). The administration later shifted to "rebalance" (seen as less provocative). Obama's November 2011 Australian Parliament speech signalled the U.S. would "play a larger and long-term role in shaping this region." *(Autorschaft, Datum, dreifache Nutzung von "pivot" bestätigt via Foreign Policy/FiveThirtyEight.)*
Frequency: DOMINANT (2011–2015). The single most-used foreign policy buzzword of Obama's second term.

**"String of pearls"** — Origin: Booz Allen Hamilton report, 2005, "Energy Futures in Asia." Described China's network of naval facilities and commercial ports across the Indian Ocean. Widely adopted by Washington Post, NYT, think-tank reports.
Frequency: COMMON in security/foreign policy press (2005–2015).
Semantic peculiarities: Vivid metaphor that entered press from a defense contractor report — unusual provenance for a press term.

**"Anti-access/area denial" (A2/AD)** — Pentagon terminology that entered press via defense reporters and think tanks. The 2012 Defense Strategic Guidance used "defeat antiaccess/area-denial (A2/AD) capabilities."
Frequency: MARGINAL in general press; COMMON in defense press.

**"Assertive China" / "China's assertiveness"** — Used extensively in NYT, Washington Post, *Foreign Affairs*, *Foreign Policy*. Emerged c. 2009–2010, accelerated after South China Sea incidents.
Frequency: COMMON (2009–2015).

**"Cyber warfare" / "cyber threat"** — Became dominant especially after Operation Aurora (2010) and the Mandiant APT1 report (2013). See Frame 5 for detailed attestation.
Frequency: DOMINANT (2010–2015).

### Frame 3 — Economic decline anxiety

**"Outsourcing"** — *BusinessWeek*, 3 February 2003, cover story "Is Your Job Next?" Became a campaign issue in the 2004 presidential race. Thomas Friedman, *The World Is Flat* (2005), massive bestseller that mainstreamed the concept. Used broadly but China was the primary referent.
Frequency: DOMINANT (peaked 2003–2008).

**"Offshoring"** — Used alongside "outsourcing" but distinguished as referring to relocating production rather than contracting.
Frequency: DOMINANT.

**"Hollowing out"** — *The Economist* (21 February 2004): "The Great Hollowing-out Myth" (skeptical take). Peter Navarro, *Death by China* (2011): China's rise is "systematically hollowing out the American industrial base."
Frequency: COMMON. Applied to manufacturing sectors, the middle class, and the industrial base.

**"China shock"** — The shorthand associated with the work of economists David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson. `[v1 — KORREKTUR: Die frühere Fassung schrieb, ihr Befund sei "roughly 21% of U.S. manufacturing employment decline (1990–2007), approximately 1.5 million jobs." **"Losing jobs to China" / "jobs shipped overseas"** — Ubiquitous campaign and editorial language. Frequency: DOMINANT.

**"Rust Belt"** — Not China-specific but frequently invoked in China trade coverage. The *BusinessWeek* "China Price" article was set in Akron, Ohio. Became politically salient in 2016.
Frequency: DOMINANT as geographic-economic shorthand.

**"Deindustrialization"** — Standard term in longform journalism and editorial pages. Often paired with Rust Belt imagery.
Frequency: COMMON.

**"Manufacturing decline"** — Straightforward descriptive term. Alliance for American Manufacturing (AAM) was a major institutional source for press quotes and data.
Frequency: DOMINANT.

### Frame 4 — Civilizational/system rivalry

**"State capitalism"** — Ian Bremmer, *The End of the Free Market: Who Wins the War Between States and Corporations?* (2010). Reviewed in *Washington Post* (28 May 2010). Bremmer defined state capitalism as driven by political motives (maximizing state power) not economic ones (maximizing growth).
Frequency: COMMON (peaked 2010–2012).
Semantic peculiarities: Served as a more analytical, less polemical alternative to "Communist China." Implied China was playing a fundamentally different game, not just cheating at the same one.

**"Authoritarian capitalism"** — *Foreign Affairs*: Azar Gat, "The Return of Authoritarian Great Powers" (2007). *Washington Post* review (2010) used "authoritarian capitalism" and "authoritarian model" interchangeably.
Frequency: COMMON (2008–2015).

**"Beijing Consensus" vs. "Washington Consensus"** — Joshua Cooper Ramo, "The Beijing Consensus" (2004), a Foreign Policy Centre paper that coined the term. Stefan Halper, *The Beijing Consensus: How China's Authoritarian Model Will Dominate the Twenty-First Century* (2010), further popularized the frame.
Frequency: COMMON in policy press; MARGINAL in daily newspapers.

**"China model"** — Used in press to describe China's development path as a potential alternative to Western liberal-democratic capitalism. Daniel Bell and Zhang Weiwei were prominent proponents discussed in press. Often framed as a question: "Is there a China model?"
Frequency: COMMON (2008–2015).

**"Responsible stakeholder"** — Deputy Secretary of State Robert Zoellick, September 2005 speech ("Whither China: From Membership to Responsibility?") to the National Committee on U.S.–China Relations. Called on China to become a "responsible stakeholder" in the international system. Press noted the phrase was seen as "paternalistic and insulting" in Beijing.
Frequency: COMMON (2005–2012).
Semantic peculiarities: A term of conditional inclusion — implying China was not yet responsible, not yet a full stakeholder. Defined the engagement-era frame.

**"Communist China" / "Red China"** — By 2000–2015, "Communist China" appeared occasionally in editorial pages and conservative media (National Review, Washington Times) but was largely archaic in mainstream press. "Red China" was even more marginal.
Frequency: MARGINAL. Residual, confined to conservative commentary.

### Frame 5 — Imitation/technology theft

**"Intellectual property theft" / "IP theft"** — Used across all major publications. Navarro's *Death by China* (2011) listed it as one of the core Chinese "cheating" practices. Grew steadily from 2005.
Frequency: DOMINANT (especially post-2013).

**"Cyber espionage"** — Operation Aurora (January 2010): Google disclosed hacking originating from China targeting 20+ companies. Extensive coverage in NYT, WSJ, Washington Post. Mandiant APT1 Report (18 February 2013): linked PLA Unit 61398 to cyber espionage against 141+ organizations. NYT front-page headline: "Chinese Army Unit Is Seen as Tied to Hacking Against U.S." (Sanger, Barboza, Perlroth, 18 February 2013). The report documented theft of "technology blueprints, manufacturing processes, clinical trial results, pricing documents, negotiation strategies." Obama made cyber espionage a primary focus of the June 2013 U.S.–China presidential summit. *(Report, Datum, Unit 61398, "141+ organizations" und NYT-Schlagzeile bestätigt via NYT/Mandiant/CFR.)*
Frequency: DOMINANT (2010–2015).
Semantic peculiarities: The term "advanced persistent threat" (APT) entered press vocabulary from cybersecurity professionals during this period. Mandiant's report was a watershed that gave the press a concrete, nameable entity (Unit 61398) and a specific Shanghai location to pin the threat on.

**"Forced technology transfer"** — Used throughout the period in trade policy coverage. Described the practice of requiring foreign companies to share technology with Chinese partners as a condition of market access.
Frequency: COMMON (accelerated post-2010).

**"Industrial espionage"** — Umbrella term covering both cyber and traditional espionage methods. May 2014: U.S. federal grand jury indicted five Chinese military officers for "cyber espionage and stealing trade secrets" — first-ever such indictment of state actors. Covered by all major outlets.
Frequency: COMMON.

**"Trade secrets theft"** — Legal term that entered press through DOJ indictments. Attested in coverage of GE Aviation, Boeing (Dongfan "Greg" Chung case, 2008), SolarWorld hack (2014).
Frequency: COMMON.

**"Counterfeit goods" / "knockoffs" / "piracy"** — Used extensively in 2000s, particularly in entertainment industry coverage. "Piracy" applied to software, film, and music counterfeiting. China placed on USTR "Priority Watch List" annually.
Frequency: COMMON (especially 2000–2010).

**"Shanzhai" (山寨)** — Used in *Wired*, tech blogs, and occasional NYT/WSJ features to describe China's culture of copying/remixing products.
Frequency: MARGINAL. Mostly confined to tech press and cultural features rather than threat-framing.
Semantic peculiarities: More of a cultural curiosity term than a threat term in American press usage. In Chinese discourse, "shanzhai" carried connotations of grassroots innovation and subversion, nuances largely lost in American press adoption.

**Cross-cutting note for Dyad 3:** Press language evolved through distinct phases: optimistic engagement with "level playing field" caution (2000–2003); industrial anxiety centered on "China Price" and "currency manipulation" (2003–2006); civilizational/system framing as the 2008 crisis undermined Washington Consensus credibility (2007–2010); cyber espionage and technology theft as dominant frame after Operation Aurora and Mandiant (2010–2015). Peter Navarro's *Death by China* (2011) consolidated the populist-protectionist lexicon, combining all five frames into a single polemical bundle that prefigured Trump-era discourse. "Trade war" was marginal in 2000–2015 but became dominant only after 2017.

## Comparative observations across all three dyads

The five frames recur across all three dyads, but their relative prominence shifts. Unfair competition is always present but operates through different focal terms: "Made in Germany" (labeling fraud), "Japan Inc." (coordinated state-corporate entity), "currency manipulation" (macroeconomic distortion). National security threat language escalates in each case through military metaphors applied to commerce — "commercial invasion," "economic Pearl Harbor," "cyber warfare" — though the specific military reference shifts from naval supremacy to WWII to cyber conflict. Economic decline anxiety is expressed through the "national efficiency" movement, "competitiveness crisis," and "outsourcing" / "hollowing out" respectively, each reflecting the incumbent's specific vulnerabilities of the moment. Civilizational framing operates through antinomies: liberalism vs. Prussianism, Anglo-American capitalism vs. the developmental state, market democracy vs. state capitalism. Technology theft language evolves from design "piracy" and "fraudulent marking," through "reverse engineering" and "copycat," to "cyber espionage" and "forced technology transfer" — tracking the increasing formalization and securitization of IP concerns.

A consistent pattern across all three dyads is semantic inversion of origin-marking phrases: "Made in Germany" transformed from stigma to badge of quality; "Made in Japan" underwent the same reversal; "Made in China" was beginning a similar trajectory by 2015, though the process was less complete. In each case, press language devised to stigmatize the challenger's products eventually became a mark of competitive achievement — a recurring irony that the press itself rarely acknowledged in real time.
